Dutch firm Intelic's AI-based Nexus C2 software links multi-vendor sensors and interceptor drones, now being rolled out with Ukrainian brigades

2026-10-08

Defense News, carrying a Reuters report, describes Amsterdam-based software company Intelic and its Nexus command-and-control system, which links radars, sensors, cameras and interceptor drones from different vendors. Intelic has a €30 million (about $33.5 million) contract with the Dutch Defence Ministry to upgrade air defences. In a test in late September at a disused Dutch airbase, Nexus used radar and AI to detect and verify a mock target, a reconnaissance drone confirmed it with image recognition, and an interceptor drone was launched. A human made the final launch decision, although the process could run fully autonomously. Intelic has an office in Kyiv and says Nexus is being rolled out on the Ukrainian battlefield, supplementing Ukraine's own battlefield management system Delta. According to the company, Ukrainian brigades often field drones from different vendors and Nexus lets them connect these in one system; an Intelic engineer called it the "connecting tissue" between drone systems. The report puts this in the context of Russian air attacks: it says Russia uses high-altitude, jet-propelled attack drones and, citing Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergii Koretskyi, that in the first three weeks of September Russia launched 2,100 such drones and 136 missiles at Ukraine, killing 288 people. It also cites Kiel Institute figures of about €1.4 billion in Dutch funding for Ukraine's drone programme since 2022, total Dutch military aid above €12.5 billion including nearly 1 million drones, and European drone aid rising from €400 million (2022) to €1.2 billion (2025) and €1.6 billion in the first four months of 2026.

He linked this to recent air raids on Odesa that came in waves with very short gaps between them. According to him, such long, wave-by-wave strikes would be extremely difficult to plan and coordinate without AI. He said: "Russian forces use AI to lay out attack routes and to recognize targets." Storms and fog complicate operations, and the open sea gives Russia more freedom in choosing drone routes. Defense Express concludes the missile is still in development (although the Alazan-6 itself has been mass-produced since the 2000s) but could become a serious problem for Ukrainian drones, including jet-powered UAVs. Until then, Bars was known as a strike weapon used against Moscow, with production reportedly running at about 2,000 units a month. The outlet stresses several times that its analysis is only preliminary assumptions, to be added to when more information becomes available. Defense Express notes that three warhead options have previously been declared for Bars: 22 kg, 60 kg and 105 kg. A smaller warhead gives more range and better manoeuvrability, while a larger one gives more destructive power at a shorter range. For use against jet Shaheds, the outlet expects the 22 kg or 60 kg variant to be more practical. ПЗРК «Ива». Новый комплекс российской ПВО и иностранный след

2026-10-01SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar author Kirill Ryabov analyses the 'Iva' man-portable air defence system (MANPADS) that appeared in a 27 September report by Rossiya 1's 'Vesti Nedeli' on Rostov Oblast. Soldiers of a mobile fire group (MOG) tasked with regional air defence, call signs 'Leshiy' and 'Modny', showed machine-gun mounts and a new MANPADS: a transport case holding two missile tubes (transport-launch containers), a detachable reusable gripstock and four small canisters of undisclosed purpose, plus the weapon in ready-to-fire state. A soldier stated a launch range of up to 4 km; Ryabov stresses this is the only stated figure, no live firings were shown, and the seeker type (presumably infrared), its bands and jamming resistance are unknown. He notes the name fits the Russian 'plant' naming tradition (Strela, Igla, Verba), which weakly suggests a Russian design but proves nothing, and that one TV report cannot show whether Iva is a serial product, prototype, upgrade or closed-programme system. The article addresses claims that circulated after the broadcast that Iva is practically identical to Chinese QianWei-family MANPADS such as the QW-18 or QW-19, or to Iran's Misagh-2/Misagh-3, which are themselves derived from the Chinese designs - the same identification debate as in the earlier tracker entry on the unmarked shoulder-fired SAM (possibly QW-18A/QW-19) seen in this Rossiya 1 footage. Ryabov warns that external similarity does not prove origin and lists possible explanations: an independent Russian design, use of foreign technical solutions, a localised or upgraded foreign product, direct import of ready-made systems, or similarity limited to the case and packing layout. Direct Chinese delivery and Chinese design roots should not be conflated, he argues: a Chinese-lineage design could have reached Russia through a third country. In an editorial assessment, Topwar calls a single design lineage going back to the Chinese QianWei family the most economical explanation, making the 'Chinese' and 'Iranian' versions the same thing at different distance from the original. It judges an Iranian channel most likely, because open data show Iran delivering ready-made weapons to Russia while there are no direct reports of Chinese arms deliveries, with the matching packing layout as only a weak supporting argument; it explicitly does not present this as fact. Possible motives for an import - replenishing stocks amid heavy expenditure against drones, production rates, component availability - are mentioned, but the author says Iva's appearance alone does not show that Russian industry cannot meet demand. Conclusion: one Russian unit has the Iva with a claimed 4 km range; everything else is hypothesis. Caveat: this is single-source reporting from Topwar (Russian pro-government military-analysis site); the claims and assessments have not been independently verified.
