Ukrainians get near-real-time maps of Russian drones: volunteer 'monitor' map and Defence Ministry's Air Alert app now show approximate positions and headings

2026-10-20

Two new tools let Ukrainians see roughly where Russian attack drones are flying, Euromaidan Press reports. A Ukrainian air-threat project called monitor announced a free, ad-free beta map on X on 13 October. It does not use radar: it aggregates posts from regional Telegram channels that report drones and missiles over their oblasts, cross-checks them algorithmically to estimate each target's type, approximate position and heading, and overlays official air-raid alerts from alerts.in.ua (areas shown red or yellow). A target log lists each drone's district and course, the map refreshes about every 20 seconds while targets are airborne, and it works in a browser or on Telegram on a weak connection. The beta focuses on drones, though cruise and ballistic missiles are already listed as target types. The developers warn that the dots are estimates that appear late, that some targets are never tracked and that "an empty map does not mean you are safe". On 17 October Ukraine's Defence Ministry said its Air Alert app now shows the approximate position and heading of some targets, namely cruise missiles, Banderol drone-launched missiles and Shahed-type drones, using data from the Air Force over secure channels. For security reasons it shows no exact coordinates and no true real-time tracking and may lag; ballistic missiles and anything faster than 2,000 km/h are not shown. Both the ministry and the monitor developers tell users to go to shelter first and check the map from there. The tools respond to the changing Russian strike campaign: nightly raids of over a hundred drones, jet-powered Shaheds that can cut their engines and glide in, and the fact that since September drone-only (yellow) alerts in some oblasts no longer shut everything down, so many people stay at work during drone alerts. Publishing approximate tracks is a trade-off between public safety and giving Russia feedback on its routes, which explains the deliberate delays and coarseness in the official version.

Cities are expanding underground and metro schools (10 underground schools in Zaporizhzhia, metro schools in Kharkiv), mobile shelters and protective netting, gabions and anti-drone cages on minibuses and petrol stations. At the same time, residents increasingly judge risk themselves: many ignore drone alerts, follow the 'two-wall rule' and only take shelter for ballistic missile threats, and shelter capacity remains uneven, notably in Dnipro.
