Ukrainian F-16 pilot downs nine Russian strike drones in one sortie using only the M61A1 Vulcan cannon

2026-11-06

A Ukrainian F-16 pilot shot down nine Russian strike drones in a single sortie using only the aircraft's 20mm M61A1 Vulcan cannon, according to the Ukrainian Air Force's 107th Separate Aviation Wing, which relayed its commander's remarks at the International Fighter Conference in Munich. Days earlier the same pilot had downed eight drones in one flight, firing 400 rounds and landing with 100 left; on the record flight he returned with 80 rounds still in the belt. "The tactic is simple: just get into range and fire the cannon," the wing commander said. These are claims by the Ukrainian side and have not been independently verified. According to the figures cited by Euromaidan Press, Ukraine's F-16s have downed about 4,700 Russian strike drones over the course of the war, roughly 1,100 of them in October alone, most of them with the cannon. The gun is partly a workaround for scarcity: Ukraine's F-16s are so short of air-to-air missiles that pilots increasingly engage drones with the cannon and save missiles for harder targets. The method has drawbacks: falling 20mm rounds behave like grenades on impact and can endanger civilians over a wide area. The approach depends on foreign ammunition supplies. Denmark has sent more than 50,000 cannon rounds, and Spain, Belgium and Poland have also supplied 20mm rounds, with a larger package expected. The 107th is the first of the NATO-style "wings" Ukraine is forming as it restructures its air force on the alliance model by 2035.

2026-11-06

Rostec, Russia's state arms conglomerate, said on Telegram that the Kalitka-KA automated anti-drone turret, made by Kalashnikov, can receive target data from external radars and scouts so it can spot approaching drones earlier. Euromaidan Press reports that the turret carries a 7.62mm PKT machine gun, two daytime cameras, a rangefinder and an optional thermal imager, and comes in stationary and pickup-mounted versions. Rostec claims Russian troops rate its effectiveness at 70–100% depending on drone type. That is a manufacturer claim, not independent data. Kalashnikov told the Russian state news agency TASS that the system already protects Russian artillery positions, command posts, and assault and supply routes. The capability is not new. During the Center-2026 strategic exercises, the Kalitka-KA took target designation from radar stations and the Uzor-K software suite, according to Rossiyskaya Gazeta. Kalashnikov representatives said Uzor-K uses artificial intelligence to merge radar and other sensor data. At a demonstration at Patriot Park near Moscow on 2 November, the turret downed an FPV drone and a fixed-wing drone at under 150 metres, according to TASS. Kommersant reported that thick fog caused the rest of that demonstration to miss. A hit by the Kraken interceptor drone could not be confirmed, and riflemen and machine gunners hit nothing. According to Kommersant, the turret is meant to be the inner layer of a layered site defence. The stationary Kalitka-KA would cover 100 metres to 1 km, the Krona missile system (still in experimental trials) 1 to 5 km, and interceptor drones and mobile Kalitkas out to 11 km. Euromaidan Press notes that the fixed turret is a last line around a site, not a substitute for longer-range air defence. It links this need for deep point defence to Ukrainian long-range strikes such as the July hit on the Omsk refinery, about 2,430 km from the front.

Ukraine lets private air defence groups protect other companies' sites and share systems

2026-11-05

The Ukrainian government has expanded its experimental private air defence project: companies that run their own air defence groups may now protect the facilities of other companies under contract, not only their own sites. Ukrainska Pravda reports this citing the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence. The amended rules also allow air defence systems to be transferred for use between authorised companies, on the basis of a joint decision with Air Force Command. The entry criteria now include status as a resident of Defence City (Ukraine's special regime for defence firms) and/or designation by the Ministry of Defence as a critically important business. Private groups are now allowed to use manned aircraft, the government has regulated companies' use of warheads and electric detonators for interceptor drones, and the minimum age for group members has been lowered from 21 to 18. The private groups remain under the control of the military command and operate within the unified air defence system of the Armed Forces of Ukraine. According to the same report, by 14 October almost 100 companies were taking part in the project and about 50 air defence groups had been formed; groups from six companies had flown combat missions and destroyed more than 60 aerial targets. The step follows an earlier simplification, at the end of October, of the General Staff's rules for radars and EW used by private air defence, and comes as firms such as Nova Post already field their own private air defence unit.

«Why Did russia Use Mostly Conventional Shahed Drones in Its Latest Attack on Ukraine?» / «Експерт пояснив, чому росіяни запускали більше бензинових «Шахедів», ніж реактивних» (Defense Express / ArmyInform: 'Expert explains why the Russians launched more petrol Shaheds than jet ones') — cloud cover as a counter to Ukrainian interceptor drones

2026-11-01SINGLE SOURCE

ArmyInform and Defense Express both point out, citing Ukraine's Air Force, that Russia's overnight attack of 31 October-1 November relied mainly on conventional petrol-engined Shaheds rather than jet-powered versions: only 64 of the 433 strike drones launched from the evening of 31 October were jet-powered. Air defence destroyed or suppressed 371 drones, 47 of them jet-powered. A day earlier jet-powered Shaheds had made up more than half of the night wave. Both outlets attribute the shift to the weather, quoting Serhii Beskrestnov (callsign 'Flash'), adviser to the President of Ukraine on defence technology. He wrote that there was solid cloud cover over Ukraine, so the enemy was launching dozens of ordinary petrol Shaheds in the expectation that Ukrainian interceptor crews would find them hard to detect. Defense Express adds that Russia was apparently trying to exploit these conditions to reduce the effectiveness of Ukraine's interceptor drones, which are increasingly used against Russian long-range UAV attacks. Jet-powered Shaheds fly faster and are generally harder to intercept, it notes, but propeller-driven drones also pose a challenge when weather limits visual detection. This is an explanation from a Ukrainian official, not a confirmed Russian rationale; neither outlet offers other evidence for the link with cloud cover.
