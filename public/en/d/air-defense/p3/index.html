Russia has built at least 33 air-defence positions around the Alabuga Shahed/Geran drone production zone in Tatarstan since spring 2026, satellite imagery shows

2026-10-21

At least 33 air-defence positions have been built around the Alabuga Special Economic Zone in Tatarstan since spring 2026. Alabuga is Russia's main production site for Geran (Shahed-type) attack drones. The count comes from Radio Liberty's Russian service, which analysed satellite imagery taken on 21 October 2026; Militarnyi and Ukrainska Pravda reported it. The new structures include concrete towers and platforms for Pantsir, Tor and other air-defence systems around the main production site, plus new surface-to-air missile sites. There are also towers for heavy anti-aircraft machine guns and frames for stretching anti-drone nets over the workshops. Radio Liberty published an interactive map of the positions. Radio Liberty calls Alabuga one of the best-protected sites in Russia against drone strikes. It compares it with Vladimir Putin's residence at Valdai, which has 31 towers and two S-300/400 positions. It also counts more than 100 new air-defence positions built in Moscow and Moscow Oblast since the full-scale invasion. Earlier Militarnyi reporting, citing the Institute for Science and International Security, described two rings of towers at Alabuga: shorter towers around the key production buildings and taller ones on an outer perimeter. Production keeps growing while the defences expand. Dnipro Osint estimates from satellite imagery that the underground facilities under construction cover 42,000 sq m. Images from late August showed at least 10 new workshops and dozens of warehouses. Militarnyi reports that Russia has raised output of the jet-powered Geran-4/5 to 4,000 a month. Ukraine first struck Alabuga in April 2024; the latest attack with documented damage was in June 2025.

2026-10-21

The Ukrainian Navy has published footage of a Sargan 3000 uncrewed surface vessel (USV) destroying a Russian Geran (Shahed-type) attack drone with an interceptor drone launched from the boat. According to Militarnyi, the interceptor was a Sting, made by the Ukrainian company Wild Hornets, and it was launched from a container-based platform on the USV. Euromaidan Press summed up the engagement as a drone boat launching a drone that shot down a third drone, all of them uncrewed and all at sea. The Navy announced the kill in October 2026 on Facebook and Telegram. Neither outlet gives an exact location, date or operating unit; Militarnyi files the story under the Black Sea. This is the Sargan 3000's third reported kill against Russian unmanned systems in a few weeks. In September it destroyed a Russian USV with machine-gun fire, in what was described as the first engagement between uncrewed surface platforms. In early October it shot down a Shahed with its Protector 12.7 mm remote weapon station (e-20261008-11). This time it used a launched interceptor drone instead of its gun. The Navy took the Sargan 3000 into service in April 2026. It has an operational radius of up to 1,600 km, a speed of 40–55 knots and a payload of up to 450 kg, and in July it was used to sink a Russian second-rank border patrol ship. According to Wild Hornets, the Sting is an FPV interceptor for Shaheds/Gerans, Lancets and reconnaissance drones, with a top speed of 280 km/h. The company claims a typical hit rate of 80–90% and more than 3,000 drones destroyed in its first seven months of use. Militarnyi adds that Ukraine's Defence Ministry has authorised Wild Hornets to form its own air-defence groups for its Night Hornets counter-drone unit, supported by remote-control centres and the remote launch system Hornet Vision Ctrl. The engagement points to an air-defence layer pushed out to sea, which could intercept Shaheds before they reach Odesa's ports, the coast and shipping. The kill claim comes from the Ukrainian Navy, and the Sting performance figures come from its manufacturer; neither has been independently verified. Ukraine still lacks a scalable answer to jet-powered Gerans: Brave1 says seven fast interceptor types have scored kills and over 1,000 are on order, while frontline crews and Sternenko's foundation point to speed and cost gaps

2026-10-21

Ukrainian units on the front line describe jet-powered Gerans as largely beyond the reach of their current interceptor drones. At the command post of the 'Posipaky' battalion of the 39th Anti-Aircraft Missile Regiment on the Zaporizhzhia front, commander 'Ramses' told Ukrainska Pravda that jet drones flying at around 500 km/h are simply left alone, because conventional interceptor drones cannot catch them. Russia now sends them one by one at 20–30 minute intervals rather than in large groups, and most cross the front line; they climb and accelerate over occupied territory and appear on screens only at the last moment at 5,000–8,000 m, above MANPADS reach. Most are Geran-4s, with the cruise-missile-like Geran-5 also in use, and according to Ramses they are operator-controlled via mesh networks in which drones relay the radio signal to each other. He said Russia used about 2,400 jet-powered and 2,400 conventional Gerans in August; the Air Force reports downing only 1,460 jet drones that month. 'Betsyk', commander of an interceptor battalion of the 1030th Anti-Aircraft Missile-Artillery Regiment 'Aquila' (3rd Army Corps), told ArmyInform (via Euromaidan Press) that his crews destroyed several thousand Russian aerial targets during Operation Vivaldi north of Lyman, but only small numbers of jet drones: interceptors need 500–600 km/h, so the regiment relies mainly on self-propelled anti-aircraft guns and SAM/MANPADS systems against them. He expects Russia to adapt once effective interceptors appear, but not to double drone speed. On the countermeasures side, Brave1 head Andrii Hrytseniuk told Ukrainska Pravda that funding for anti-jet-drone solutions began in summer 2025: 22 teams are working on high-speed interceptors (12 with Brave1 funding), with miniature turbojet, propeller-electric and electric ducted-fan designs. Seven interceptor types have been used in combat and scored kills, including an unidentified turbojet interceptor used by the SBU against a Geran-5, and the government has ordered 'well over 1,000', though production is still ramping up; scaling took six months for propeller-Shahed interceptors and 'won't happen overnight' this time. Eleven Ukrainian firms now make small turbojet engines, six in mass production. Brave1 also has ten teams developing cheap anti-aircraft missiles (target price at most $20,000 each), two of which are preparing for operational use; Luch's RK-2PZ, the Shershen system and the Nika missile are among other projects. According to Zelensky, as of early October the Air Force still accounted for half of all jet-drone kills, often with cannon fire at close range because of missile shortages, and the Bars drone for about a third. Euromaidan Press reports on footage released around 17 October by the 1021st Anti-Aircraft Missile-Artillery Brigade showing a ramp-launched, rocket-boosted Bars steered by infrared camera towards a Geran-5 (see e-20261019-07); Zelensky claimed on 4 October a hit rate of about 30% for Bars, the highest among Ukrainian interceptors. Its manufacturer says it builds over 2,000 Bars a month, mostly strike versions, and a Ukrainian-made Bars engine passed final tests in September. Defence Minister Yevhen Khmara says hundreds of domestically made automated machine-gun turrets are in service. Cost is the other constraint. A foundation led by volunteer Serhii Sternenko (which says it has bought 291,502 FPV drones) estimates that a jet-Geran kill costs $25,000–1.2 million with fighter aircraft, about $30,000–4 million with ground-based systems such as IRIS-T, NASAMS, Vampire or Avenger, and from $2,000 with interceptor drones, which are not yet scalable; it claims to have found a cheaper counter-method with Ukrainian manufacturers but gives no details. Ukrainska Pravda puts a conventional Geran at up to $70,000, with the jet version tens of thousands more, against hundreds of thousands for a Stinger and about $500,000 for an AIM-9X. Interviewees stress that no single system suffices and that only a mix of interceptor drones, turrets, guns, aircraft and missiles can work. The kill counts, hit rates and order figures are Ukrainian claims and have not been independently verified. Europe weighs letting utilities and infrastructure operators detect, jam and shoot down drones; France has already legislated, Austria and Poland follow

2026-10-21

European governments are considering giving operators of critical infrastructure, including energy companies, powers to counter drones themselves, Reuters reports via Defense News. Some utilities have asked for permission to shoot drones down. There is no single EU proposal: countries are moving separately, and the article says the technology is maturing faster than the rules for using it. France has gone furthest legally. In August its parliament passed a law allowing approved critical-infrastructure operators and their contractors to detect, jam and shoot down drones near designated sites, after drone flights over the Île Longue nuclear submarine base near Brest in December 2025. A senior official said the government wants an expanded list of sensitive sites equipped by 2027: "We were defenseless in the face of the (drone) threat." Operators such as EDF and SNCF would get a catalogue of approved systems ranging from jammers to interceptor drones. The Systematic Paris-Region test programme at Brétigny-sur-Orge has catalogued three integrators and 17 counter-drone systems. The defence ministry says the law covers only infrastructure under its authority. In Germany, where a drone carrying explosives was found in August near a Ukrainian cargo aircraft at Leipzig/Halle airport (Berlin blamed Russia, which denied it), counter-drone action remains a police task; the interior ministry is examining how operators could legally take part. Austria announced in September plans to give critical-infrastructure operators, including large energy companies, similar powers; Defence Minister Klaudia Tanner said the state cannot protect every site alone. Poland is furthest along in practice: operators have deployed counter-drone systems and trained with military and police units, and drone-restricted zones around critical energy sites let authorised security units destroy, disable or take control of drones. Sources at Polish transmission companies say clearer guarantees on liability for collateral damage are still needed, possibly a court precedent. Industry views differ. Eurelectric secretary general Kristian Ruby warned of possible "heating outages this winter due to hybrid attacks", and E.ON CEO Leonhard Birnbaum said operators of about 2,720 German substations must assume some attacks will succeed. Poland's PSE patrols some 16,500 km of high-voltage lines by helicopter, and the Baltic states are building a fortified substation near the Russian border, along with anti-drone nets and stocks of spare parts. Finland's Fingrid and Fortum, by contrast, want responsibility to remain with the authorities and the armed forces; Fingrid's CEO called expanded powers "a very, very big change". Open questions are who may order an engagement and who is liable if a downed drone causes damage. Rheinmetall says the main obstacle is the delay between detection and the decision to act, and a French official expects operators eventually to respond "at the press of a button".

Ukrainians get near-real-time maps of Russian drones: volunteer 'monitor' map and Defence Ministry's Air Alert app now show approximate positions and headings

2026-10-20

Two new tools let Ukrainians see roughly where Russian attack drones are flying, Euromaidan Press reports. A Ukrainian air-threat project called monitor announced a free, ad-free beta map on X on 13 October. It does not use radar: it aggregates posts from regional Telegram channels that report drones and missiles over their oblasts, cross-checks them algorithmically to estimate each target's type, approximate position and heading, and overlays official air-raid alerts from alerts.in.ua (areas shown red or yellow). A target log lists each drone's district and course, the map refreshes about every 20 seconds while targets are airborne, and it works in a browser or on Telegram on a weak connection. The beta focuses on drones, though cruise and ballistic missiles are already listed as target types. The developers warn that the dots are estimates that appear late, that some targets are never tracked and that "an empty map does not mean you are safe". On 17 October Ukraine's Defence Ministry said its Air Alert app now shows the approximate position and heading of some targets, namely cruise missiles, Banderol drone-launched missiles and Shahed-type drones, using data from the Air Force over secure channels. For security reasons it shows no exact coordinates and no true real-time tracking and may lag; ballistic missiles and anything faster than 2,000 km/h are not shown. Both the ministry and the monitor developers tell users to go to shelter first and check the map from there. The tools respond to the changing Russian strike campaign: nightly raids of over a hundred drones, jet-powered Shaheds that can cut their engines and glide in, and the fact that since September drone-only (yellow) alerts in some oblasts no longer shut everything down, so many people stay at work during drone alerts. Publishing approximate tracks is a trade-off between public safety and giving Russia feedback on its routes, which explains the deliberate delays and coarseness in the official version.
