Order to cap Starshield speeds for Ukraine came from inside the White House, officials say, as SpaceX's limit cripples Ukrainian drone and interceptor operations against Russian forces; The Telegraph reports Musk himself pushed Trump for it

2026-10-14

Since the night of 13–14 October, the military "Starshield" service that SpaceX provides to Ukrainian forces has stopped working on fast-moving objects without prior warning, according to Ukrainian presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov. Starshield is SpaceX's military business unit, with enhanced encryption and dedicated terminals running over the Starlink constellation. A source in Ukraine's Defence Ministry told Reuters that receivers now work in motion again, but only up to 100 mph (160 km/h). Ukraine's 7th Rapid Reaction (Air Assault) Corps reported link losses of 10–20 seconds at 110–120 km/h on the Huliaipole front, cancelled missions, and fast mid-range strike drones that can no longer be used as intended; activist Serhii Sternenko, citing military sources, reported drops at 60–70 km/h. The cap also hits Starlink-guided interceptor drones, which Beskrestnov called Ukraine's most effective defence against Russia's jet-powered Shaheds (Geran). Russian jet drones and missiles fly at roughly twice the cap speed, according to The Telegraph (via Euromaidan Press). On 16 October President Zelensky said the restrictions had disrupted several operations, including interceptions of jet Gerans. He added that Ukraine does not use Starlink for strikes inside Russia, and that the Defence Ministry had received signals from the White House that capability would be restored, although speeds had not yet returned to previous levels. Who ordered the cap is disputed. Reuters (published by Defense News) reports, citing two US officials and a Western official, that the order came from inside the White House; Pentagon leaders received a message that Starshield service for Ukraine had to be slowed, but the officials did not say who made the decision. A senior Ukrainian official said Kyiv was told the same after calls to the White House, State Department and Pentagon. Musk wrote on X that Starshield is "owned and controlled by the US government" and that the military or the president decides any speed or usage limits. The Telegraph, citing four anonymous sources and as reported by Euromaidan Press, gives a contradictory account: Musk himself proposed slowing the service to Trump, Trump agreed, and the Pentagon then restricted Starshield, while a senior source says Musk later grew worried about a backlash from European customers. The denials are just as divided. A senior US official told Reuters that "no such request was ever made by the president", an anonymous US official said much the same to AFP, and Trump said he did not believe he or Musk was responsible. The White House, Pentagon and SpaceX did not respond to Reuters. According to Reuters, the cap follows US pressure on Kyiv to stop strikes on Russian refineries, which Trump blamed for high energy prices ahead of the midterms. That pressure included a tough meeting with Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff in Miami on 9 October and a US diesel deal with Russia. For Russian forces, the cap temporarily eases pressure on front-line positions and rear logistics hit by Starlink-guided strike drones, and it weakens Ukraine's cheapest counter to jet Gerans. US Senator Richard Blumenthal warned it could cripple Ukrainian air defence. Poland, which supplied about 29,500 terminals and pays some $50 million a year, warned through Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski: "As your European customers, we are watching you." All accounts of the order's origin rest on anonymous sources, and the Telegraph version was seen only through Euromaidan Press's summary; neither version has been independently confirmed. Militarnyi (17 October) adds detail from an audio message Zelensky sent to journalists. Above 161 km/h, he said, the Starlink link on jet-powered interceptor drones is completely lost after about two minutes and the drone is lost with it. Zelensky said Ukraine had driven Starlink-equipped vehicles in EU countries at 180 km/h or more without the link dropping, and has records of this, so the cut-off applies inside Ukraine. He put the share of jet Shaheds downed by Ukrainian aviation at about 55-60%. Militarnyi notes this puts a strain on a small pool of pilots and on the F-16 fleet, roughly half of which is in Europe for long-term repair and maintenance. Interceptors used against jet Shaheds include the P1-SUN JetKiller, SPYS and Sting S, which derive from electric quadcopters, plus a likely jet-powered interceptor based on the Bars missile drone; according to the minister Militarnyi cites, Yevhen Khmara, one unit destroyed five targets with five Bars, and the approach is being scaled up. Militarnyi reports that SpaceX has not responded to the Ukrainian military or the Defence Ministry, that US officials blame "technical difficulties", and that the restrictions were still in place as of 17 October. These are Ukrainian statements and have not been independently verified.

Rostec claims its 30 mm ZAK-30 Tsitadel anti-drone gun with programmable shrapnel rounds outperforms Western rivals such as Skynex and Phalanx.

2026-10-14

Russian state defence conglomerate Rostec is marketing its new 30 mm ZAK-30 "Tsitadel" (Citadel) anti-drone gun as a direct competitor to Western systems such as Germany's Skynex and the US Phalanx and its land-based C-RAM variant, Defence Blog reports. In a statement this week Rostec claimed that combat use has shown advantages in automation, cost per shot and effectiveness. According to Rostec, Skynex's 35 mm AHEAD rounds produce a powerful fragment cloud but make the system heavier and more expensive, while Phalanx/C-RAM fire 20 mm Vulcan Gatling guns at very high rates without programmable rounds and therefore use more ammunition per target. These are manufacturer claims and have not been independently verified. Tsitadel fires 30 mm shrapnel shells with a remotely set fuze; the fire-control computer calculates the optimal burst point on the drone's flight path so the shell detonates just ahead of the target, which Rostec says reduces the number of rounds per kill. Targets are detected and tracked by radar and an electro-optical sensor working in visible light and infrared, with a highly automated detect-to-engage sequence. Russian media report that the gun module appears to be based on the BM-30-D Spitsa weapon station with the 2A42 cannon used on Russian infantry fighting vehicles, with a range of roughly 1 km against fixed-wing drones and about 800 m against small multicopters; Russian industry sources say a truck-mounted version on Ural chassis is planned. Russian military commentator Dmitry Kornev told Izvestia the system is far ahead of older guns such as Germany's Gepard, which Ukraine uses against Shahed-type drones. Rostec first unveiled Tsitadel in May at a security forum near Moscow as a system to protect fixed sites such as plants, depots and energy facilities against multicopter and fixed-wing drones around the clock, and said at the time it had already been tested in combat in Ukraine. Defence Blog notes that the Western systems have their own combat records: Skynex protects key sites in Ukraine against Russian drones and cruise missiles, Phalanx serves on US Navy ships, and C-RAM has defended US bases in Iraq and Afghanistan.

«Мобильные огневые группы ПВО: угрозы, концепция и решения» (Topwar: Mobile air-defence fire groups — threats, concept and solutions)

2026-10-14SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar author Kirill Ryabov describes Russia's mobile air-defence fire groups (MOG PVO) as its main answer to small drones and to mass long-range Ukrainian drone raids. Their job is to fight light, numerous UAVs; larger targets are a secondary task. According to the article, the first Russian groups appeared in 2023. Some were formed as regional and volunteer units under the BARS reserve project. They now serve in the ground forces, other arms and the Rosgvardia, both in the war zone and in rear areas covering cities, military and industrial sites. A MOG is a small team with its own vehicle, communications, sensors and weapons. It stands duty at a set position or redeploys on headquarters orders to close gaps in other systems' coverage and to block newly spotted drone routes. The author says MOGs work best when tied into the overall air-defence command loop, so targets can be shared with conventional SAM units. Equipment is not standardised, because the groups were set up in haste. Most use pickups or buggies, often Russian-made, or converted UAZ-452 'Bukhanka' vans. Some groups have compact radars or emitter detectors. The main weapons are PKM/PKT, DShK, NSV and Kord machine guns, sometimes the GShG-7.62 aircraft machine gun, often on multi-gun mounts. The article gives an opening-fire range of about 1 km for rifle-calibre and up to 1.5 km for heavy machine guns, and notes the chance of a kill at those distances is much lower. Shotguns with special buckshot work only out to a few dozen metres, 100 m at most. The groups also use interceptor drones such as the Yolka: 1.3 kg according to the developer, up to 2 kg according to an Arkhipelag-2025 data sheet, with up to 3 km engagement range and operator or automatic electro-optical guidance. Vehicle-mounted jammers are said to disrupt drones out to a few hundred metres. The author also lists weaknesses. Performance drops sharply at night and in fog or rain, firing at low-flying targets puts civil aviation at risk, and Ukraine is raising raid sizes and improving EW, including against the interceptors. He expects new mounts, better anti-drone ammunition and better interceptors. Single source: this is an article by the Russian pro-government military outlet Topwar that has not been independently verified.
