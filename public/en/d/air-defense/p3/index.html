Voennoe Obozrenie (Topwar), in a piece by Kirill Ryabov, describes 'Pauk' ('Spider'), a Russian acoustic system for detecting drones. The system was first reported by the Russian daily Izvestia on 22 September. The system fits a wider trend of cheap, passive acoustic sensor networks against low-flying drones, which Ukraine has also used at scale. Ukrainian expert: Russia's best missile defences ring Moscow, leaving other strategic sites exposed to Ukraine's emerging ballistic missiles

2026-09-23

Vadym Kushnikov, a military analyst with the Ukrainian defence portal Militarnyi, argued on Ukrainian Radio (reported 23 September 2026 and picked up by Ukrinform, UNIAN and Euromaidan Press) that Russia cannot protect all of its strategic sites against Ukrainian ballistic missiles. In his assessment, Russia does have systems capable of intercepting ballistic missiles — S-300, S-400 and the newer S-500 "Prometey" — but it has concentrated the most capable ones around the capital. He said the most modern S-500 complexes, with 77N6-N and 77N6-N1 interceptors derived from the Soviet-era A-135 Moscow missile-defence system, are deployed in Moscow Oblast. Elsewhere, Kushnikov said, Russia suffers a shortage of air-defence complexes, including S-300 and S-400, relative to the size of its territory — a gap he said is visible in satellite imagery, although exact inventories are not public. According to UNIAN's account, he also pointed to Russia having spent down its interceptor stocks, and concluded that even a strong air defence cannot cover every strategic object across such a vast country, leaving regions outside Moscow more exposed to massed and combined Ukrainian strikes. This matters as Ukraine's deep-strike arsenal shifts from foreign-supplied to domestic weapons: alongside the Neptune and Flamingo cruise missiles, Ukraine now fields the Sapsan, an Iskander-class ballistic missile with a range of about 500 km that President Zelenskyy confirmed in combat use in late 2025. A domestic ballistic missile would allow deep strikes into Russia without Western approval. Kushnikov was frank about the constraints on the Ukrainian side: production sites, many of them in eastern Ukraine, are direct targets of Russian strikes, and key components — notably solid-fuel rocket motors and guidance for a warhead travelling at over five times the speed of sound on a ballistic trajectory — are hard to manufacture; scaling up production is a separate challenge.
