Russia ramps up ballistic strikes on Ukraine using converted S-400 missiles

2026-09-18

Russia has driven its ballistic missile campaign against Ukraine to record intensity through mass use of the RM48U — a converted training/target missile originally built on decommissioned S-400 interceptor missiles (48N6DM family) and used to train air-defence crews — according to a Reuters exclusive based on official Ukrainian data. The missiles are now fitted with warheads and fired from S-400 launch systems on a high, ballistic trajectory toward ground targets. The RM48U is less powerful and less accurate than Russia's primary short-range ballistic missile, the Iskander, deviating from intended targets by roughly 300 meters on average. The scale is substantial: RM48Us made up more than half of the 228 ballistic and hypersonic missiles fired at Ukraine in July and early August 2026, and the tempo has since increased further. Ukraine estimates Russia's stockpile at around 400 RM48Us against only about 100 Iskanders, and Ukrainian military intelligence (GUR) reports Russia plans to raise RM48U production to 480 units in 2026, up from 200 in 2025. A RUSI analyst notes this lets Russia conserve its scarcer precision weapons — Iskanders, KN-23s, and Zircons — for winter use. The shift has had measurable consequences, including a deadly strike on a railway platform near Brovary in early August that forensic analysis attributed to an RM48U, while Russia's defense ministry claimed a different target. Ukraine's stock of Patriot interceptors — its only system capable of intercepting ballistic missiles — is nearly exhausted, and July 2026 saw the highest monthly civilian death toll since the start of the full-scale invasion. Reuters was unable to independently verify Ukraine's forensic findings, and Ukraine's air force has stopped publishing regular interception statistics since August, citing concerns about aiding enemy propaganda.

Ukraine Receives Australian Slinger 30mm Anti-Drone Systems

2026-09-17

Ukraine's armed forces have received Slinger anti-drone modules from the Australian company Electro Optic Systems (EOS), based in Canberra. The system is a remotely operated weapon station built around the 30 mm Bushmaster M230LF cannon, purpose-built to detect and destroy low-flying drones ranging from FPV kamikaze drones to reconnaissance UAVs. Slinger pairs an Echodyne 4D radar with active electronic beamsteering to a 4-axis optoelectronic sighting unit that tracks independently of the gun in both elevation and azimuth, aided by an integrated wind sensor for precision. The fully stabilised mount, weighing under 400 kg, can track and engage moving drones beyond 800 metres using specialised 30x113mm ammunition fitted with radio-frequency proximity fuzes, allowing effective kills against small, fast-moving targets without requiring a direct hit. In Ukraine the modules are being mounted on pickup trucks to form mobile counter-drone fire teams, and the system is also designed for integration onto light armoured vehicles and armoured personnel carriers. The delivery is part of a broader 2023 agreement covering 160 units, with 110 intended for integration onto M113 APCs and 50 ordered by the company Praktika. The systems add a mobile, precision-guided layer to Ukraine's counter-UAS defenses against the ongoing Russian drone and Shahed threat.

Ukraine Fields Italian-Swiss Skyguard/Aspide Air Defense System, Built Around a Cold War Sparrow-Derived Missile

2026-09-16

Ukraine's 38th Anti-Aircraft Missile Regiment has released the first combat footage of a Skyguard/Aspide air-defense system in operational use, showing crews loading missiles and the system standing watch. The Skyguard/Aspide combines a Swiss-made fire-control unit — originally from Oerlikon Contraves, now part of Rheinmetall Air Defence — with Italian-built Aspide missiles. The system can engage targets at ranges of roughly 12 to 25 km and altitudes from 15 meters up to 6,000 meters, and is designed against aircraft, helicopters, cruise missiles and drones. The Aspide missile itself traces its lineage to the American AIM-7 Sparrow, a radar-guided weapon whose development the US Navy began in the late 1940s for Cold War-era bomber interception; Aspide is essentially an Italian evolution of that mid-20th-century design, adapted for today's threats. Ukraine received its Skyguard/Aspide systems as Western aid: Spain began deliveries in November 2022, after training 19 Ukrainian personnel a month earlier at its Zaragoza base, and Italy pledged further units in February 2023. Italian-supplied variants reportedly differ from the Spanish ones, carrying target-illumination radar and TV-tracking equipment mounted directly on the launcher. The deployment comes as Ukraine faces a widening shortage of higher-end interceptors: Patriot missile deliveries are reported to have fallen from 675 units in 2023 to a projected 264 in 2026. Short-range systems such as Skyguard/Aspide are being used against drones and cruise missiles, letting Ukraine conserve its scarce Patriot interceptors for ballistic-missile threats — a trade-off made more pressing as Ukraine enters its fifth consecutive winter of Russian strikes on energy and other infrastructure.

France Tests Tethered Balloon to Protect Nuclear Rafale Airbase From Drone Strikes

2026-09-12

The French Air and Space Force trialled a tethered balloon (aerostat, 12 by 7 metres) as a drone-detection sensor platform at 113th Airbase Commandant Antoine de Saint-Exupéry in Saint-Dizier-Robinson from 31 August to 4 September 2026. Hovering at roughly 200 metres, the balloon carried an EO/IR camera paired with an AI-based threat-detection algorithm feeding real-time imagery to base security; the manufacturer is not named in available reporting. Saint-Dizier is strategically sensitive: it hosts France's Strategic Air Forces, including the nuclear-tasked Rafale squadrons 1/4 Gascogne and 2/4 La Fayette (ASMP-A/ASMPA-R stand-off missiles, roughly 600 km range). The technical rationale is straightforward: fixed ground-based counter-UAS radar struggles against small, slow, low-flying drones because clutter and terrain masking create a blind zone close to the ground and behind obstacles. A sensor elevated to 200 metres gets a look-down angle that covers exactly that gap, and can stay on station continuously for days at a fraction of the cost of a manned or unmanned aerial patrol. The concept itself is not new — similar optronic captive balloons were used to secure French forward operating bases in Afghanistan and the Sahel — but this marks its first application to a home-station airbase with a nuclear mission. The trial fits a broader European pattern: following the wave of drone sightings over airfields and military sites across 2024-2026 (including Volkel airbase in the Netherlands and the Île-Longue submarine base in France, alongside earlier incidents in Belgium, Germany and Denmark), NATO members are seeking cheap, persistent complements to expensive radar networks. The French Air Force explicitly cites Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb — where FPV drones struck Russian strategic bombers on the ground — as the scenario it wants to forestall. Results are still under evaluation, and any operational fielding will depend on the trial's conclusions.

Russian analysis: 'The Evolution of the Geran Drones — Speed versus Range'

2026-09-04SINGLE SOURCE

SINGLE SOURCE — Russian state-aligned outlet (topwar.ru, author Kirill Ryabov). The piece is an analytical overview of how Russia's 'Geran' family of loitering munitions has evolved over four years of use, from slow piston-engine aircraft to fast jet-powered variants. Its central claim is an engineering trade-off: gains in speed come at the cost of range. The piston-powered Geran-2 (an Iranian Shahed-136 derivative, MD550-type engine of about 50 hp) is credited with 170–220 km/h, a range of 1,000–2,500 km, and a 40–50 kg warhead; the Geran-1 is put at ~170 km/h over ~900 km. The jet variants trade that range for speed: Geran-3 (turbojet, JT80-type) at ~350 km/h and 500–550 km, Geran-4 at 300–500 km/h and ~450 km, and Geran-5 at 450–600 km/h and ~950 km. The argument: higher speed sharply compresses the Ukrainian air-defense reaction window. A Geran-2 at 180 km/h covers 600 km in about 3.3 hours, whereas a Geran-5 at 550 km/h does it in a little over an hour. The downsides of jet propulsion are a larger infrared signature, higher fuel consumption (hence shorter range), and probably higher unit cost, while the piston version retains advantages in cost, low detectability, and reach. The article frames the jet variants as an adaptation to a partly depleted Ukrainian air defense rather than a pure capability leap, and references production/localization at Alabuga that ramped up in 2023. It cites Israel's Harop (up to 1,000 km, 16 kg warhead) for comparison. It gives no figures on production volumes, unit cost, or decoy drones (Gerbera). One operational claim is repeated: Ukrainian air defense is said to have intercepted roughly 86% of Shahed-136/Geran-2s between April 2023 and August 2024, with monthly rates of 77–97%. This is a single Russian state-aligned source; the quoted specifications, speeds, ranges, and interception figures are not independently verified. Follow-up (Topwar, 5 Sep 2026, author Aleksandr Marks — single Russian state-aligned source). A companion analytical piece from the same outlet argues that headline "range" figures for long-range strike drones and loitering munitions are computed in at least three incompatible ways: maximum range (under an undisclosed favourable flight profile), range with full payload, and combat radius (ingress plus return with a fuel reserve). A one-way expendable spends on the outbound leg the fuel a recoverable aircraft keeps for the return, but the figures cannot simply be halved because payload, altitude, wind, engine setting, routing and reserves are not disclosed. The author turns this criticism on Russian reporting itself, warning that domestic publications routinely place a manufacturer's spec number, a newspaper paraphrase and an adversary's strike claim side by side, conflating a design characteristic, a programme stage and a combat episode. For air defence it concludes: "compare missions, not kilometres." As with the parent item, this is a single Russian state-aligned commentary with no independent corroboration.
