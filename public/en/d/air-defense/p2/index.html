NATO's top commander Grynkewich outlines a 'theory of victory' against a Russian attack: a drone and robot zone on the eastern flank, long-range strikes on Kaliningrad, the St Petersburg area and Belarus, then a counteroffensive

2026-10-12

General Alexus Grynkewich, NATO's Supreme Allied Commander Europe (SACEUR), set out what he called a 'theory of victory' for the eastern flank at a security conference in Warsaw on 12 October. Meduza bases its account largely on reporting by the Wall Street Journal and Reuters. Grynkewich did not name Russia, but he said any attack on an ally would be 'utterly futile' and that NATO would 'break the warfighting system' used against it, including everything that 'supports, sustains, and controls' the attacking force. According to the reporting, the concept has three layers. The first is an 'autonomous zone', or drone wall, on the eastern flank, where drones and ground robots would slow an advance without heavy NATO casualties. The second is long-range missile strikes on Russian command posts and air defences in Kaliningrad, near St Petersburg and in Belarus, to contest control of the air so that the US Army's V Corps and other allied ground forces can counterattack. The third is air strikes on Russian reinforcements and rear-area targets. A cloud-based network for sharing classified targeting data between allies would link the system; parts of it were reportedly tested during the US attack on Iran. The NATO command-post exercise Steadfast Duel, with more than 20,000 personnel, simulated one million NATO troops repelling an invasion by an army of similar size. Bild reported a comparable Baltic plan in August, which included disabling Russian missile systems in Kaliningrad. Meduza also sets out the Russian response. In September, Russia's embassy in Ireland claimed NATO was preparing an air and naval blockade of Kaliningrad and warned that Russia was ready to use 'any weapon', including nuclear weapons. A Russian note to NATO, obtained by Reuters, accused the alliance of a 'dangerous and reckless course'. Putin said a direct attack on Russian territory would put all of Russia's weapons on the table. NATO says its exercises do not threaten Russian territory. For Russia-watchers, the plan's significance is that it treats Kaliningrad, the Leningrad Military District area and Belarus as early targets for strikes against command and air defence. Moscow's messaging presents exactly that as grounds for nuclear escalation.

Why Does russia Need DF-3 Gadyuka Jet-Powered Interceptor Drone With 800 km Range, How Could It Actually Be Used? (Defense Express)

2026-10-11SINGLE SOURCE

The DF-3 Viper (Russian: Gadyuka) is a concept for a jet-powered interceptor drone with a stated range of 800 km and a total mass of 240 kg, presented by the Russian company DOFA-Tek alongside its other jet-powered drones. These include the DF-1 Roscherk interceptor (250 km range, up to 500 km/h, 15 kg total mass including a 1.5 kg warhead), which according to the company has entered factory testing with a next round of tests scheduled for next month, and the DF-5 Iskra strike drone (1,200 km range, up to 850 km/h, 20 kg warhead, 120 kg total mass). On DOFA-Tek's website both the DF-3 and the DF-5 are labelled "technical proposals", i.e. at an early stage of development; the company has not specified the DF-3's payload capacity or type, and the only concept image published so far resembles the Italian Aeritalia G.91 light fighter-bomber. Defense Express argues that an 800 km range makes little sense for a conventional interceptor: more endurance means more fuel, a larger airframe and higher cost, which could make intercepting cheap UAVs economically unviable. The 240 kg mass, double that of the longer-ranged DF-5, points to a large payload, yet a warhead of tens of kilograms would be excessive for a normal interceptor drone. The outlet therefore hypothesises that the DF-3 may be intended as a reusable 'unmanned fighter' carrying smaller interceptor drones, electronic warfare equipment or even machine guns, engaging several targets across a whole region in a single sortie before returning to its launch point, which would explain the range. It stresses that these are unconfirmed hypotheses until more details emerge. Single source: this is a Ukrainian military-affairs outlet (Defense Express) report that has not been independently verified.

A guided interceptor based on the civilian Alazan anti-hail rocket was displayed at a weapons exhibition at the Chebarkul training range in the Urals on 2 October 2026, which Vladimir Putin toured, Euromaidan Press reports, drawing on an original report and analysis by the Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi. The guided version appeared in Russian state TV (Russia 1) coverage of the visit and in Kremlin pool photos; the Kremlin's own account of the exhibition, which lists more than 300 weapons, does not mention the rocket. According to Militarnyi, the sample is built on the Alazan-2M or Alazan-6 series. In civilian service these rockets reach about 10 km in range and climb to about 8 km; the developers reportedly plan to replace the roughly 0.5 kg weather reagent with a fragmentation warhead. Militarnyi's reading of the published photos (hedged as 'probably') points to four cruciform deflector fins, likely combined with a seeker or fuze, folding stabilisers and a solid-fuel motor. A unit cost of up to $12,000 is cited without a named source. The developer is not identified. The article frames the rocket as one of several Russian attempts to find cheaper answers to Ukrainian drones, noting that Russia's missile-based air defence was designed for aircraft and cruise missiles rather than masses of low-flying drones. Its air-defence role is inferred from the display and not officially confirmed. The Ukrainian outlet Defense Express separately analysed what appears to be the same system (an Alazan-6-based interceptor costing about 1 million roubles); that single-source report is tracked as a separate entry.

Sources: Euromaidan Press

Ukrainian deputy air defense commander says Russia appears to use AI to plan wave attacks on Odesa and for target recognition in Geran-4 drones

2026-10-07

Colonel Yurii Cherevashenko, Deputy Air Force Commander for Air Defense, told Ukrinform (as relayed by Euromaidan Press and Interfax-Ukraine) that Russian forces appear to use artificial intelligence to plan air attacks and to recognize targets. He linked this to recent air raids on Odesa that came in waves with very short gaps between them. According to him, such long, wave-by-wave strikes would be extremely difficult to plan and coordinate without AI. He said: "Russian forces use AI to lay out attack routes and to recognize targets." This is his own assessment, not a confirmed fact: he noted that the scale of Russian AI use is hard to measure, but that Ukrainian specialists see elements of it in how the attacks are organized. The report also states that Russia has equipped its jet-powered Geran-4 drones with machine vision, which lets them recognize and track targets autonomously in the final seconds of flight. A single jet-powered drone hit a locomotive in Odesa Oblast in late September and killed two railway workers. These details come from the Ukrainian-side reporting and are not independently verified here. Cherevashenko also explained why defending the Odesa area is hard. Odesa Oblast hosts Ukraine's largest port, "Southern". Airspace over water is harder to defend than over land: some air defense assets perform worse over the sea, and in some cases crews can engage a target only as it approaches. Storms and fog complicate operations, and the open sea gives Russia more freedom in choosing drone routes. Ukraine is developing AI of its own, including automated turrets with machine vision against drones; Army Media reports Air Force-confirmed shoot-downs, including of Geran-5 drones, with one soldier saying fifty rounds were enough for one Geran. In late September Ukraine's Defense Ministry presented SPECTR, an AI system built by its A1 AI Center that analyzes battlefield data, predicts Russian movements and suggests options to commanders; several combat units are testing it.

Sources: Euromaidan Press · Interfax-Ukraine

Ukrainian Navy says a Sargan 3000 naval drone with a turret shot down a Russian Shahed at sea

2026-10-07

According to the Ukrainian Navy, which released combat footage on its Telegram channel on 7 October, a Sargan 3000 uncrewed surface vessel (USV) shot down a Russian Shahed-type attack drone at sea. The Navy said the Shahed was operating on a mesh network and was hit by fire from a turret aboard the USV; the video shows several bursts of machine-gun fire, after which the drone falls into the sea and explodes. Militarnyi identifies the weapon as the Protector 12.7 mm remote weapon station (RWS) with electro-optical sights — according to Naval News analyst H.I. Sutton, cited by Euromaidan Press, a Kongsberg-made system — and freeze-frames show the Shahed through the Protector's camera. Ukrainska Pravda, Militarnyi and Euromaidan Press give no exact location, date or operating unit. Euromaidan Press notes that Ukrainian sea drones had previously downed Russian aircraft with missiles, but this is the first such kill it describes with a machine gun. It places the engagement in the context of Russian Shahed waves against Odesa and Russia's use, since autumn 2025, of mesh radio networks in which control signals are relayed from drone to drone to ground antennas, allowing operators to re-route Shaheds in flight to evade air defences. Sutton has also written that Russian attack USVs work closely with Shaheds, which can relay the boats' mesh signals. Per Militarnyi, the Sargan 3000 was adopted by the Ukrainian Navy in April 2026 for missions far from the coast, with a combat radius of up to 1,600 km, a speed of 40–55 knots and a payload of up to 450 kg. In September a Sargan 3000 with the same RWS destroyed a Russian USV, described as the first battle between uncrewed naval platforms, and in July one was used to sink a Russian second-rank border patrol ship near Novorossiysk. The reports present the USV as a mobile, armed platform for coastal and air defence over water; the claim comes from the Ukrainian Navy and has not been independently confirmed.

Sources: Ukrainska Pravda (EN) · Militarnyi · Euromaidan Press

russia Developing New $12,000 Surface-to-Air Missile to Counter Drones: What Is Known, What Could Its Specifications Be? (Defense Express)

2026-10-07SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express reports that Russia is developing a surface-to-air missile to intercept drones based on the unguided 82.5 mm Alazan-6 anti-hail rocket. The missile was shown to Putin at an arms exhibition held alongside the Center-2026 exercise, presented as designed to intercept UAVs. Its reported cost is about 1 million roubles (roughly $11,700). This appears to be the same Alazan-derived interceptor that Euromaidan Press and Militarnyi describe from the Chebarkul exhibition; that report is tracked as a separate verified entry. The outlet judges from the missile's dimensions that it is based on the Alazan-6 rather than the out-of-production Alazan-2M or the 60 mm Alazan-9/8M. Baseline Alazan-6 figures given: range up to 12 km, altitude up to 9.3 km, total mass 8.8 kg, 0.66 kg of ice-forming compound and 0.24 kg of self-destruct explosive. Defense Express speculates that the spray payload and self-destruct mechanism would be replaced by a new nose section with control surfaces, a guidance unit and a small warhead; guidance would most likely be radio command from the ground, since a radar or infrared seeker would probably push the cost above $12,000. The launcher has not been shown and could be a modified automated Eliya-2 with 16 Alazan-6 rails. No developer is named. Defense Express concludes the missile is still in development (although the Alazan-6 itself has been mass-produced since the 2000s) but could become a serious problem for Ukrainian drones, including jet-powered UAVs. The specifications beyond the civilian baseline are the outlet's own estimates; it cites no external sources. Single source: this is a report from Defense Express (Ukrainian defence outlet) that has not been independently verified.

Sources: Defense Express

WarGonzo reports that a fighter with the call sign 'Rovny' from the 'Araba' volunteer detachment of the 'BARS-Kursk' brigade shot down a Ukrainian drone at a range of 550 metres with a standard-issue AK-74 assault rifle. According to the post, the drone was small, fast and flying at an altitude of about 70 metres; the channel attributes the hit to skill, composure and a degree of luck, and accompanies the post with video. The channel says the downed drone was controlled via a fibre-optic link — a type that is immune to radio-frequency jamming, which is why small-arms fire remains a last-ditch defence against it. The claimed range and the drone type cannot be verified from the post. Single source: this is a post by a Russian milblogger channel, reflecting a personal/editorial voice rather than an MoD position; milbloggers can contradict each other and the MoD, and the claim has not been independently verified.
