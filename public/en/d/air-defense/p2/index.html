Ukraine lets private air defence groups protect other companies' sites and share systems

2026-11-05

The Ukrainian government has expanded its experimental private air defence project: companies that run their own air defence groups may now protect the facilities of other companies under contract, not only their own sites. Ukrainska Pravda reports this citing the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence. The amended rules also allow air defence systems to be transferred for use between authorised companies, on the basis of a joint decision with Air Force Command. The entry criteria now include status as a resident of Defence City (Ukraine's special regime for defence firms) and/or designation by the Ministry of Defence as a critically important business. Private groups are now allowed to use manned aircraft, the government has regulated companies' use of warheads and electric detonators for interceptor drones, and the minimum age for group members has been lowered from 21 to 18. The private groups remain under the control of the military command and operate within the unified air defence system of the Armed Forces of Ukraine. According to the same report, by 14 October almost 100 companies were taking part in the project and about 50 air defence groups had been formed; groups from six companies had flown combat missions and destroyed more than 60 aerial targets. The step follows an earlier simplification, at the end of October, of the General Staff's rules for radars and EW used by private air defence, and comes as firms such as Nova Post already field their own private air defence unit.

NATO opens counter-drone marketplace with 16 pre-selected systems from five companies

2026-11-05

NATO opened a Counter-Unmanned Aircraft Systems (C-UAS) marketplace on 5 November 2026. Allies can now buy systems that detect, track or defeat drones directly from a NATO catalogue, with no tender of their own. The NATO Communications and Information Agency (NCIA) ran the competition and chose 16 solutions from five companies: Alta Ares, Axon Enterprise, Northrop Grumman, OSL UK and Quantum Systems. The sources do not name individual systems or say how many solutions each company supplies. The only breakdown given is static, transportable and on-the-move systems. NATO says the selection drew on experience from NATO and national innovation activities and from exercises, and that all chosen solutions have already been deployed in Allied countries and in Ukraine. The NCIA, which also hosts the NATO Counter-UAS Centre of Expertise, manages the multi-award framework contracts, which run for 18 months. NATO plans regular competitions to bring in newer technology and has set aside EUR 3.1 million for further testing of the solutions by nations or NATO commands. The marketplace is part of Drone Edge, the initiative announced at the NATO Summit Defence Industry Forum in Ankara in July 2026. Under it, Allies have pledged to invest more than USD 40 billion in counter-drone capabilities over the next five years. The announcement names no buyers or delivery quantities.

Ukrainian military intelligence says Russia can produce over 200 missiles a month, up to 110 of them ballistic, as Ukraine intercepted only about 7% of ballistic missiles in October

2026-11-04

Ukraine's military intelligence agency (HUR) said on its Telegram channel on 4 November that Russian industry can produce more than 200 ballistic and cruise missiles per month, up to 110 of them ballistic, and keeps increasing output despite sanctions. As of October, HUR estimates Russia holds up to 700 ballistic missiles and several thousand cruise missiles. According to HUR, about 115 of the ballistic stock are 9M723 Iskander-M missiles (with production of roughly 65 a month), the 3M22 Zircon stockpile likely exceeds 120 (more than 30 built in the first seven months of 2026), Kalibr stocks exceed 450 with a significant share sent to the strategic reserve, Oniks stocks exceed 600 (fitted with a stronger warhead and longer range for ground strikes; more than 55 made in seven months), and Kinzhal production is temporarily paused over accuracy problems. HUR did not say how it obtained the data; the figures have not been independently confirmed. The gap with Ukraine's defences is stark. According to the Ukrainian air force, Russia fired roughly 150 ballistic missiles in October; the Defence Ministry reports that 11 Iskander-M or North Korean KN-23 missiles were intercepted (up from 8 in September), about 7%. By contrast, the General Staff says 4,859 of 6,473 Shahed-type drones were destroyed in October, with 66% of jet-powered and 89% of propeller-driven drones stopped. On 3 November, in Russia's biggest attack on energy facilities this year, Zelensky said 17 ballistic missiles were launched and, per the air force, only three were shot down. The ministry itself said ballistic missiles remain a separate challenge requiring better countermeasures. In a Euromaidan Press analysis, David Axe cites forecasts by missile expert Fabian Hoffmann and analyst Anastasiia Lapatina that Ukraine could face 130 to 190 ballistic projectiles per month through winter 2026–2027, including S-400 missiles fired in ground-attack mode (60–200 over four months) and 5–15 Zircons a month. Ukraine has around eight Patriot batteries and two SAMP/T batteries, but interceptor supply is the bottleneck: the 500 PAC-3 missiles ($2.1 billion) approved on 31 October will take years to arrive, Greece and Spain have declined to give up Patriot missiles, and the Freyja domestic air-defence project still depends on European seekers, radars and integration.
