Kyiv's air defence crews struggle to keep pace with Russia's jet-powered Geran drones

2026-10-26

A Kyiv Independent field report (26 October 2026), based on two days with Ukraine's 112th Separate Territorial Defense Brigade outside Kyiv, describes how jet-powered Geran drones, used against Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast since late summer, are straining the capital's layered air defence. Waves of jet drones now arrive day and night between larger mass assaults. Some variants fly at about 600 km/h and at 5–6 km altitude, beyond the reach of mobile machine-gun crews. Soldiers say drones change route and manoeuvre when interceptors approach, and that some newer Geran variants carry cameras and mesh radios, giving operators a live video feed and the ability to redirect them in flight. The report details each layer. Radar and other sensors pass direction, altitude and distance via combat command posts to three-man mobile fire groups armed with a truck-mounted Browning M2HB, a SkyLock targeting system and thermal imagers. They fire only within assigned sectors and must calculate the lead themselves. One gunner says he has about five seconds to engage, and another says a high-flying jet drone was barely visible to him. An Igla (9K38) MANPADS operator says that, unlike propeller Shaheds, jet drones and cruise missiles give almost no audible warning, and some cross his engagement area in about 20 seconds. Operators of Sting interceptor drones (about 320 km/h top speed) say they struggle to catch the faster jet drones. On 18 October, Brave1's then-head Andrii Hrytseniuk said seven Ukrainian interceptor types had already downed jet drones, that 22 teams were developing high-speed models (some with miniature jet engines), and that more than 1,000 had been ordered, though production is still being scaled up. NASAMS and IRIS-T remain the main systems against cruise missiles and aircraft, and Patriot against ballistic missiles. The brigade's EW/ELINT chief (call sign "Secret") says jamming and spoofing can only complement kinetic interception, because some drones fly on to pre-programmed coordinates without a datalink. His unit analyses wreckage to track how Russia keeps changing drone communications. He says the "fashion" changes every three to six months, so new equipment must be added while old systems stay in service. An officer describes Russian probing with small salvos from all directions to map routes ahead of a mass attack. Crews have worked 20-hour shifts during intense periods, which one commander calls a tactic of "constant exhaustion". All of this is the brigade's own account, as reported by the Kyiv Independent.

Ukrainian drone and missile attack on Voronezh kills two and injures 17; VASO aircraft plant and Sibur rubber plant reportedly targeted

2026-10-26

Ukrainian forces struck the Russian city of Voronezh with drones overnight on 25–26 October; Meduza also reports missiles, and the regional governor declared a missile alert, though no missile type has been named. Governor Aleksandr Gusev said, citing preliminary data, that two people were killed and 17 injured, including a child; many of the injured were on an Anapa–Moscow passenger train hit by drone debris. Russian Railways said a carriage was damaged at Somovo station in the Voronezh region, five passengers were hurt and traffic on the line was halted. Gusev claimed 113 drones were downed over the region (Meduza quotes him as saying 117), more than 70 of them over the city itself; Russia's MoD claimed 290 drones downed over 15 regions and the Moscow area. These are Russian official claims and have not been independently verified. According to the independent Telegram channel Astra and eyewitness footage, drones hit several apartment buildings and the 25-storey Galereya Chizhova business centre, and cars burned in nearby car parks; residents told Astra that air-raid sirens did not sound. Gusev listed fires at several industrial enterprises, which he said had no serious consequences, plus damage to an apartment block and a school stadium. Euromaidan Press notes that residential damage may have been caused by downed drones or by falling Russian air-defence missiles. Ukrainian Telegram channel Supernova+ reported that the targets included the VASO aircraft plant (part of Ilyushin / United Aircraft Corporation, about 190 km from the border), which builds Il-96 airliners and supplies pylons, engine nacelles and flap rails for the Il-76MD-90A military transport, and Sibur's Voronezhsintezkauchuk synthetic-rubber plant; it also claimed an attack on the city's Northern Bridge. Kyiv has not commented, so damage at VASO remains unconfirmed. Voronezh was last attacked on 23 October, when drones targeted the Baltimor military airfield; the Tolyattikauchuk rubber plant in Samara Oblast was reportedly attacked again the same night as Voronezh.

2026-10-26SINGLE SOURCE

In an opinion piece, Topwar columnist Yevgeny Fedorov argues that slow piston-engined long-range strike drones are losing effectiveness on both sides. He says Russia was first to notice the problem with its own Geran drones: they were slow enough for Ukraine to move air-defence teams onto their routes and shoot them down with cheap interceptor drones. Russia's answer was jet-powered strike drones, culminating in the Geran-5, which he says Ukrainian propeller interceptors cannot catch and which leaves Ukraine relying on MANPADS. He claims Russia produces 'at least 3,000' jet kamikaze drones a month with localised engines, while Ukraine lacks small-turbojet technology and has only the jet-powered 'Palianytsia' in small numbers. On defence, he credits systematic protection of Russian refineries against slow drones for ending this summer's fuel shortages and lifting rationing in some regions. He cites a 2026 CIAM (Central Institute of Aviation Motors) study, published in the journal Aviatsionnye Dvigateli by Lukovnikov and Zinenkov. It modelled an 8,000 kg UAV with turbojet, turboprop, piston and electric power: piston engines gave the longest range at low altitude, while turbojets perform best at 11-13 km. From this he argues that a Ukrainian deep-strike jet drone would have to fly high and would be intercepted. On the latest raid on St Petersburg, he says Ukraine launched 'up to a thousand' drones but only 15 reached the city. He reports one person killed and about 15 injured, a VK data centre and an Ozon warehouse that kept operating, and Pulkovo airport closed for six hours. He puts the cost of the attack to Kyiv at $60-80 million. He warns that Ukraine could pick unprepared targets such as Yandex (he says rooftop chiller units at data centres were left uncovered), shift its mass launches to a 200-500 km band, or turn to attacks on crowded places. The piece is strongly polemical, including language about the 'Zelensky regime', and gives no sources for its production and raid figures. «How Much Will Shershen Air Defense System Cost, and What About Its New Missiles?» (Defense Express)

2026-10-26SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express describes Shershen, a Ukrainian modular short-range surface-to-air missile system intended to engage aircraft, helicopters, cruise missiles, UAVs and precision-guided munitions, including under heavy electronic warfare. According to the developers, its radar detects targets out to 85 km and tracks up to 24 at once; depending on the missile, the engagement envelope is 1.5–25 km in range and 20 m–10 km in altitude, against targets up to 1,000 m/s. It can engage six targets simultaneously with a single-shot kill probability of 0.75. Its open architecture accepts Soviet-era R-27 and R-73 and Western AIM-9 and AIM-132 air-to-air missiles, launched from the Boryey launcher. Two new missiles resemble the R-27 family: the R-287T with the Ukrainian GS-203T imaging infrared seeker, said to lock onto a typical target at least 30 km head-on and over 100 km from behind, and the R-287 with a Ukrainian active radar seeker that, unlike the semi-active R-27R/ER, needs no continuous target illumination (comparable in concept to NASAMS with AMRAAM). The article links this to other Ukrainian SAM work such as the 'S-300 Clone' and GAM-67 under the US-Ukrainian FrankenSAM initiative, but stresses that none of these has entered serial production and technical challenges remain. Shershen has completed testing and its components are codified, yet no procurement decision has been taken. On cost, the article compares Norway's 2023 NASAMS purchase for Ukraine ($860 million for four batteries, about $215 million each) and a current NASAMS III price of €410 million with the developers' preliminary export estimate for Shershen of €150–170 million; the price of the active-radar missiles is unknown. Developers claim Shershen could be built much faster than the wait for new NASAMS or IRIS-T SLM. This is a single source from Defense Express (Ukrainian defence outlet) relaying developer claims, not independently verified; performance figures are manufacturer data.

Russia builds outer air-defence ring of new towers near Putin's Valdai residence, bringing the total to about 50 positions, roughly half the number around Moscow

2026-10-25

Russia has built more than a dozen new towers for air-defence systems around Vladimir Putin's residence at Valdai (Novgorod region) in October, bringing the number of air-defence positions in the area to about 50, or by some counts more than 50. The finding comes from Radio Liberty's (RFE/RL) Russian Service, based on satellite imagery analysed jointly with an OSINT researcher who posts on X as @jembobineuse (jembob); Radio Liberty journalist Mark Krutov mapped the positions. Ukrainska Pravda and Militarnyi report 16 new towers, Meduza's summary of the same investigation 'at least 15'. They were built within a 50 km radius, mainly south of the residence on the side facing Ukraine, forming an additional outer ring. The journalists assume they are meant for Pantsir-S1 gun-missile systems or Tor surface-to-air missile systems. No new positions have yet been found in the northern part of the outer ring; these may follow in the coming months. The new ring runs past Khotilovo military airfield, which is used for Putin's flights and is linked to the Valdai residence by a dedicated railway line; one tower there was built as early as March 2026. Several more positions sit on the shore of the Vyshny Volochyok Reservoir, around Lisiy Island, where according to an earlier Radio Liberty investigation the Russian military built a laboratory complex that may be linked to a biological weapons programme; construction of two towers near that facility began in early September, around the time that investigation was published. Meduza adds the wider picture from the OSINT analyst's count: Russia now has about 177 towers carrying short-range air-defence systems, of which about 98 are in and around Moscow and about 50 near Valdai. Fewer than 100,000 people live within 50 km of Valdai, against more than 20 million in the capital region. The build-up has been gradual: the first air-defence system near the residence appeared in January 2023 and a second in July 2024; by April 2026 satellite imagery showed at least 27 units around it, after seven Pantsir towers were added in March. Russian authorities claimed that more than 90 Ukrainian drones tried to attack the residence in late December 2025; Ukraine denies this, and according to Meduza the CIA concluded that the target of the Ukrainian drone attack in Novgorod region was an unspecified military facility not near the residence. Militarnyi also points to the parallel expansion around Moscow: about 24 new Pantsir towers were reported in July, and at least seven more in June after a Ukrainian drone attack in May. The findings rest on open-source satellite analysis and have not been confirmed by Russian authorities; the exact counts differ slightly between reports.

Sources: Ukrainska Pravda (EN) · Militarnyi · Meduza (EN)

Russian unit fired ~100 Lancets in three months for only three hits, pro-Russian figure Gubarev claims

2026-10-25

A Russian drone team fired about 100 Lancet loitering munitions over roughly three months from mid-June and hit only three targets, according to pro-Russian militant Pavel Gubarev, the self-declared 2014 'people's governor' of Donetsk who now comments on the war on social media. Defence Blog reports his account via the Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi. Gubarev said the drones were launched by a team from the 'Sparta' unit under Senior Lt. Kirill Ozerov (call sign 'Nevsky'), without naming the sector of the front. The three hits were a self-propelled artillery gun, an infantry dugout and a roadside shop. Most of the other drones were neutralised by Ukrainian air defences, and some were destroyed by Russian forces themselves because units coordinated poorly. Gubarev put the price of one Lancet at 3 to 5.5 million roubles ($37,000–67,000), so the three hits cost roughly 300–500 million roubles (about $3.7–6.1 million). Gubarev blamed the commanders rather than the operators: in his account they used the expensive drones without proper preparation and sent reports of supposed successes up the chain of command. 'The questions are for the commanders,' he said. The claim comes from one partisan pro-Russian voice and has not been independently confirmed. Defence Blog adds background. The Lancet family is built by ZALA Aero, part of the Kalashnikov Group. It has X-shaped double wings, an optical seeker in the nose, an electric pusher motor, a catapult launch and a briefcase-style control station. Its stated range is over 40 km, and the distances to targets it has struck suggest a maximum of about 50 km. Some versions carry a KZ-6 shaped-charge warhead of about 3 kg, reportedly held in the fuselage with mounting foam. For much of the war the Lancet was one of Russia's most effective weapons against Ukrainian artillery and armour. Ukraine now shoots down growing numbers of them, including with interceptor drones that hunt them in the air.

Lithuania unveils X-12 jet-powered interceptor drone aimed at Russia's Geran-5, reportedly combat-tested in Ukraine

2026-10-25

Lithuania has presented the X-12, a turbojet-powered interceptor drone built to destroy fast aerial targets, according to Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi, which cites the Lithuanian news site Delfi. The primary target named is Russia's jet-powered Geran-5 attack drone. The project is linked to the Strategic Defense Alliance (SDA), an association of Ukrainian, Lithuanian and Latvian defence-technology companies. No single manufacturer is named.
