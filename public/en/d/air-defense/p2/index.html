Russia's campaign against Kyiv's 'power ring': the capital's own CHP generation is out and strikes on 330/750 kV substations raise the prospect of 8-20 hour daily blackouts this winter

2026-10-24

In an analysis for Ukrainska Pravda (24 October 2026, translated from Ekonomichna Pravda), energy journalist Mykola Topalov describes how Russia is systematically attacking Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast with two aims: to destroy the city's combined heat and power (CHP) plants and to break the so-called 'power ring'. That ring is the system of high-voltage lines and 330 kV substations linking the capital to the national grid from several directions, with the 750 kV Kyivska substation as a key node through which power from the Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear plants reaches the city. Kyiv normally uses 1-1.2 GW (12-15% of national demand), rising to 1.8-2 GW in winter. Before the large-scale winter attacks of 2025, local generation from CHP-5, CHP-6 and Darnytsia CHP (CHP-4) supplied up to about 1.3 GW, according to an energy-company source. Those plants can no longer generate electricity, yet all three were hit again on the night of 13-14 October. The article explains the tactical logic. Russia does not need to destroy the whole ring: damaging a few critical nodes that carry a large share of the flow is enough. Power may then exist in the system but cannot be delivered to Kyiv in sufficient volume, forcing dispatchers into emergency or scheduled cuts. The ring's key components have been under constant attack for more than a month. Repairs are slowed by repeat strikes, long air-raid alerts, risks to repair crews, and government plans to cut the share of energy workers exempt from mobilisation. Replacing a large transformer takes much longer if no spare is held in reserve. The article does not give numbers of missiles or drones per strike. It stresses that protection of these sites against ballistic missiles depends mainly on air defence, that the engineering protection built at most substations cannot guarantee equipment survives a direct hit, and that Patriot interceptors are in short supply. The article sets out three winter scenarios. If repairs keep pace and new strikes cause no critical losses, outages would be about 8-10 hours a day. With continued attacks and lingering damage during moderate cold, they would be 12-16 hours. The worst case, regular large-scale strikes on key substations during severe frost, would mean up to 20 hours a day. Energy-sector sources nevertheless do not expect Russia to 'plunge Kyiv into darkness' entirely. Critical infrastructure (water pumps, heating, transport, hospitals, communications) needs about 200 MW. According to Ekonomichna Pravda's sources, 150 MW of distributed generation is already running, rising to 180-185 MW by the end of 2026, and private plants could add another 55-60 MW before winter, about half of which are ready. The scenarios are the outlet's assessment based on unnamed energy-sector sources.
