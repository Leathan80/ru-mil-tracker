In an opinion piece, Topwar columnist Yevgeny Fedorov argues that slow piston-engined long-range strike drones are losing effectiveness on both sides. He says Russia was first to notice the problem with its own Geran drones: they were slow enough for Ukraine to move air-defence teams onto their routes and shoot them down with cheap interceptor drones. Russia's answer was jet-powered strike drones, culminating in the Geran-5, which he says Ukrainian propeller interceptors cannot catch and which leaves Ukraine relying on MANPADS. He claims Russia produces 'at least 3,000' jet kamikaze drones a month with localised engines, while Ukraine lacks small-turbojet technology and has only the jet-powered 'Palianytsia' in small numbers. On defence, he credits systematic protection of Russian refineries against slow drones for ending this summer's fuel shortages and lifting rationing in some regions. He cites a 2026 CIAM (Central Institute of Aviation Motors) study, published in the journal Aviatsionnye Dvigateli by Lukovnikov and Zinenkov. It modelled an 8,000 kg UAV with turbojet, turboprop, piston and electric power: piston engines gave the longest range at low altitude, while turbojets perform best at 11-13 km. From this he argues that a Ukrainian deep-strike jet drone would have to fly high and would be intercepted. On the latest raid on St Petersburg, he says Ukraine launched 'up to a thousand' drones but only 15 reached the city. He reports one person killed and about 15 injured, a VK data centre and an Ozon warehouse that kept operating, and Pulkovo airport closed for six hours. He puts the cost of the attack to Kyiv at $60-80 million. He warns that Ukraine could pick unprepared targets such as Yandex (he says rooftop chiller units at data centres were left uncovered), shift its mass launches to a 200-500 km band, or turn to attacks on crowded places. The piece is strongly polemical, including language about the 'Zelensky regime', and gives no sources for its production and raid figures. War on the Rocks: deception against automated sensing and decision chains, drawing on Russo-Ukrainian combat lessons

2026-10-23

In an analysis for War on the Rocks, Édouard R. Maurin (call sign FIDES) argues that concealment alone no longer protects manoeuvre on a battlefield made transparent by sensors and drones. Maurin is a French veteran who served in 2024–2025 with an international volunteer section attached to a Ukrainian special-purpose battalion near Pokrovsk. He also runs Fog Defence, a company that develops decoys, which is a commercial interest in the subject. His argument is that deception should target the whole automated chain of sensing, sensor fusion, classification and decision, not only the human commander. He wants it judged on profitability: does the adversary spend more engaging a decoy than the decoy costs? He also wants it nested from section to corps level under one signature-management system. He adds that US doctrine (JP 3-13.4, FM 3-13.4) still assumes a human decision-maker. Most of the evidence comes from Russia and Ukraine. Maurin describes a fake radio call at Kotlyne on 5 January 2025, transmitted while Russian forces were listening, after which two soldiers left a bunker about two minutes later; he watched this himself through a drone's thermal camera. He also mentions feigned holding of terrain at Pokrovsk while survivors were evacuated. On the Russian side he cites Gerbera foam and wooden Parodiya decoy drones fitted with Luneburg lenses to mimic the radar signature of a Shahed/Geran. According to media reports, these decoys make up about half of the swarms, and Alabuga reportedly keeps their cost below $10,000. He contrasts Western interceptors costing hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars with Ukraine's Sting interceptor (about $2,500). He also quotes Unmanned Systems Forces commander Robert 'Magyar' Brovdi, who puts a mass-produced kamikaze drone at about $400. The article also describes Operation Vivaldi (September 2026). There, Ukraine's Third Army Corps first degraded Russian EW and drone-pilot teams and then used feints and fake signatures to retake the Nove settlement and its heights. According to deputy commander Maksym Zhorin, an EW 'protective dome' disrupted or suppressed about 20,000 Russian drones. Corps commander Andriy Biletsky called deception a key factor in the success. Maurin recommends company-level signature fleets, battalion-level signature management cells, and procurement of cheap active decoys. He states explicitly that the effect on automated classifiers is his hypothesis and has not been observed. Much of the Russo-Ukrainian evidence rests on media and unit reports and has not been independently verified.

Ukrainian 'Nemesis' drone detachment caused about 70% of a US armored brigade's early casualties at Hohenfels, brigade commander writes

2026-10-20

Ukrainian drone operators playing the enemy in a US Army exercise caused about 70% of an American armored brigade's casualties in the first three days, according to an article in Military Review, the US Army's professional journal, reported by Defence Blog. The authors are Col. Michael Ziegelhofer, commander of the 3rd Armored Brigade Combat Team, 1st Cavalry Division ("Greywolf"), and six of his officers. They describe Combined Resolve 26-07, held in spring 2026 at the Hohenfels Training Area in Bavaria, where the opposing force was reinforced by a detachment of the 412th Regiment "Nemesis" of Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces. Over the roughly 10-day rotation the opposing force initiated contact with drones more than 750 times, either striking troops directly or spotting for artillery. By the end of day three the share of drone-caused casualties had fallen below 30%, without the enemy easing off: "Greywolf changed how it fought". According to the authors, roughly 60% of the brigade-level counter-drone fight is fieldcraft and drills. The brigade's counter-drone/EW systems jammed control links in protective bubbles up to 2 km wide, but had to be set up at fixed sites and moved forward in bounds, leaving units exposed on the move, and were less effective against the smaller number of unjammable fiber-optic drones. More than half the formation relied on cover, concealment, dispersion, smaller routes and fast battle drills. Survival had a price: crews switched off Blue Force Tracker, cut radio use and even dropped Starshield satellite links to reduce their electronic signature, and resupply took twice as long as planned because the enemy hunted supply lines. On the offensive side, the brigade brought in the 3rd Battalion, 504th Parachute Infantry Regiment (then an experimental drone battalion) and used more than 700 drones from platoon to brigade level; with relay drones, nearly all could fly 25-40 km, covering the whole ~14 x 27 km training area. That abundance initially overwhelmed the headquarters: collection-planning tools, the operations floor and per-drone airspace management could not keep up, and the staff never had a full picture of how many drones were airborne. The brigade adapted by giving drone units priorities and letting them hunt within the commander's intent, moving the coordinated fire line forward, making enemy drone ground control stations a top target and holding drone munitions in reserve; it ended the rotation with two simultaneous breaches about 6 km apart that the opposing force failed to stop. The officers recommend multifunctional drone strike troops in armored brigades (long-range surveillance to at least 40 km, medium/heavy bomber drones, strike drones with relays), one common operational picture for all drones modelled on Ukraine's Delta system, and counter-drone defences that work on the move, from hard-kill protection on every vehicle to brigade-level radar and defeat systems. This is a different, earlier exercise than the Stork and MV7 trials covered previously; it is one of the clearest first-hand accounts of how a NATO heavy brigade fares against Ukrainian-style drone TTPs, which mirror what Russian forces face and increasingly employ themselves. The figures come from the brigade's own after-action account and concern a training environment.
