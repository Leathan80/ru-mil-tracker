NATO uses Ukrainian forces and Ukrainian-made sea drones as 'red team' in exercises to 'trigger an adaptation cycle', says ACT commander Vandier

2026-11-11

NATO is using Ukrainian forces as the opposing 'red team' in its exercises to 'trigger an adaptation cycle' within the alliance, Adm. Pierre Vandier told Breaking Defense. Vandier is Supreme Allied Commander Transformation, heading NATO's Allied Command Transformation (ACT). He said NATO had taken Ukrainian forces and Ukrainian-made uncrewed surface vessels (USVs), hidden them, and made them the red team against NATO's blue force. He did not name specific exercises. Ukraine's Defence Ministry has said that in the 2025 REPMUS exercises off Portugal, Ukrainian red-team forces defeated NATO members at sea in five out of five combat scenarios, reportedly in part using USVs. Vandier said that an amphibious operation today would face both naval and aerial drones. In his words, no operation can be planned without counter-drone defences and the ability to counter incoming swarms. NATO is also running an initiative called 'Fight Club' at its Joint Warfare Centre. It is a forum of external experts and NATO staff that builds 'military vignettes': focused hypothetical scenarios that test concepts and doctrine to find their limits. As an example, he cited testing how many drones in a swarm it takes to overwhelm a navy. Vandier also said Russia has kept pace in undersea warfare, pointing to the Yasen-class nuclear-powered attack submarines. He called anti-submarine warfare one of the alliance's main areas of concern. Separately, a NATO official told Breaking Defense last month that Russian naval presence in the Mediterranean had declined significantly, with a two-month naval pause there that ended in early September.

War on the Rocks analysis by ex-PM Honcharuk: Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces compete in a 'Time Domain' of adaptation speed, a model Russia has copied

2026-11-10

In an opinion-driven analysis for War on the Rocks, Oleksiy Honcharuk (Ukraine's prime minister in 2019-2020, former adviser to the first commander of the Unmanned Systems Forces, chair of the Aerorozvidka supervisory board and, since 2025, chairman of the defence-tech firm UFORCE) argues that the decisive dimension of the war is how fast each side adapts. He calls this the 'Time Domain': a contest over the interval between a new capability reaching the front and the enemy's working counter, which can now shrink to weeks. Ukraine created the Unmanned Systems Forces against opposition from career generals; he states that with about 2.5% of personnel the branch accounted for roughly a third of Russian losses in the past year. On Russia, Honcharuk credits it with being first to field jam-resistant fibre-optic drones at scale, around Kursk, through Rubicon, a defence-ministry centre that links developers, staff officers and operators in one loop; that lead lasted months, until Ukraine had more than 20 certified fibre-optic models from 11 producers by April 2025. He writes that Putin ordered a dedicated branch in December 2024 and that Russia's Unmanned Systems Forces were operational by late 2025, which he reads as Moscow copying a form that works. He presents Ukraine's 412th Brigade 'Nemesis' and Lasar's Group as 'Combat Hubs' that fuse combat, analysis, R&D and production on daily, six-weekly and six-monthly cycles; the Nemesis figures (over 520 targets destroyed for 107 airframes lost in seven months) come from the project's own records, not public data. He urges Western militaries to track adaptation time in planning, buy adaptation cycles rather than platforms, train against unpredictable opposition and create a small dedicated unmanned branch, and suggests a visible adaptation capacity could deter, while warning that a rapidly closing window can also provoke an aggressor. This is an argument piece by an author with a stake in Ukrainian and British-Ukrainian defence technology, not a neutral assessment; his loss figures and claims about Russia's force structure are not independently sourced in the article.

2026-11-07

In a Bloomberg interview, Lieutenant General Pasi Välimäki, commander of the Finnish Army, said Finland is looking at Ukrainian-style anti-tank ditches and obstacles for its border with Russia. Finland shares 1,340 km of border with Russia, about half of NATO's land border with the country. Välimäki noted that Ukraine has used extensive defensive lines and anti-tank obstacles to slow attacking forces, and that Russian forces have not found a reliable way through them. His main reservation is the climate. He asked how long it would take for deep ditches like Ukraine's to turn into ponds in Finland's harsh northern conditions. No decision or construction has been announced. Finland cannot stop that rearmament. Its goal is deterrence: convincing the Russian military that an attack would fail. Finland, with 5.6 million people, can mobilise about 280,000 troops in wartime. Since the full-scale war in Ukraine began, it has put 60,000 reservists through refresher training in two years. Finland is reorganising its land forces through the end of the 2030s. Välimäki said the basic deterrence approach has not changed, only the scale needed. TASS, a Russian state news agency, carried the same Bloomberg interview with further points. Välimäki said supply lines to front-line troops must be kept open in a long conflict, and that Finland does not want "a war of attrition". He wants mass production of supplies that can be sustained in peacetime and expanded tenfold in a crisis. As part of NATO deterrence on the eastern flank, the army must be able to strike missile and drone launch systems before they are used against Finnish territory. TASS added Vladimir Putin's statement at the Valdai Club that Russia has no intention of attacking anyone. The Bloomberg original was not available; these details come from the two reports above.

Drapatyi's first 100 days as Ukraine's commander-in-chief: assault-regiment audit, mission command and new appointments

2026-11-05

Major General Mykhailo Drapatyi (43) marked 100 days as commander-in-chief of the Ukrainian Armed Forces in late October, the Kyiv Independent reports (Asami Terajima, Francis Farrell, Jimmy Rushton). He was appointed in July after President Zelensky dismissed Oleksandr Syrskyi amid mass protests in Kyiv. According to the outlet he inherited an understrength, overstretched army with little strategic reserve, while Russia's summer push in Donetsk Oblast had reached deep into Kostiantynivka and Ukraine's Patriot stocks had run out. Almost immediately he ordered an audit of personnel use and paused replenishment of the largest Assault Forces regiments, which had a reputation for high losses and misuse of soldiers. Instead of disbanding them he placed them under strong corps: the 425th 'Skelia' under the National Guard's 1st Azov Corps near Dobropillia, the 225th under the Air Assault Forces' 7th Corps. New appointees include Serhii Sobko as deputy commander-in-chief, Sviatoslav Zaiets as Ground Forces commander and ex-Unmanned Systems Forces commander Col. Vadym Sukharevskyi as head of the Marines. The central change is command style. In a September manifesto Drapatyi wrote that he will command 'through intent' and not run every battle. FPRI analyst Rob Lee, citing brigade commanders, says corps commanders now have autonomy over holding positions and counterattacks, where previously they could not even authorise withdrawal from positions of questionable value. An anonymous deputy brigade commander says units were told that orders deemed impossible or too costly would no longer come with the threat of dismissal; former Azov chief of staff Lt. Col. Bohdan Krotevych and Kraken regiment commander Oleh Romanov are also positive. Not everyone sees change: an anonymous senior corps officer in Kharkiv Oblast says 'not much has changed' there. Drapatyi has given no press interviews since his appointment; the assessment rests on commanders and experts. Challenges named by the outlet: an expected renewed Russian offensive on the remaining Ukrainian-held part of Donetsk Oblast, continued disruption of US-supplied air defence interceptors and possible Starlink/Starshield 'malfunctions', reform of training centres and after-action reviews towards NATO practice, and the political risk that his popularity makes him a perceived rival to the President's Office, as happened with Valerii Zaluzhnyi.

NATO is drafting a defence plan that foresees conventional strikes deep inside Russia on bases, depots and industry, the WSJ reports

2026-11-04

NATO is revising how it would counter a Russian attack and is developing a defence strategy that drops earlier restrictions in favour of conventional strikes far inside Russian territory, according to The Wall Street Journal as reported by Ukrainska Pravda. After nearly five years of war in Ukraine, and with lessons from the Middle East, Alliance strategists have reportedly concluded that defending the eastern flank with barriers and checkpoints alone cannot stop an invasion. Deterrence now requires "denial": not only hitting troops crossing the border but also military bases, supply depots and industrial facilities behind the front line, on Russian soil. The WSJ notes the plan does not envisage a transition to nuclear weapons. Supreme Allied Commander Europe Gen. Alexus Grynkewich has set out this approach in his own "theory of victory": the Allies first block the initial wave of an attack and then take away the enemy's ability to keep pushing, by hitting "both advancing forces and anything and everything that supports, sustains or controls that force". Polish Maj. Gen. Ireneusz Nowak said NATO would want to strike Russia's defence-industrial base in a war and should treat deep precision strike as deterrence, adding that the message to Moscow must be credible and therefore backed by actual capabilities. Ukraine's ambassador to NATO, Alyona Getmanchuk, likened air defence to a painkiller that eases symptoms while the source of the pain must be targeted. To support the approach, the US is modernising and replenishing its Tomahawk cruise missile and ATACMS ballistic missile stocks, while European partners aim to expand Storm Shadow/SCALP and Taurus inventories and develop new low-cost long-range strike systems. Fabian Hoffmann of the Norwegian Institute for Defence Studies said at least 48 low-cost cruise missile projects have been announced in Europe. The plan itself has not been published; the details rest on the WSJ's reporting.

Ukrainska Pravda column: Russia's army adapts tactics under pressure but its top-down command culture persists, citing Voyennaya Mysl studies

2026-10-08

In an opinion column in Ukrainska Pravda (8 October 2026), Liubov Tsybulska, a Ukrainian strategic-communications and hybrid-threats expert and director of the NGO Join Ukraine, compares Russian and NATO officer training, drawing on a Join Ukraine study of how Russia prepares officers for war. She contrasts NATO mission command, a professional NCO corps and the blame-free After Action Review with Russia's Soviet-derived top-down command, in which junior officers rather than sergeants carry small-unit leadership and admitting mistakes invites punishment, so reports to superiors are often embellished. She cites early-war failures at Hostomel airport, the Siverskyi Donets river crossing and Chornobaivka airfield as examples of units losing time while waiting for orders. The column draws on two articles in the Russian MoD journal Voyennaya Mysl, without giving issue numbers or author names. The first, from 2023, surveyed more than a hundred mostly tactical-level artillery officers, 81% of them with combat experience. Only 12% chose the line of 'reasonable initiative', 40% named 'excessive tutelage and commanders' distrust of subordinates' as the obstacle, and only 30% supported a ban on punishing failed initiative. The authors also noted that the concept of 'initiative' does not appear even in the document governing education at MoD universities. In the second article, researchers at the General Staff Military Academy acknowledged that small Ukrainian units with one or two guns and a single, often civilian, drone, coordinating horizontally, struck Russian columns and withdrew before battalion tactical group (BTG) commanders could assess the situation, turning the BTG into a 'big cow' against a pack of small wolves. Their proposed remedy is 'counter-planning': the concept flows top-down, while tasks, coordination and support requests flow bottom-up. Tsybulska calls this mission command in all but name, and notes that the authors based it on the experience of the Taliban and the Ukrainian armed forces, not on NATO doctrine. According to the column, the Russian forces have nonetheless adapted since 2022. They restructured logistics against HIMARS strikes, changed armour use in response to FPV drones, integrated commercial UAVs into reconnaissance at scale, shortened the detect-to-strike cycle and expanded small assault groups. Junior commanders also informally delegate responsibility to experienced soldiers. Change is fastest in C4ISR, UAV-operator training and the transfer of front-line experience into higher military education, at institutions such as the Mikhailovsky Artillery Academy, the Military Academy of Army Air Defence, the Military Academy of Communications and the Military University of Radio Electronics. Tsybulska argues that these changes do not make the Russian army Western. She warns that Russia could combine new technology and tight control with a low value placed on soldiers' lives. This is an opinion piece by a Ukrainian author; the Voyennaya Mysl findings are quoted second-hand, have not been checked against the original articles, and their exact issues are not identified.
