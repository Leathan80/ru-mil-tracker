Poland switches to sensor-based identification for shooting down aerial objects during Russian strikes on Ukraine, as Sweden returns Gripens to guard the Rzeszów aid hub

2026-09-30

Poland put simplified procedures for detecting and shooting down foreign objects in its airspace into force on 1 October, Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz announced, according to Ukrainska Pravda citing European Pravda and Gazeta Prawna. Deputy Defence Minister Cezary Tomczyk explained on 28 September that the change mainly concerns how Polish pilots on airspace-protection missions during Russian strikes on Ukraine identify targets: the peacetime requirement to identify a target visually before engaging it is replaced by dynamic, sensor-based identification. Most aircraft will operate from bases in Sweden with only a small forward presence in Poland; the number of jets and the duration were not disclosed. Jonson cited 'repeated violations of NATO airspace' as the reason for high readiness on the eastern flank. Swedish Gripens guarded the same hub before, from May 2025 (six Gripen C and about 120 personnel of the Blekinge Wing), after a first deployment of six Gripens to Malbork in March 2025.

France Tests Tethered Balloon to Protect Nuclear Rafale Airbase From Drone Strikes

2026-09-12

The French Air and Space Force trialled a tethered balloon (aerostat, 12 by 7 metres) as a drone-detection sensor platform at 113th Airbase Commandant Antoine de Saint-Exupéry in Saint-Dizier-Robinson from 31 August to 4 September 2026. Hovering at roughly 200 metres, the balloon carried an EO/IR camera paired with an AI-based threat-detection algorithm feeding real-time imagery to base security; the manufacturer is not named in available reporting. Saint-Dizier is strategically sensitive: it hosts France's Strategic Air Forces, including the nuclear-tasked Rafale squadrons 1/4 Gascogne and 2/4 La Fayette (ASMP-A/ASMPA-R stand-off missiles, roughly 600 km range). The technical rationale is straightforward: fixed ground-based counter-UAS radar struggles against small, slow, low-flying drones because clutter and terrain masking create a blind zone close to the ground and behind obstacles. A sensor elevated to 200 metres gets a look-down angle that covers exactly that gap, and can stay on station continuously for days at a fraction of the cost of a manned or unmanned aerial patrol. The concept itself is not new — similar optronic captive balloons were used to secure French forward operating bases in Afghanistan and the Sahel — but this marks its first application to a home-station airbase with a nuclear mission. The trial fits a broader European pattern: following the wave of drone sightings over airfields and military sites across 2024-2026 (including Volkel airbase in the Netherlands and the Île-Longue submarine base in France, alongside earlier incidents in Belgium, Germany and Denmark), NATO members are seeking cheap, persistent complements to expensive radar networks. The French Air Force explicitly cites Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb — where FPV drones struck Russian strategic bombers on the ground — as the scenario it wants to forestall. Results are still under evaluation, and any operational fielding will depend on the trial's conclusions.
