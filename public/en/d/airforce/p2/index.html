Russia's glide bomb strikes hit new monthly record in August 2026

2026-09-10

According to figures from Ukraine's General Staff, compiled and circulated via the OSINT monitor Oko Gora, Russia dropped a record 8,766 glide bombs on Ukrainian positions and cities in August 2026 — the highest monthly total since the start of the full-scale invasion. That works out to an average of roughly 283 bombs a day. The figure continues an upward trend that has run almost without interruption since early 2025 (when monthly totals were closer to 3,500), and it surpasses the previous highs of June (8,266) and July (over 8,300). Most of these weapons are Soviet-era FAB free-fall bombs fitted with a UMPK kit (pop-out wings plus satellite guidance), letting Russian strike aircraft — chiefly Su-34s — hit targets tens of kilometres away without approaching Ukrainian air defences. That standoff range has continued to grow in recent years through improved kits. Ukrainian countermeasures cited include attempts to intercept Russian carrier aircraft before release using F-16s (armed with AIM-120 AMRAAM), ground-based GPS/EW jamming that can degrade UMPK guidance accuracy, and the anticipated arrival of Swedish JAS 39 Gripen jets carrying Meteor air-to-air missiles. Ukraine's Ministry of Defence describes countering Russian tactical aviation as "one of Ukraine's key challenges."

Ukraine strikes Novorossiysk naval base; Navy claims Neptune hit on Kalibr frigate Admiral Essen

2026-09-09

On the night of 8–9 September 2026, Ukraine carried out a large combined deep-strike against Russia's naval base and commercial port at Novorossiysk (Krasnodar Krai) and against targets near Anapa and Gelendzhik. The Unmanned Systems Forces (USF), Defence Intelligence (HUR/DIU), the Security Service (SSU) and the Navy were all involved. USF commander Robert "Madyar" Brovdi said the Novorossiysk naval base "lost a number of military vessels, including missile carriers" — a statement Militarnyi attributed directly to Brovdi in a dedicated 9 September article, though that article does not itself name individual vessels. The Novorossiysk mayor confirmed a fire but blamed falling debris. According to the Ukrainian Navy, the Project 11356R frigate Admiral Essen — a Kalibr cruise-missile carrier — was hit again by a Ukrainian-made R-360 Neptune missile, starting a fire on the ship's superstructure (per HUR). Ukraine's General Staff separately reported that the same operation also struck a second Kalibr-capable frigate, the Admiral Makarov, the Project 21631 small missile ship Buyan-M, and the Project 22160 patrol ship Vasily Bykov — meaning four vessels rather than the Admiral Essen alone. Satellite imagery published by Exilenova+, and reported independently by Defence Blog on 10 September, shows fires and apparent damage at the port consistent with the reported hits on the Admiral Essen, the Admiral Makarov and the Buyan-M — the first (partial) visual corroboration of the strikes, though the source remains Ukrainian (an OSINT channel, not Russian or third-party confirmation), and Ukraine's own General Staff says assessments of some of the reported strikes are still being finalized. The Admiral Essen was previously struck on the night of 1–2 March 2026 and again in August 2026 (alongside the Admiral Makarov and the Vasily Bykov); that August damage is the same event described earlier in tracker entry e-20260823-05. Russian state news agency TASS reported four people killed (including a child) and 29 injured in Novorossiysk, with damage to residential buildings, an Orthodox church and a kindergarten; Defence Blog cites similar figures. Russia's Defence Ministry claimed air defences shot down 596 Ukrainian fixed-wing drones nationwide. Moscow has issued no account of its own regarding damage to the four named warships, and neither TASS nor any other Russian source acknowledges naval losses. The Neptune hit, the number of vessels struck, and the extent of the damage therefore remain Ukrainian claims — now backed by satellite imagery from a Ukrainian OSINT channel, but still not independently confirmed by Russia or a third party.

Suspected Russian Shahed-type drone violates Moldovan and Romanian airspace, crashes in Moldova

2026-09-08

On 8 September 2026 an uncrewed aerial vehicle violated the airspace of both Moldova and NATO member Romania before crashing on Moldovan soil. According to Moldova's Defence Ministry, air surveillance systems detected the drone at 4:20 p.m. local time as it entered the country from the direction of Ukraine. The military tracked it until it left Moldovan airspace at 4:37 p.m. heading toward Golaiesti in Romania; Romanian radars lost contact after 5:00 p.m. The drone ultimately came down in the area of Mihaileni Vechi, Moldova, roughly 25 km east of the Romanian border near Botosani County, where dry grass caught fire at the impact site. Moldovan officials identified the aircraft as a 'Shahed-type' drone, an Iranian-origin design Russia uses in its strikes on Ukraine. As the drone crossed into Romanian airspace, Spanish F/A-18 fighter jets operating under NATO's air-policing mission were scrambled from Mihail Kogalniceanu Air Base. Romanian authorities issued emergency alerts to residents across multiple regions, instructing them to shelter in basements or civil-protection shelters, and Iasi International Airport suspended takeoffs and landings until the drone left the area. Moldovan authorities monitored the incursion throughout and confirmed the airspace violation. The sources place the incident within a sustained pattern of spillover from Russia's air campaign against Ukraine into neighbouring states. Kyiv Independent notes that Russian drones and missiles have routinely violated the airspace of both Moldova and Romania in recent months, citing a NATO fighter jet shooting down a suspected Russian drone over Romanian airspace near the Moldovan border on 16 August 2026 and a Russian cruise missile entering Moldovan airspace on 17 August 2026 during a strike on Odesa.

Romania says it disrupted a Russia-run sabotage-reconnaissance operation scouting NATO bases and An-124 airlifters

According to the SRI, an intermediary instructed him to take photos and videos of targets of strategic military interest. Notably, the man was specifically tasked against Ukraine's Antonov An-124 "Ruslan" heavy cargo aircraft — the strategic airlift that moves weapons and equipment into Ukraine — giving the case a direct connection to the supply chain sustaining Kyiv. The SRI assessed that the tradecraft (reconnaissance and imagery by a recruited operative, run through a cut-out intermediary) matched sabotage networks attributed to Russian handlers elsewhere in Europe, and pointed to an incident at Leipzig airport where an explosives-rigged drone was found near an An-124. The case fits a sustained run of arson, drone incursions and covert surveillance across NATO's eastern flank. Details of formal charges or expulsion had not been released at publication; the man was detained in Romania. The information comes from the SRI and Romanian and Ukrainian media.

Russia markets 'combat-proven' weapons for export at Egypt's El Alamein Airshow, claims new Su-57E deals

2026-09-07

Ahead of the El Alamein International Airshow (EIA 2026, Egypt, 8–10 September), Russia's state arms exporter Rosoboronexport and the United Aircraft Corporation (UAC) mounted a sales push built around systems Moscow calls "combat-proven" in Ukraine. Rosoboronexport CEO Alexander Mikheyev said on 7 September that Russia has signed new export contracts for the Su-57E, the export version of its fifth-generation fighter, with deliveries starting in 2026; he named no buyers and disclosed no values. UAC planned a Su-57E flight demonstration and the public debut of two new air-launched weapons: the S-71M "Monokhrom" (a jet-powered loitering munition with autonomous target recognition) and the S-71K "Kovyor" (a low-observable guided munition for fixed targets). Mikheyev asserted the Su-57 "has proven its superiority in actual combat" against an adversary equipped with modern air defence and electronic warfare, and that acquiring it would give a partner air force "a long-term strategic advantage in the region" — without offering combat data. He said the Yak-130M "can meet the combat aviation needs of most sub-Saharan African countries," and that the Lancet-E had "demonstrated excellent performance in real-world conditions." UAC director general Vadim Badekha called North Africa an important market. Caveat: Defence Blog is the only independent source here. Defence Blog notes Russia has largely kept the Su-57 out of contested airspace over Ukraine, using it mainly for standoff cruise-missile launches, following setbacks including a June 2024 Ukrainian drone strike on a Su-57 on the ground at Akhtubinsk and an October 2024 friendly-fire episode in which a Su-57 shot down Russia's own S-70 Okhotnik drone.

Swedish Gripen Fires Taurus Cruise Missile for First Time Ahead of Schedule, Opening 350km+ Strike Capability Relevant for Ukraine's Future Fleet

2026-08-23SINGLE SOURCE

The development is relevant to Ukraine: Kyiv is set to receive both used Gripen C/D jets and newer Gripen E aircraft in the coming years, making this integration milestone potentially significant for Ukraine's future air fleet and the threat picture it could pose against targets inside Russia. A major obstacle remains, however: German export restrictions currently prevent Ukraine from accessing Taurus missiles, despite Germany being one of Ukraine's largest arms suppliers.

Sanctions Push Russia's Aviation Industry Into Crisis, Forcing Rostec Restructuring

2026-07-28SINGLE SOURCE

Ukrainian defense-news outlet Defense Express reports, citing Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service (SZRU), that Western sanctions have pushed Russia's aviation industry into a structural crisis. Since 2022, sanctions have cut Russia off from Western engines, avionics, precision components and maintenance support, driving what the report describes as growing technological isolation of the sector. In response, state conglomerate Rostec is consolidating several aviation businesses under a single holding company, to be managed by Ilyushin Finance Co.: passenger carrier Red Wings, cargo airline Sky Gates, medical aviation assets, and leasing firm Aviacapital-Service. Officially, the merger is framed as centralizing management and establishing a unified maintenance system. According to the SZRU, however, the real purpose is to obscure the deteriorating financial condition of the individual companies by merging their financial reporting into one holding. Concrete symptoms of the crisis cited include: at Red Wings, only one of three Boeing 777 aircraft remains in scheduled passenger service; Sky Gates is struggling with shortages of engines and spare parts and extended maintenance cycles due to an aging fleet; and the domestic Tu-214 airliner program reportedly meets only around 10% of its planned production output. Military programs such as the Tu-160 and Tu-22M3 are also linked to this broader production strain. CAVEAT — this is a single-source report: the information comes exclusively from the Ukrainian outlet Defense Express, which in turn relies entirely on a statement by Ukraine's foreign intelligence service SZRU. The article does not name specific sanctions packages, dates, or verified financial figures, and none of the claims have been independently confirmed by Russian, Western, or other sources.

Why Does Russia Need More Than 40 MiG-31 Kinzhal Carriers if It Has Only About 100 Missiles?

2026-07-21SINGLE SOURCE

According to an analysis by Defense Express (Ivan Kyrychevskiy, July 20, 2026), Russia now operates more than 40 MiG-31K/I aircraft converted to carry the Kh-47M2 Kinzhal hypersonic missile, while the stockpile of that weapon stands at only around 100 units, according to Ukraine's Defense Intelligence directorate (HUR). Production runs at roughly five missiles per month, with a target of 60 for all of 2026, and the weapon has reportedly been used only eight times so far this year. HUR says Russia has deliberately slowed both production and operational use of the Kinzhal because of its high cost and relatively limited combat effectiveness. The number of Kinzhal-capable MiG-31s grew from 24 at the end of 2023 (per The Military Balance) to 40+ now, an increase of at least 16 airframes over two and a half years. Since Russia no longer manufactures new MiG-31 interceptors, these additional carriers almost certainly come from long-term storage stock, estimated at 130-150 airframes as of 2018. The newest variant, the MiG-31I, adds automatic navigation to the missile launch point as its main improvement over the earlier MiG-31K. The author himself calls the logic behind this fleet expansion 'questionable': with 40+ carriers against roughly 100 missiles in stock plus an expected production of about 30 more this year, Russia effectively has only about three missiles available per aircraft. The article exposes this mismatch but does not offer a firm explanation for the scale-up itself — it frames it mainly as an open question about Russian weapons logistics. Note: this is a single-source analysis from Defense Express, a Ukrainian military-analysis outlet, and its figures and conclusions have not yet been independently corroborated by a second source.
