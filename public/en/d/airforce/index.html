Poland's operational commander says the air force is on nightly maximum readiness against Russian drones and has switched from visual to positive identification to engage them faster.

2026-10-14

Russian drone incursions are forcing the Polish Air Force to operate at the highest readiness every night, putting heavy strain on personnel and equipment, Maj. Gen. Ireneusz Nowak, operational commander of the Polish Armed Forces and responsible for Polish airspace, said at the Warsaw Security Forum, as reported by Breaking Defense. He said 80 percent of his attention goes to air defence, while Poland's integrated air defence modernisation is only about 30 percent complete: new systems are being procured, but the ground and air components are still three to four years away. To bridge the gap, the air force changed its rules of engagement on 1 October: instead of Visual Identification (VID), in which a pilot must confirm a target's type, markings or nationality by sight before engaging, it now applies Positive Identification (PI), allowing engagement based on radar data, other sensors and intelligence, including information passed on by Ukraine. "So now we are much more flexible, much more responsive," Nowak said. He acknowledged that using fighter jets against cheap drones is a poor cost ratio, but said there is currently no alternative, and called for "massive, responsive" counter-UAV weapon systems. Norway's military representative to NATO, Lt. Gen. Rolf Folland, said the air superiority model of recent decades is under pressure because munitions cannot be expended "in one blast" but must be used again and again. Near-term options are delayed. Integration of APKWS laser-guided rockets on Polish F-16s is held up because Poland could not obtain the certified fuzes used by the US Air Force due to business restrictions and is waiting for a new fuze type. Nowak called the F/A-50GF, which the defence minister announced has entered combat duty, a very promising drone hunter against the current Russian threat, but said weapon integration means it will only be fully operational in about two years - "the time is too long," he said.

Russia builds the first three of 16 large bomber shelters, each about 63 x 63 m, at Engels-2 after repeated Ukrainian drone strikes (Defence Blog)

2026-10-13

Russia has nearly finished the steel frames of the first three of 16 planned large aircraft shelters at Engels-2 in Saratov Oblast, the main base of its strategic bomber fleet. Defence Blog reports this based on satellite imagery published by the open-source analysis group AviVector. An Airbus Defence and Space image dated 12 October shows three shelter frames side by side on new concrete pads, each connected to the airfield by a freshly paved taxiway. AviVector first saw ground being broken in imagery from 5 September. AviVector estimates that each shelter measures about 63 by 63 metres, with 7-metre side walls and a peak height of about 17 metres. That is large enough for a Tu-160, which is about 54 m long with a wingspan of almost 56 m when its wings are fully extended, and for the smaller Tu-95MS. Earth berms behind the frames deflect jet blast upward. This suggests that aircraft are meant to start their engines and taxi directly from the shelters. The frames have not yet been clad, the wall and roof materials are unknown, and no completion date has been given. The Russian Ministry of Defence has not commented. The construction follows repeated Ukrainian long-range drone attacks on Engels, about 600 km from the front line, since December 2022. Those attacks first hit fuel and ammunition storage and, in recent months, parked aircraft. According to President Zelenskyy, the SBU destroyed a Tu-95 at Engels in July, and satellite imagery taken days later appeared to show a wrecked bomber. A second Tu-95 was reportedly damaged in an SBU drone strike on 28 August. Until now the bombers were parked in the open. Shelters would hide which aircraft are present and offer some protection against drone warheads and debris. Russian crews had already painted outlines of Tu-95s on the apron as decoys. Russia can no longer build new Tu-95s, so every airframe lost permanently shrinks the long-range fleet.

Ukrainian intelligence: Kh-47M2 Kinzhal shares ~90% of its components with the Iskander-M's 9M723, and 58 Russian firms make it

2026-10-12

On 12 October Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR/DIU) published a breakdown of the Kh-47M2 Kinzhal and its production chain, including an interactive 3D model on its War&Sanctions portal, as reported by Militarnyi and Euromaidan Press. HUR gives the missile the Russian industrial designation 9-S-7760 and describes it as a single-stage, solid-fuel aeroballistic missile. According to HUR, about 90% of its structural components and systems are the same as, or very similar to, those of the 9M723 ballistic missile fired by the Iskander-M system. The Kinzhal-specific features listed are a tail aerodynamic fairing that separates after the motor ignites, a radar homing seeker, an onboard active jamming (EW) station and a decoy-release system meant to complicate interception. HUR also states that the active radar seeker advertised for the Iskander "exists only on paper" and has not been documented in strikes on Ukraine. According to HUR, the Kinzhal can carry either a penetrating high-explosive warhead in a thick-walled steel casing, designed to break through floors, concrete and fortified structures, or a nuclear warhead. Russia is reportedly developing a high-explosive fragmentation warhead. HUR links this to possible accuracy problems, since a penetrator only works with very precise guidance. HUR further says the Russian MoD sharply cut its 2026 state order for Kinzhal and moved production capacity, components and raw materials to 9M723 production for Iskander-M. It gives a second reason: the MiG-31I/MiG-31K carrier fleet is limited, and Russian industry no longer builds the MiG-31. HUR counts 58 Russian enterprises in the Kinzhal production network and says 22 of them are not sanctioned by any member of the sanctions coalition. Neither outlet names individual companies. Euromaidan Press recalls that Ukraine's sanctions commissioner Vladyslav Vlasiuk said in September that US-made Texas Instruments processors had again been found in a newer Kinzhal version. It also notes that Ukraine's MoD says Patriot PAC-3 can intercept the Kinzhal. The release appeared on the same day the EU sanctioned 1,569 individuals and entities tied to Russia's defence industry. All of these claims come from Ukrainian official sources, a party to the conflict, and have not been independently verified. The Euromaidan Press page is labelled as advertorial content.

Third Yak-130M prototype flies as Russia turns its jet trainer into a radar-equipped light combat aircraft

2026-10-11

The third prototype of the Yak-130M, an upgraded version of the Yak-130 jet trainer reworked to carry radar and guided weapons, has made its first flight from the Irkutsk Aviation Plant, according to Russia's United Aircraft Corporation (UAC). The aircraft, side number 137, was flown by Yakovlev test pilots Oleg Mutovin and Alexander Guskov; UAC said all systems worked normally but did not give the date of the flight. According to UAC, the third test aircraft should allow trials to finish on schedule and deliveries to begin in 2028; it did not name a first customer or planned numbers. UAC says the jet can use a wider range of guided and unguided weapons by day and night and in all weather. When the first prototype was unveiled in 2025, Rostec pitched the aircraft partly as a drone hunter, able to intercept UAVs and carry air-to-air missiles as well as satellite- and laser-guided bombs. Yakovlev first showed the Yak-130M as a mock-up at Army-2024, framing the upgrade as a way to make the aircraft more competitive for export and announcing three test aircraft. The first prototype made its maiden flight on 25 June 2026 and a second was rolled out in October 2025. The only export customer named so far is Vietnam: in July, the head of the Irkutsk Aviation Plant said during a visit by Vladimir Putin that a contract was being signed for 18 Yak-130Ms. These are manufacturer claims that have not been independently verified.

2026-09-21SINGLE SOURCE

ArmyInform, the information agency of Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, reports comments by Colonel Artem Skuratovskyi, head of the air defence department of Ukraine's 7th Rapid Response Corps, made on Radio NV on 21 September 2026. He argued that the most effective answer to Russian guided glide bombs (KABs) is not to intercept the bombs but to shoot down the aircraft that release them. According to Skuratovskyi, Russia uses KABs intensively against infantry positions, and in one month it directed about 120 KABs at the Sumy direction alone. In his view the situation will only improve once Ukrainian aviation can hit Russian aircraft at ranges comparable to the KAB release distance. For that, Ukraine needs long-range air-to-air missiles such as Meteor (for the Gripen), AIM-120 (for the F-16) and MICA (for the Mirage). The argument reflects a long-standing Ukrainian view: glide bombs are released far outside the reach of most ground-based air defence, and the bombs themselves are hard and costly to intercept, so the carrier aircraft is the more practical target. The article gives no new data on intercepts or aircraft losses. This is a single-source report from ArmyInform (Ukrainian MoD media, i.e. a party to the conflict), not independently corroborated.

Ukraine's private air-defense program grows from 24 to 81 companies in five months

2026-09-16

Ukraine's Ministry of Defense reports that 81 private companies have now been authorized to run their own air defense against Russian drone and missile strikes, protecting factories, warehouses and energy infrastructure. The pilot program, launched in late 2025, grew from 24 participating firms in April 2026 to 30 in June, 51 in July, and 81 by mid-September — roughly one new company joining per day since July. A further 92 firms have applied to join. The underlying driver is that, despite Ukraine's layered air defense system, the state cannot protect every target from the relentless Russian Shahed and missile campaign against production sites and their workers. The private units consist of trained, certified civilian teams operating mobile fire posts with heavy machine guns and interceptor drones. They do not act independently but remain subordinate to Air Force Command within the national airspace-management structure, preventing uncoordinated firing in shared airspace. By mid-2026 the private units had already shot down more than 20 Russian attack and reconnaissance drones, including one jet-powered Geran variant. The program's stated goal is to detect every aerial threat and destroy 95% of incoming drones. The rapid growth — more than tripling in five months — reflects both the intensifying threat to Ukrainian industry and the limited capacity of state air defense to cover every target. The program illustrates a broader pattern of civil-military adaptation, with companies taking on their own protection within a state-controlled framework rather than relying solely on government-provided defenses.
