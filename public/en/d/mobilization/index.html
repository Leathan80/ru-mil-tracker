RUSI: Russia has not avoided mobilisation but normalised it into a permanent, regionalised recruitment system

2026-09-29

In a RUSI commentary published on 29 September, Zoe Neiman argues that asking whether Putin will announce a new mobilisation misses the point: since 2022 the Kremlin has turned mobilisation from a single, visible political event into a permanent process, pushed down to regional governments and aimed at Russians least able to refuse. The September 2022 partial-mobilisation decree (around 300,000 reservists) was never rescinded, so men mobilised then cannot be discharged except on narrow grounds. Since 1 January 2026 conscription has been a year-round administrative process (target: 261,000 conscripts in 2026), and a December 2025 decree allows reservists to be called up for training with the armed forces, National Guard and security agencies, creating the basis for rolling call-ups without a formal announcement. According to the author, money rather than compulsion has so far been the engine, but it is becoming expensive. Federal budget data point to around 71,000 contract soldiers in Q1 2026, the lowest first-quarter figure in three years and about 20% down year on year, even as the average enlistment payment hit a record 1.47 million roubles in March; that is roughly 70% of the pace needed for the 2026 target of about 409,000 (Medvedev claims some 200,000 contracts in the first half-year, which is hard to reconcile). Regions carry much of the cost despite record deficits, and coercion fills the gap where budgets are thinnest: forced detentions and pressure to sign contracts are documented in at least 17 regions (including raids in Penza, Ulyanovsk and Rostov), recruiters face quotas of 20 to 30 men a month, and targets include men with debts, criminal cases or addictions, convicts and students. Foreign recruits (over 1,400 verified Africans; Kenya reports over 1,000 Kenyans) and North Korean troops (about 11,000 in Kursk Oblast at the start of 2026) are numerically marginal but politically useful, because none belongs to the constituency whose mobilisation would cost the Kremlin most. RUSI does not see an imminent crisis: the system is durable enough to avoid a new nationwide call-up, but depends on keeping recruitment costs dispersed. Western estimates put Russian losses at over 30,000 killed and wounded a month against roughly 27,000 recruits, and at least 28,000 desertion convictions have been recorded. The indicators to watch are whether signing bonuses keep rising, whether coercion spreads beyond poorer and remote regions, and whether reservists move from training into sustained deployment; a formal mobilisation would signal that the quiet system has run out of people or regional money. The author concludes that Russia's manpower constraint is as political as it is demographic. This is an analytical commentary; the views are the author's own and not RUSI's.

ISW assesses Russia's mass drone strikes aim to force Ukraine's capitulation by spring 2027

2026-09-28

The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) assesses that Russia is running a large-scale long-range strike campaign, built on jet-powered drones and ballistic missiles, with the aim of forcing Ukraine to capitulate by spring 2027, as reported by Euromaidan Press. ISW calls this Putin's theory of victory: launching cheap Geran (Shahed-type) jet-powered drones in such numbers that enough of them get through Ukrainian defences. The figures cited: Russian use of jet-powered drones rose eightfold between June and August; about 7,700 have been used since their first launch; more than 2,730 Shahed drones were launched in September alone, of which Ukraine intercepted or suppressed over 1,500. On 26 September Zelensky put the interception rate for jet-powered Shaheds at 55%. ISW says the approach relies on Ukraine's shortage of Patriot interceptors against ballistic missiles, the degradation of the power grid and Ukraine's limited air defence resources. On 25 September Putin said Ukraine 'must feel' Russia's 'retaliatory' strikes, which ISW reads as a sign of intensification over the winter. Possible disruptions: Presidential Office head Kyrylo Budanov indicated that mass deployment of Ukrainian interceptor drones could begin within months, though it is uncertain whether it comes in time for the winter escalation. ISW also assesses that Putin may postpone large-scale forced mobilisation until the results of the drone campaign are clear, despite groundwork already being laid. Note that the capitulation timeline is an ISW assessment, not a stated Russian aim, and the figures on launches and interceptions come from Ukrainian and ISW reporting.

Report: up to 25,000 North Koreans may be building military drones for Russia on student visas

2026-09-17

According to a report by the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team (MSMT), published on September 16, 2026, Russia and North Korea have built an extensive system to evade UN sanctions on North Korean labor migration. The report's estimates range from 15,000 to 30,000 North Korean workers employed illegally in Russia in 2025; the majority of them — possibly up to 25,000 people — are believed to have been working on military drone production, particularly at the Alabuga industrial site in Tatarstan, which produces Shahed/Geran attack drones among other systems. The workers reportedly enter Russia through a purpose-built student visa scheme that formally registers them as students rather than employees, circumventing the work permits that would otherwise be required. The report also describes the use of tourist visas, changes to Russian law allowing student-visa holders to earn income, and the bribing of Russian universities to sustain the arrangement. Each worker is said to generate roughly $7,500 a year — up to five times what comparable work pays in China — with most of the wages handed over to the North Korean state. North Korea's overseas labor program is estimated to have generated between $450 million and $800 million in 2025, revenue the sanctions monitors say helps fund Pyongyang's nuclear and ballistic missile programs. UN Security Council resolutions have banned member states from employing North Korean laborers since 2017; if the findings hold, Russia and the workers involved are in ongoing violation of those obligations. The MSMT — an 11-country group including the US, South Korea, and Japan — is the sole source for these figures, which are the monitoring group's own estimates and have not been independently verified.

Russia's recruitment of foreign nationals amounts to human trafficking, Amnesty International says

2026-09-10

In a report published on September 10, Amnesty International concludes that the way Russia recruits foreign nationals for its war against Ukraine amounts, in many cases, to human trafficking. According to Ukrainian figures, 27,407 foreign fighters had been identified on the Russian side as of March 30, drawn from at least 135 countries. Russia primarily targets vulnerable people from low-income countries in the Global South; nationalities named include Sri Lankans, Brazilians, and citizens of Egypt, Somalia, Iraq, Algeria, Syria, Morocco, and Jordan. The recruitment methods described are systematically deceptive and coercive. Recruiters advertise fake jobs — for instance as cooks or in IT — that end in front-line deployment, and exploit migrants' precarious status in Russia by weaponizing overstayed work visas or alleged criminal offenses, presenting enlistment as an alternative to detention or deportation. Victims often sign contracts in a language they do not understand, receive as little as two weeks of training before combat deployment, have their passports confiscated, and are threatened if they try to leave. Amnesty Secretary General Agnès Callamard states that the level of exploitation and coercion involved leads the organization to conclude that in many cases this amounts to human trafficking as defined under the UN's Palermo Protocol, and calls on Russia to immediately end all abusive practices in the military recruitment of foreign nationals. Neither article records a Russian government response to the allegations. Ukraine's Coordination Headquarters reports that one to three third-country citizens are captured weekly through its "I Want to Live" surrender initiative.

Moscow Military District to form reservist drone-interceptor units for capital air defense

2026-09-09

On 9 September 2026 the exiled Russian outlet Meduza, citing the state news agency TASS, reported that the Moscow Military District will form dedicated drone-interceptor units manned by reservists to reinforce the air defense of "critically important facilities" around the capital. The units are to operate high-speed interceptor drones (FPV interceptors) as an added layer above existing point air defense. According to the district's press service, combat-experienced instructors are being retrained first, after which training of the reservists themselves will begin; no deployment date has been announced. Meduza notes that reservists have already been used in a comparable rear-area counter-drone role in the Leningrad region since spring 2026. The move operationalizes a legislative package adopted in late 2025. A State Duma law (submitted 23 October 2025, signed by Putin in November) for the first time allows "rezervisty" — volunteers under a standing contract with the Defense Ministry, drawn largely from the BARS reserve created in 2021 — to be summoned in peacetime for "special gatherings" (spetssbory) to protect critical and life-support infrastructure, explicitly including energy and transport sites, without a formal mobilization declaration. Putin's 30 December 2025 decree ordered mobilization-reserve citizens sent to such "special" assemblies for facility protection in 2026; the government compiles the list of protected sites and the Defense Ministry designates the responsible units. Officials (Vice Admiral Tsimlyansky) have said such call-ups carry extra pay, last no more than two months, and see reservists serve in their home regions. This is the Moscow region's concrete application of that framework and a modest escalation of it: earlier descriptions centered on "mobile fire teams" with truck-mounted machine guns and small arms, whereas the new Moscow Military District units are to field interceptor drones. The step follows sustained Ukrainian long-range strikes on Russian refineries and energy infrastructure and repeated drone approaches to Moscow. It runs parallel to e-20260906-11 (Putin backing medically "category D" unfit volunteers for rear-area counter-drone duty) but rests on a different legal track and a different personnel pool. The unit formation and instructor training are officially confirmed via TASS and the Defense Ministry; Meduza, an independent outlet in exile, frames the broader reservist call-ups — consistent with ISW and other analysts — as a further stage of "covert mobilization."
