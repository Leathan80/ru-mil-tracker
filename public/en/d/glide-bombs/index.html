Ukrainian intelligence: Russia can probably add 10–15 Su-34s a year, but builds no new Tu-95s, Tu-22M3s or Su-24s

2026-10-26

In a reply published by the Ukrainian news agency UNIAN on 23 October, Ukraine's military intelligence agency HUR said Russian aircraft plants still produce modern fighter-bombers and fighters, enough to partly cover losses. Older bombers are different: Russia has no serial production of new Tu-95s, Tu-22M3s or Su-24s and keeps them flying through repairs and upgrades, so every permanent loss leaves a lasting gap. HUR estimates Russia can probably deliver 10–15 Su-34s a year now; deliveries rose from eight in 2022 to 11 in 2023 and 12–14 in 2024, though HUR says that trend does not confirm the same pace today. For the Su-35S it cites an average of about 11–12 a year in 2021–2025 as a rough benchmark, not an established 2026 output. Su-30SM2 and Su-57 production continues, but reliable rates cannot be determined, partly because Su-30 figures mix new builds with upgrades of older Su-30SMs. By the end of 2025, HUR estimates, Russia had about 125 Su-34/Su-34M bombers, 140–145 Su-30s of various versions, 135–140 Su-35S and about 20 Su-57s. The agency stresses these are fleet estimates, not the number of jets available for combat at the same time, since some are in repair or upgrade, and it does not disclose the current fleet size. The Su-34 is one of the main carriers of Russia's glide bombs. Ukraine's Navy said Neptune missiles hit six Su-34Ms at Borisoglebsk airbase (Voronezh Oblast) overnight into 15 October; Zelenskyy later said four were destroyed and two damaged. HUR also listed 2026 losses: a Tu-22M3 crash in Irkutsk Oblast on 15 June; two Tu-95MS hit at Engels on 17 July and 28 August (the first with critical tail damage, the second a probable irrecoverable loss); a Su-35 Ukraine says it shot down on 8 July; a Su-57 crash in Moscow Oblast on 23 July; and a collision of two Su-35S over Kursk Oblast on 7 September. Strikes on several Su-57s and a Su-34 at Shagol on 25 April and on a Su-24 in Crimea on 7 September were reported, but HUR says counting them as destroyed would be premature. Euromaidan Press notes the published list omits the Borisoglebsk Su-34s and a Tu-95MS that Russia's defence ministry said crashed on takeoff in Amur Oblast in late September. All figures are Ukrainian intelligence estimates and have not been independently verified.

Ukraine says it has obtained a Russian plan for a winter strike campaign against its critical infrastructure. Russia's glide bomb strikes hit new monthly record in August 2026

2026-09-10

According to figures from Ukraine's General Staff, compiled and circulated via the OSINT monitor Oko Gora, Russia dropped a record 8,766 glide bombs on Ukrainian positions and cities in August 2026 — the highest monthly total since the start of the full-scale invasion. That works out to an average of roughly 283 bombs a day. The figure continues an upward trend that has run almost without interruption since early 2025 (when monthly totals were closer to 3,500), and it surpasses the previous highs of June (8,266) and July (over 8,300). Most of these weapons are Soviet-era FAB free-fall bombs fitted with a UMPK kit (pop-out wings plus satellite guidance), letting Russian strike aircraft — chiefly Su-34s — hit targets tens of kilometres away without approaching Ukrainian air defences. That standoff range has continued to grow in recent years through improved kits. Ukrainian countermeasures cited include attempts to intercept Russian carrier aircraft before release using F-16s (armed with AIM-120 AMRAAM), ground-based GPS/EW jamming that can degrade UMPK guidance accuracy, and the anticipated arrival of Swedish JAS 39 Gripen jets carrying Meteor air-to-air missiles. Ukraine's Ministry of Defence describes countering Russian tactical aviation as "one of Ukraine's key challenges."

Ukraine unveils indigenous Shershen SAM with R-120K missile and Ai-Petri anti-glide-bomb EW system at MSPO 2026

2026-09-09

At the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Kielce, Poland, Ukrainian company Radionix presented its modular "Shershen" (Hornet) air-defence system, built around the Perun command-and-control vehicle, the Athena surveillance/tracking radar (detection range roughly 85 km) and four Borei launchers per battery. A more detailed report from The War Zone (TWZ, 10 September 2026) confirms and sharpens the picture: the unit on display was the second operational prototype and is described as "close to its production configuration." The design's core feature is a deliberately open architecture — the manufacturer states the system is "deliberately open... to use different missiles from different manufacturers" — letting the launcher fire both Soviet air-to-air missiles (R-27/AA-10, R-73/AA-11, R-60) and Western types (AIM-9 Sidewinder, AIM-132 ASRAAM), alongside the new, Ukrainian-developed R-120K. Manufacturer-claimed performance: engagement range up to 25 km, altitude up to 10,000 m, and up to six radar-guided missiles engaged simultaneously. TWZ situates Shershen within Ukraine's broader pattern of "FrankenSAM" improvisation — earlier examples include Buk launchers paired with Sea Sparrow missiles, Osa systems adapted to fire R-73s, and the UK-Danish containerized Gravehawk system — but argues Shershen is the first to systematize this approach, building calibre-agnostic launching in as a core design principle rather than an emergency workaround. TWZ is explicit about the caveats, however: advertised performance figures "have not been independently verified," the R-120K's motor is still under development so its "eventual performance and production status remain uncertain," and actual performance "will vary widely depending on the type of interceptor" fired. Separately, at the same exhibition, company Storkway showed the "Ai-Petri" electronic-warfare system (named after Crimea's highest peak), which jams both drone control links and the guidance of Russian glide bombs (KAB/UMPK). It operates across 300 MHz-6 GHz, scans a full 360 degrees, has a terrain-dependent range of 20-30 km, and draws 250 W in detection mode rising to about 2,000 W while jamming; the mobile pickup-mounted variant can be set up by a two-person crew in roughly 10 minutes. Unlike Shershen, Ai-Petri is not an exhibition concept: development began in 2014, tactical versions have been fielded since 2024, and the manufacturer claims around 400 systems delivered. As with Shershen, its claimed effectiveness against glide bombs remains independently unverified.

Russia hammers Kyiv and other Ukrainian cities with drones, missiles and glide bombs, 8-10 September 2026

2026-09-09

Russia continued its intensive bombardment of Ukrainian cities through a second consecutive night, extending the heavy combined salvo of 8-9 September into the night of 9-10 September. On 8-9 September, Russian forces struck with Iskander-M ballistic missiles, S8000 "Banderol" missiles and roughly 196 Shahed-type attack drones (including jet-powered variants), alongside Gerbera drones and Parodiya decoys; Ukraine's Air Force reported 165 drones shot down or suppressed, with impacts at 23 locations. On the night of 9-10 September, the barrage continued at a slightly smaller but still substantial scale: 149 Shahed (about half jet-powered), Gerbera and Parodiya decoy drones were launched from Millerovo, Kursk and Primorsko-Akhtarsk in Russia as well as occupied Donetsk Oblast and Hvardiiske in Crimea, with air defence reporting 128 downed or jammed by 08:00 and hits recorded at 13 locations plus debris at nine more. The pattern fits the sustained, round-the-clock pressure on Kyiv flagged since 27 August. In Kyiv on 8-9 September, a drone struck a 24-storey apartment block in Darnytskyi district overnight, injuring five, while a second, daytime strike hit a nine-storey building in Dniprovskyi district, killing a 70-year-old woman. Mykolaiv was hit by a combined missile-and-drone attack that killed an elderly woman and injured four, while Russian forces dropped five FAB-250 glide bombs with UMPK kits on a residential area of Kramatorsk. The following night and day (9-10 September) brought further damage: a large-scale drone attack on Kryvyi Rih hit five locations, damaging residential buildings, schools and businesses and leaving two people in surgery; Kyiv Oblast's Obukhiv, Bucha and Fastiv districts were struck on the morning of 10 September, killing one person and injuring two more. In Sumy Oblast, two women died of wounds from an 8 September drone strike on the Kyiv-Sumy commuter train, and a drone hit the Starokozache vehicle checkpoint on the Ukraine-Moldova border, killing two. The 9-10 September night added an international dimension: a Russian drone attack on the tugboat Tedy in the Black Sea killed two Azerbaijani crew members and injured two others, according to Azerbaijan's foreign ministry. Across both nights, the common thread remains deliberate, continuous pressure on urban civilian life: high-rise towers, commuter trains, border crossings and now Black Sea shipping are all being drawn into the target set. Casualty and volume figures are Ukrainian and Azerbaijani statements and vary slightly between outlets.
