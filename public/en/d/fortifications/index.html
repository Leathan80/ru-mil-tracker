Ukrainian FPV commander recalls drone kill on Russian UR-77 mine-clearer carrying over two tons of explosives

2026-10-07

"Skipper", an FPV company commander in Ukraine's 413th Raid Unmanned Systems Regiment, told Army TV that his proudest strike was destroying a Russian UR-77 mine-clearing vehicle with a cheap FPV drone during Ukraine's 2023 counteroffensive. According to his account, the vehicle detonated after a second hit, and the blast was so large he thought it was "a small nuclear blast". The strike itself is therefore not new; the account appears in an Euromaidan Press interview piece published on 8 October 2026. The regiment now operates on the Zaporizhzhia front. The UR-77 ("Zmiy Horynych") dates from the 1970s and is built on the chassis of the 2S1 Gvozdika self-propelled howitzer. It fires a rocket-propelled line charge that reportedly contains more than two tons of explosive, clearing a lane about six metres wide and setting off anti-personnel mines across a band of up to 15 metres. Citing Militarnyi, the article notes that although the system was designed to open routes for fast tank advances, Russia now more often uses it as an assault weapon against Ukrainian fortifications, using the line charge to flatten defensive positions. The account rests on the commander's own description and has not been independently verified; the article gives no location or exact date for the strike.

Sources: Euromaidan Press

678 окупантів і 120 одиниць техніки за місяць: як дрони й НРК масштабують дистанційне мінування (ArmyInform: 678 occupiers and 120 vehicles in a month: how drones and UGVs scale up remote mining)

2026-10-07SINGLE SOURCE

According to ArmyInform, Ukrainian Defence Forces killed 678 Russian soldiers and destroyed 120 units of weapons and military equipment on engineering obstacles (minefields) over the course of one month. The Command of the Support Forces of the Armed Forces of Ukraine released video of this work. A further 19 targets were reportedly hit on barriers laid by Ukrainian engineer units. Strikes are recorded in real time and confirmed results are entered into the DELTA system. The article says mine-laying has shifted from sappers working by hand to drones that deliver and emplace part of the mines, so routes can be blocked without sending personnel into areas under fire or under Russian drone observation. Ground robotic systems (UGVs, such as the 'Tarhan' shown in a photo) also lay barriers in front of Ukrainian positions or on routes Russian units use to move troops and vehicles. As an example it cites an earlier report on the 91st Separate Okhtyrka Support Brigade, whose engineers laid about 3,000 mines in one week, cutting Russian movement routes and creating a 'kill zone'. Single source: this is a report from ArmyInform, the news agency of the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, that has not been independently verified.

"Droneports": Ukrainska Pravda and OSINT group CyberBoroshno map Russia's purpose-built Shahed/Geran launch bases, such as Tsimbulova, and explain why hardened, dispersed layouts make them so hard to destroy

2026-10-01

In an investigation with the Ukrainian OSINT community CyberBoroshno, Ukrainska Pravda describes how Russia moved from launching Iranian-supplied Shaheds off existing military airfields (from autumn 2022, in Crimea and Bryansk and Kursk oblasts; known sites included Cape Chauda, Yeysk, Primorsko-Akhtarsk, Kursk, Seshcha and positions near Balaklava) to a network of purpose-built "droneports". Domestic Geran production meant drones had to be assembled, stored, inspected and prepared on site, so Russia added garages, underground stores large enough for drones and their warheads, and launchers placed in earth- or blast-walled revetments. The best-known site is Tsimbulova in Oryol Oblast, about 180 km from the border and covering some 5 sq km; according to OSINT analyst Harbuz (Dnipro Osint), Alabuga began building it in August 2024 and finished the main facilities by February 2025, as the first site designed entirely for storing, maintaining and launching drones. UP's satellite analysis counts 23 launchers, 387 garages and 26 underground stores there, with four more jet-drone launchers and 83 garages/shelters being prepared; in 2026 larger launchers for jet-powered Geran-4/5 drones were added. Another droneport sits on the ruins of Donetsk airport, about 50 km from the front. The design assumes attack: facilities are dispersed, separated by earthworks, and munitions and fuel are kept under concrete and earth so that a strike's blast stays contained and cannot trigger a chain reaction. Sites have their own power (solar panels, diesel generators), fuel depots, radio and navigation equipment for drone control, multiple access and exit roads, and air-defence and electronic-warfare cover; Shahed attack drones and Gerbera decoys are also stored in the open. Harbuz says Tsimbulova was built with large spare capacity (up to 3,000 combat drones) when Ukrainian intelligence put Shahed output at about 2,000 a month in late 2024, and that damaged structures can be rebuilt quickly and cheaply. According to the article, up to 95% of launches come from bases in Bryansk, Rostov, Smolensk and Oryol oblasts, Krasnodar Krai and occupied Crimea; UP has identified at least 63 launchers at the main droneports. Leaked Alabuga documents published by the Molfar Intelligence Institute reportedly show civilian Alabuga staff working at the sites complaining of "unjustified risk" after a December 2024 strike and being threatened to keep working. Ukrainian strikes have therefore aimed at disrupting launches rather than destroying the bases outright. The article cites the 2024 strikes near Balaklava (after which the area was no longer used) and Yeysk (followed by 48 days without Shahed launches), Storm Shadow strikes on the Tsimbulova area under construction in December 2024 and January 2025 (the Ukrainian General Staff claimed over 200 Shaheds destroyed), repeated drone and even FPV strikes on Donetsk airport by the 1st Separate Centre of the Unmanned Systems Forces, and a 21 September 2026 strike that penetrated a drone bunker at Tsimbulova. Ukraine has also hit Geran/Gerbera relay and ground control stations (Trubchevsk, Zaliznyi Port, Skadovsk, Chornomorsk, Olenivka). An officer of that centre, Falco, calls trained launch operators the scarcest and most valuable target; the authors suggest Ukrainian ballistic missiles could be needed against underground depots. Strike-effect claims come from Ukrainian sources and have not been independently verified.

Sources: Ukrainska Pravda (EN)

2026-09-30SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

The Russian milblogger channel Dva Mayora relays a statement by Kursk governor Alexander Khinshtein that a Russian Ministry of Defence employee responsible for accepting (signing off) defensive structures in Kursk Oblast has been detained. Khinshtein describes it as a continuing unravelling of a corruption tangle involving embezzlement of budget funds in the construction of fortifications in the Kursk border area. According to the governor's quoted statement, the detained MoD representative was responsible for acceptance of defensive structures in the Kursk region and in neighbouring frontline oblasts. Preliminary findings, he says, are that the man signed acceptance certificates 'without looking', i.e. without actually accepting any work. A criminal case has been opened against him under Part 3 of Article 285 of the Russian Criminal Code (abuse of official powers). Khinshtein says he is sure the story does not end here and that the Kursk regional government intends to keep cooperating actively with the justice authorities to expose everyone involved in what he calls a crime unprecedented in scale and cynicism. The claims come from a Russian regional official, relayed by a milblogger channel, and cannot be independently checked. Investigation: Russia has been rebuilding Facility 1335 in the Urals, the bunker linked to the 'Dead Hand' (Perimeter) nuclear command system

2026-09-26

A joint investigation by the Danish outlet Danwatch, The Sunday Times and the Dossier Center reports that Russia's Ministry of Defence has been carrying out a large modernisation of Object 1335 (codename 'Grot') since 2018. The facility is an underground complex inside Mount Kosvinsky Kamen near the village of Kytlym in the Sverdlovsk region of the Urals. According to the reporting it serves as a command post of the Strategic Missile Forces and is linked to Perimeter (Perimetr), the Soviet-era backup command system known in the West as 'Dead Hand'. Perimeter was built in the mid-1980s to make sure a retaliatory nuclear strike could still be launched if the leadership were killed or communications cut off. Militarnyi describes the complex as having at least 24 rooms, with the main and emergency exits about 2.5 km apart. The evidence consists of two parts. The first is Russian defence procurement records: the investigators searched about 2.5 million records from 2018 to 2024 and found thousands of tenders naming Object 1335. These cover more than 22,000 tonnes of reinforcing steel, about 56,500 tonnes of cement, 600 km of electrical cable, 182 ventilation units, 5 MW of refrigeration capacity (bought in 2019), 14,000 lamps, heavy machinery, rock-drilling equipment, blast-resistant equipment and filters against radioactive, chemical and biological contamination. The records also list Western-made components such as Rockwool insulation and Danfoss and Belimo fittings. The second is satellite imagery, which shows extensive earthworks at the mountain's entrances and work on about six new access points. A Danish construction professor, Per Goltermann, called the quantities an 'enormous' construction project. These records and images establish that there has been a large construction and hardening effort at Object 1335. The link to a revived and more automated Perimeter comes from interpretation and from Russian testimony. Retired Major General Viktor Khalin, who says he worked on developing Perimeter in 2005–2010, wrote in a Russian veterans' magazine in January 2026 that the system was reactivated in 2023 and can now work 'not only in an automated mode but also in a fully automatic mode'. Western experts such as Jeffrey Lewis and Andy Weber think reactivation is the most likely explanation. Tom Røseth describes the digging and reinforcement as a defensive measure to protect a retaliatory capability. Experts stress that claims about fully autonomous nuclear launch must be qualified, because Perimeter's operational details remain classified. The Kremlin did not respond to the investigators.

Sources: militarnyi · danwatch.dk

Operation Vivaldi: Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps takes Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka after suppressing Russian drones; ISW sees 'tactical drone overmatch' as mine-packed BTR-60s reach Russian fortifications, corps claims 250+ prisoners

2026-09-20

In the third 'season' of Operation Vivaldi, Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps says it liberated Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka in northern Donetsk Oblast near Lyman and regained control of Karpivka and Novomykhailivka, adding 51 sq km to the 176 sq km it says it has retaken since the offensive began. Corps commander Andrii Biletskyi calls Nove, a key Russian hub on the bridgehead ringed by commanding heights, the turning point; according to the corps, Russia expected the main thrust on the Shandryholove-Zelena Dolyna axis, burned its reserves counterattacking there and 'slept through' the real strike. Biletskyi claims about 2,000 Russian losses in this phase and more than 250 prisoners, and told Militarnyi that Russian units 'lost control and surrendered in groups', together with their officers, drone pilots and artillerymen. He lists the Russian formations engaged as the 20th Combined Arms Army (3rd and 144th Motorised Rifle Divisions) and the 25th Combined Arms Army (67th Motorised Rifle Division, 164th and 169th Motorised Rifle Brigades, 11th Tank Brigade). The 3rd Assault Brigade carried much of the fighting, with border guard detachments retaking Ridkodub. The strongest fortifications, notably at Nove, were broken with converted Soviet BTR-60 armoured carriers: Euromaidan Press reports they come from a batch of 110 donated by Bulgaria, and were filled with potentially dozens of TM-62 anti-tank mines, rigged for remote control, driven into Russian bunkers and detonated as unmanned vehicle-borne IEDs. The corps says they were used 'to demolish the enemy's strongest fortifications'. Euromaidan puts the cost of a conversion at perhaps a few thousand dollars, against tens or hundreds of thousands for a glide bomb, with no Ukrainian crew at risk. This adds to earlier robotic improvisations in Vivaldi: kamikaze UGVs air-dropped by heavy bomber drones more than 10 km behind the lines, and NC13 unit UGVs carrying two Bullspike grenade launchers that fire on positions and then detonate inside the shelters. What this says about the Russian defence: ISW assesses that Ukraine achieved 'tactical drone overmatch'. Old, slow armoured vehicles were driven up to heavy Russian fortifications without coming under Russian drone strikes, which ISW reads as Ukraine having neutralised Russia's drone defences in the sector, on a front where drones normally keep armour kilometres back. According to ISW this followed shaping operations and an intermediate-range strike campaign against the Russian drone and EW systems that would otherwise have hit the vehicles. Deputy corps commander Maksym Zhorin credits a 'Protective Drone' system of EW assets with neutralising around 20,000 Russian drones (no period given). ISW also notes that Russian EW runs on diesel generators, and the corps said on 17 September that strikes on logistics in occupied Luhansk Oblast had degraded fuel supply, straining Russia's use of drones and EW. Earlier strikes on mapped command posts and drone launch points (20th Army, 144th Division, Rubikon) and claimed partisan rail sabotage fit the same picture of a defence that leaned heavily on drones for surveillance and fires and gave way once they were blinded. All figures are claims by the 3rd Army Corps and Ukrainian media, a party to the conflict, partly relayed via ISW; none has been independently confirmed and the reporting does not describe Russian countermeasures.
