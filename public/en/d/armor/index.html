Russia's top tank maker takes on T-90 repairs as new builds strain

2026-10-08

According to Defence Blog, Uralvagonzavod (UVZ) in Nizhny Tagil, Russia's main tank manufacturer and the only plant building new T-90M tanks, is now carrying out major (capital) repairs of T-90Ms badly damaged in Ukraine. Photos circulating on social media this week show a train with heavily damaged T-90Ms on rail cars heading to the plant. Analysts note that such repairs were traditionally handled by the military's armored repair plants (BTRZ) and now compete with the factory's production of new tanks. Armored vehicle analyst Andrei Tarasenko attributes this to the breakdown of the BTRZ repair network, adding that a repair under factory conditions costs about as much as a new tank. Oryx lead analyst Jakub Janovsky warned that some organizations may count factory repairs and upgrades as "new production", blurring estimates of Russia's actual output. He added that recoverability depends on where a tank is hit: vehicles disabled in the contested gray zone are usually destroyed before they can be recovered, while those damaged further back can be retrieved with far less effort. Analysts link the shift to pressure on the tank industry from Western sanctions and labor shortages. Some T-90Ms leaving UVZ reportedly use refurbished hulls from older T-90As rather than newly built ones; in 2024 the company itself publicized restoring a T-90M in four weeks after it had been hit by 26 drones. UVZ still delivers vehicles: this week it reported another batch (number not given) of T-90Ms and upgraded T-72B3Ms with extra anti-drone protection, and some T-90Ms now carry the Arena-M active protection system, which Russian forces demonstrated against drones during Tsentr-2026. The interpretation of the photos rests on analysts' assessments and has not been independently confirmed.

No tanks, lone soldiers: Russia shifts to small-group infiltration tactics on the northeastern front

2026-09-20

Ukrainian Joint Forces Task Force spokesperson Viktor Trehubov said on 20 September that Russian assault tactics have shifted markedly over the past year on the northeastern front: rather than attacking in columns supported by tanks and armored vehicles, Russian troops now advance in small groups of a few soldiers, or even alone, trying to cross the "kill zone" separating rear positions from the front line undetected. Ukrainian military media separately reporting on the Kupyansk sector specifically describe a similar pattern — infantry infiltrating in small groups through remaining forest cover, spreading pressure across multiple directions rather than one concentrated mechanized thrust, with heavy equipment relegated to fire support, mine-clearing and logistics, and Ukrainian drone reconnaissance credited with detecting groups before they regroup. Oklahoma National Guard Trials Ukraine-Style Cage Armor on Convoy Vehicles

2026-09-15

During Exercise Thunderstruck 3.0, held September 12 at Camp Gruber Training Center, Oklahoma National Guard convoy vehicles were photographed and filmed fitted with welded wire-mesh cage screens against first-person-view (FPV) drones. This is the same basic principle behind the improvised 'cope cages' that Ukrainian forces began welding onto tanks and trucks as early as 2022, a practice that later spread to Russian forces: the cage is meant to detonate or deflect a drone's shaped-charge warhead before it reaches the vehicle's hull. It is thus a direct, visible instance of a Western unit adopting a defensive technique that originated on the Russia-Ukraine battlefield. Imagery shows a Humvee carrying a mesh screen mounted over the vehicle, and cargo trucks fully enclosed in boxy, welded cage frames. These vehicles moved as part of a tactical convoy that also included a marked ambulance, suggesting the Guard tested the added protection on logistics and medical-evacuation vehicles as well as combat platforms. Neither the Oklahoma National Guard's public release on the exercise nor its participating unit commanders specifically addressed the cage armor seen in the imagery, though the release did describe Thunderstruck as built around lessons from 'evolving threats,' where drones provide persistent surveillance, track troop movements, and influence how formations maneuver. Thunderstruck 3.0 folded friendly and simulated adversary drones into training alongside UH-60 Black Hawk operations, convoy movements, and medevac drills, under the Guard's Launched Effects Program, which has brought together military, industry, academic and government partners since 2022 to develop drone and counter-drone capabilities. Brig. Gen. Colby Wyatt described the goal as testing systems, tactics and procedures; retired Col. Shane Riley of RXR Worldwide spoke of using the drones for 'threat replication' to prepare units for what they might face in a future conflict. It remains unclear whether the cage screens reflect a formal equipment decision by the Oklahoma National Guard or a field-expedient addition specific to this exercise.

Почему танки перестали доминировать на поле боя? (Rybar video essay)

2026-09-05SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

Rybar published a short video essay (2:21) from its #ИсторияПодвига series, forwarded from the affiliated channel Тактикарь, asking why the tank no longer dominates the battlefield. Rybar first sketches the classic role: since the early twentieth century the tank was the "steel fist" of armies — it broke through defensive lines, supported infantry and served as an assault gun. Tanks entered the "SVO" in that role, with examples such as a T-80BV crew destroying Ukrainian armour and the "carousel" tactic used against Ukrainian infantry. Then, Rybar argues, everything changed because of "small, cheap products made from commercial components" — FPV drones — which he calls the most serious challenge to the tank since it first appeared on the battlefield. The factors cited are: mass-producible, very cheap FPV drones and anti-tank guided missiles that make any visible vehicle vulnerable; the loss of combined-arms cover (infantry, air defence, electronic warfare) so that tanks can no longer advance under protection; and a cost asymmetry in which a drone worth a few hundred dollars disables a vehicle worth millions. The consequence: both armies no longer use the tank for the breakthrough but as mobile artillery and standoff fire support — firing from cover, then withdrawing quickly. As countermeasures Rybar points to physical protection (cope cages, rubber and chain skirts, and more recently the heavier "hedgehog" cage armour), vehicle-mounted jammers against FPV control links, active protection systems, and above all adapted tactics that minimise exposure; he implicitly concedes that no single measure removes the drone threat.
