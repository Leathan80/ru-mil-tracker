Analyst: Ukraine has stabilised the front east of Sloviansk; Kramatorsk under heavier Russian fire

2026-11-13

Ukrainian forces have stabilised the front east of Sloviansk (Donetsk Oblast) after months of Russian offensives, according to analyst and mapper Clément Molin (12 November), as reported by Euromaidan Press. Molin attributes the change mainly to fresh reinforcements and to Brigadier General Andrii Biletskyi taking command of the city's defence; Biletskyi led the 3rd Army Corps through Operation Vivaldi north of Lyman and now heads Ukraine's new Center Group of Forces. Russian infiltration attempts continue, but there have been no recent reports of Russian troops entering Mykolaivka, the strongpoint on the road to Sloviansk that was the main concern in September. Ukrainian units have pushed through the forests between Mykolaivka and Lyman towards Yampil, moving drone teams and some artillery forward, which Russian sources say lets Ukraine hit groups infiltrating towards Mykolaivka. The picture is not one-sided: Ukrainian and Russian sources agree that Russian forces have taken a large part of the village of Rai-Oleksandrivka east of Sloviansk (DeepState reported an advance there on 9 November). Ukraine's General Staff reported only one Russian assault near Mykolaivka on 12 November and two near Yampil on 13 November, against 13 on the Kostiantynivka axis; ISW assessed limited Russian attacks east of Sloviansk on 12 November without advances. A Russian source cited by Molin said Russian units there suffer heavy losses and need constant replenishment. Kramatorsk, by contrast, is coming under increasing Russian fire from FPV drones, artillery and guided glide bombs, with strikes on the bridges residents need to cross rivers in the city. On 12 November Russia hit Kramatorsk ten times, four times with guided bombs, killing three people, according to Donetsk regional head Vadym Filashkin. Sloviansk and Kramatorsk anchor Ukraine's 'Fortress Belt'; Russia's negotiators demand the whole of Donetsk Oblast, of which Ukraine still holds about a fifth according to ISW. The assessment is that of a single analyst and has not been independently verified.

War on the Rocks analysis by ex-PM Honcharuk: Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces compete in a 'Time Domain' of adaptation speed, a model Russia has copied

2026-11-10

In an opinion-driven analysis for War on the Rocks, Oleksiy Honcharuk (Ukraine's prime minister in 2019-2020, former adviser to the first commander of the Unmanned Systems Forces, chair of the Aerorozvidka supervisory board and, since 2025, chairman of the defence-tech firm UFORCE) argues that the decisive dimension of the war is how fast each side adapts. He calls this the 'Time Domain': a contest over the interval between a new capability reaching the front and the enemy's working counter, which can now shrink to weeks. Ukraine created the Unmanned Systems Forces against opposition from career generals; he states that with about 2.5% of personnel the branch accounted for roughly a third of Russian losses in the past year. On Russia, Honcharuk credits it with being first to field jam-resistant fibre-optic drones at scale, around Kursk, through Rubicon, a defence-ministry centre that links developers, staff officers and operators in one loop; that lead lasted months, until Ukraine had more than 20 certified fibre-optic models from 11 producers by April 2025. He writes that Putin ordered a dedicated branch in December 2024 and that Russia's Unmanned Systems Forces were operational by late 2025, which he reads as Moscow copying a form that works. He presents Ukraine's 412th Brigade 'Nemesis' and Lasar's Group as 'Combat Hubs' that fuse combat, analysis, R&D and production on daily, six-weekly and six-monthly cycles; the Nemesis figures (over 520 targets destroyed for 107 airframes lost in seven months) come from the project's own records, not public data. He urges Western militaries to track adaptation time in planning, buy adaptation cycles rather than platforms, train against unpredictable opposition and create a small dedicated unmanned branch, and suggests a visible adaptation capacity could deter, while warning that a rapidly closing window can also provoke an aggressor. This is an argument piece by an author with a stake in Ukrainian and British-Ukrainian defence technology, not a neutral assessment; his loss figures and claims about Russia's force structure are not independently sourced in the article.

Verkhovna Rada approves in first reading a bill to create Space Forces as a new branch of Ukraine's armed forces

2026-11-09

On 9 November the Verkhovna Rada approved in first reading, with 252 votes, draft law No. 13255, which would create Space Forces as a new branch of the Armed Forces of Ukraine alongside the Ground Forces, Air Force and Navy, Defence Blog and Euromaidan Press report. The bill was registered on 2 May 2025 by 33 lawmakers, including Fedir Venislavskyi, Oleksandr Fediienko and Yurii Zdebskyi of Servant of the People and Roman Kostenko of Holos, according to Ukrainian News. The new branch would be funded from the existing security and defence budget. A first-reading vote only approves the general concept: amendments can still be made before the second reading, and the law needs the president's signature to take effect. According to Euromaidan Press, MP Mariana Bezuhla (elected for Servant of the People in 2019 and a frequent critic of the military leadership) blocked the final vote that day, as she said in a social-media post; the outlet does not explain the procedure she used. Bezuhla, who had herself signed the bill last year, dismissed it as a way "to create a couple more general-level positions" and also attacked the Committee on National Security, Defence and Intelligence. Venislavskyi called the move an act of personal revenge that "harms our country's national security", said he had filed a statement about possible criminal offences by Bezuhla, and said he would look for ways to get the bill adopted. It still needs a second reading. The bill calls for developing space technology for defence, including Ukraine's own early warning system to detect enemy missile launches and means to counter such attacks, as well as a national secure satellite communications network for the security and defence sector. According to Defence Blog, missile warning is the most pressing gap: Russia regularly fires ballistic missiles at Ukrainian cities and energy facilities, and Ukraine depends largely on allies, especially the United States, for the satellite data that gives early warning of launches. Venislavskyi says the force would let Ukraine warn of missile attacks independently and promptly and, where capabilities exist, strike deep behind enemy lines, but would mainly act as a deterrent; in April he linked it to countering Russia's Oreshnik missiles. Euromaidan Press lists planned focus areas including destroying Russian satellites, countering Russia's Rassvet system, launching Ukrainian satellites with partner support and integrating space intelligence into combat operations. Satellite communications are equally important at the front, where Ukrainian forces rely heavily on SpaceX's Starlink terminals to control drones, coordinate units and maintain command links; Kyiv wants to reduce its dependence on a single foreign commercial provider. Ukraine already buys imagery from commercial radar satellites and has worked on launch technology. Defence Blog notes the precedent of 2024, when parliament set up the Unmanned Systems Forces as a separate branch to scale up drone warfare.

Drapatyi's first 100 days as Ukraine's commander-in-chief: assault-regiment audit, mission command and new appointments

2026-11-05

Major General Mykhailo Drapatyi (43) marked 100 days as commander-in-chief of the Ukrainian Armed Forces in late October, the Kyiv Independent reports (Asami Terajima, Francis Farrell, Jimmy Rushton). He was appointed in July after President Zelensky dismissed Oleksandr Syrskyi amid mass protests in Kyiv. According to the outlet he inherited an understrength, overstretched army with little strategic reserve, while Russia's summer push in Donetsk Oblast had reached deep into Kostiantynivka and Ukraine's Patriot stocks had run out. Almost immediately he ordered an audit of personnel use and paused replenishment of the largest Assault Forces regiments, which had a reputation for high losses and misuse of soldiers. New appointees include Serhii Sobko as deputy commander-in-chief, Sviatoslav Zaiets as Ground Forces commander and ex-Unmanned Systems Forces commander Col. Vadym Sukharevskyi as head of the Marines. The central change is command style. In a September manifesto Drapatyi wrote that he will command 'through intent' and not run every battle. FPRI analyst Rob Lee, citing brigade commanders, says corps commanders now have autonomy over holding positions and counterattacks, where previously they could not even authorise withdrawal from positions of questionable value. An anonymous deputy brigade commander says units were told that orders deemed impossible or too costly would no longer come with the threat of dismissal; former Azov chief of staff Lt. Col. Bohdan Krotevych and Kraken regiment commander Oleh Romanov are also positive. Not everyone sees change: an anonymous senior corps officer in Kharkiv Oblast says 'not much has changed' there. NATO is drafting a defence plan that foresees conventional strikes deep inside Russia on bases, depots and industry, the WSJ reports

2026-11-04

NATO is revising how it would counter a Russian attack and is developing a defence strategy that drops earlier restrictions in favour of conventional strikes far inside Russian territory, according to The Wall Street Journal as reported by Ukrainska Pravda. After nearly five years of war in Ukraine, and with lessons from the Middle East, Alliance strategists have reportedly concluded that defending the eastern flank with barriers and checkpoints alone cannot stop an invasion. Deterrence now requires "denial": not only hitting troops crossing the border but also military bases, supply depots and industrial facilities behind the front line, on Russian soil. The WSJ notes the plan does not envisage a transition to nuclear weapons. Supreme Allied Commander Europe Gen. Alexus Grynkewich has set out this approach in his own "theory of victory": the Allies first block the initial wave of an attack and then take away the enemy's ability to keep pushing, by hitting "both advancing forces and anything and everything that supports, sustains or controls that force". Polish Maj. Gen. Ireneusz Nowak said NATO would want to strike Russia's defence-industrial base in a war and should treat deep precision strike as deterrence, adding that the message to Moscow must be credible and therefore backed by actual capabilities. Ukraine's ambassador to NATO, Alyona Getmanchuk, likened air defence to a painkiller that eases symptoms while the source of the pain must be targeted. To support the approach, the US is modernising and replenishing its Tomahawk cruise missile and ATACMS ballistic missile stocks, while European partners aim to expand Storm Shadow/SCALP and Taurus inventories and develop new low-cost long-range strike systems. Fabian Hoffmann of the Norwegian Institute for Defence Studies said at least 48 low-cost cruise missile projects have been announced in Europe. The plan itself has not been published; the details rest on the WSJ's reporting.
