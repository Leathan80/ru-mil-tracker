Operation Vivaldi: Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps takes Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka after suppressing Russian drones; ISW sees 'tactical drone overmatch' as mine-packed BTR-60s reach Russian fortifications, corps claims 250+ prisoners

2026-09-20

In the third 'season' of Operation Vivaldi, Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps says it liberated Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka in northern Donetsk Oblast near Lyman and regained control of Karpivka and Novomykhailivka, adding 51 sq km to the 176 sq km it says it has retaken since the offensive began. Corps commander Andrii Biletskyi calls Nove, a key Russian hub on the bridgehead ringed by commanding heights, the turning point; according to the corps, Russia expected the main thrust on the Shandryholove-Zelena Dolyna axis, burned its reserves counterattacking there and 'slept through' the real strike. Biletskyi claims about 2,000 Russian losses in this phase and more than 250 prisoners, and told Militarnyi that Russian units 'lost control and surrendered in groups', together with their officers, drone pilots and artillerymen. He lists the Russian formations engaged as the 20th Combined Arms Army (3rd and 144th Motorised Rifle Divisions) and the 25th Combined Arms Army (67th Motorised Rifle Division, 164th and 169th Motorised Rifle Brigades, 11th Tank Brigade). The 3rd Assault Brigade carried much of the fighting, with border guard detachments retaking Ridkodub. The strongest fortifications, notably at Nove, were broken with converted Soviet BTR-60 armoured carriers: Euromaidan Press reports they come from a batch of 110 donated by Bulgaria, and were filled with potentially dozens of TM-62 anti-tank mines, rigged for remote control, driven into Russian bunkers and detonated as unmanned vehicle-borne IEDs. The corps says they were used 'to demolish the enemy's strongest fortifications'. Euromaidan puts the cost of a conversion at perhaps a few thousand dollars, against tens or hundreds of thousands for a glide bomb, with no Ukrainian crew at risk. This adds to earlier robotic improvisations in Vivaldi: kamikaze UGVs air-dropped by heavy bomber drones more than 10 km behind the lines, and NC13 unit UGVs carrying two Bullspike grenade launchers that fire on positions and then detonate inside the shelters. What this says about the Russian defence: ISW assesses that Ukraine achieved 'tactical drone overmatch'. Old, slow armoured vehicles were driven up to heavy Russian fortifications without coming under Russian drone strikes, which ISW reads as Ukraine having neutralised Russia's drone defences in the sector, on a front where drones normally keep armour kilometres back. According to ISW this followed shaping operations and an intermediate-range strike campaign against the Russian drone and EW systems that would otherwise have hit the vehicles. Deputy corps commander Maksym Zhorin credits a 'Protective Drone' system of EW assets with neutralising around 20,000 Russian drones (no period given). ISW also notes that Russian EW runs on diesel generators, and the corps said on 17 September that strikes on logistics in occupied Luhansk Oblast had degraded fuel supply, straining Russia's use of drones and EW. Earlier strikes on mapped command posts and drone launch points (20th Army, 144th Division, Rubikon) and claimed partisan rail sabotage fit the same picture of a defence that leaned heavily on drones for surveillance and fires and gave way once they were blinded. All figures are claims by the 3rd Army Corps and Ukrainian media, a party to the conflict, partly relayed via ISW; none has been independently confirmed and the reporting does not describe Russian countermeasures.

Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps Claims It Crippled Russia's Elite Rubicon Drone Unit

2026-09-18

Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps claims to have significantly degraded Russia's elite Rubicon drone unit as part of Operation Vivaldi, the corps' counteroffensive north and east of Lyman in northern Donetsk Oblast. According to the corps, Ukrainian forces destroyed Rubicon's command infrastructure, logistics networks, ammunition storage and drone launch facilities in the area, and disabled the unit's command-and-control system while eliminating experienced operators. The corps attributes the results to sustained pressure combined with electronic warfare, stating that Rubicon lost personnel and equipment and could no longer deploy because of Ukrainian EW and air defence systems, forcing it to abandon positions. Ukraine says the Russian command subsequently relocated Rubicon to preserve its remaining assets. Rubicon (the Center for Advanced Unmanned Technologies) is one of Russia's most prominent and well-resourced drone formations, employing attack, FPV and reconnaissance UAVs, and has been a recurring target of Ukrainian strikes throughout 2026. This is a claim made by the Ukrainian military itself, published via Ukrainska Pravda; at the time of writing it has not been independently corroborated by other outlets or analysts such as the Kyiv Independent, Militarnyi or ISW. Given Rubicon's prominence and the unit's demonstrated resilience after previous Ukrainian strikes in 2026, the claim should be treated as unconfirmed until further reporting or open-source verification emerges.

Ukrainian radio relay survives 10 Russian strikes, keeps transmitting after building collapse

2026-09-16

HIMERA B1 is a Ukrainian-made autonomous radio repeater that extends the range of battlefield mesh radio networks. It uses AES-256 encryption and pseudo-random frequency hopping to resist interception, direction-finding and jamming. The manufacturer, Himera, was founded by a volunteer from Kyiv Oblast's territorial defense who identified critical battlefield communication gaps early in the full-scale invasion. A unit of this type was operated by Ukraine's 1st Border Guard Detachment from a telecom tower in Kharkiv Oblast, working alongside a sister relay 10.5 kilometers away, each covering roughly 5 kilometers of communications range. Russian forces struck this specific position ten times using FPV drones and guided (glide) bombs — a pattern suggesting deliberate targeting of Ukrainian communication nodes rather than incidental damage. In the final strike, the building partially collapsed and the tower came down, burying the repeater under rubble and destroying its power system. Despite the damage, the device reportedly kept transmitting and stayed connected even while buried, which is why border guards were able to recover it afterward. The episode highlights two things at once: Russia's sustained effort to disrupt Ukrainian battlefield communications through repeated precision strikes on relay infrastructure, and the emphasis Ukrainian manufacturers place on ruggedness and redundancy in this class of equipment as a countermeasure to that campaign.

German investigation files name GRU officer and Belarusian criminal behind Leipzig bomb-drone attack

2026-09-12

On 4 August 2026, Leipzig/Halle Airport was targeted in an attack involving three explosive-laden drones aimed at a Ukrainian Antonov An-124 cargo aircraft carrying roughly 25,000 litres of kerosene. Two drones were destroyed at launch or failed; the third landed right next to the aircraft carrying about 1.8 kilograms of Semtex hidden inside a vegetable tin, but the detonator became detached, so it never went off. Roughly ten days later, investigators found a third drone and about 50 grams of Semtex near Kabelsketal, Saxony-Anhalt, pointing to a pre-planned operation with multiple launch points and a pre-positioned equipment cache. According to investigation files reported by Welt am Sonntag, German security services have identified two main suspects: Russian national Oleg Le., attributed to the environment of Russia's GRU military intelligence service and allegedly responsible for preparation and logistics, and Belarusian national Andrei Ka., a criminal with prior convictions in Finland and Lithuania, allegedly responsible for the operational role. Key evidence is DNA recovered from glove remnants found on the drone's antenna control equipment, reportedly matching DNA records already on file in Finland and Lithuania. Le. is said to have entered Germany via Turkey and left via Berlin-Brandenburg airport for Serbia and then Russia; German authorities were reportedly already aware of a possible GRU link before his entry, yet he was not placed under permanent surveillance. Polish prosecutors separately link Ka. to a 2024 arson at a hardware store in Łódź. The case fits a broader pattern of suspected Russian sabotage and drone incidents against European infrastructure in 2025-2026, and is politically sensitive because Leipzig/Halle is a hub in NATO's airlift mission supplying weapons to Ukraine. On 1 September 2026, German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul and Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt publicly held Russia responsible, after which Germany closed the Russian consulate-general in Bonn and the Russian House in Berlin; Russia responded by closing Germany's consulate in St. Petersburg and shutting Goethe Institutes, while continuing to reject the accusations. An important caveat: the specific names and the GRU attribution stem from leaked investigation files and journalistic reporting, not from an official German indictment or arrest — both suspects reportedly already left Germany and have not been detained. The facts have not yet been independently confirmed in a legal proceeding.

FSB reportedly preparing leadership shake-up and major overhaul, possibly as early as October

2026-09-12

Independent Russian investigative outlet iStories reported on September 12, 2026 that the FSB — Russia's domestic security service — is bracing for a large-scale reorganization that sources say could begin as early as October 2026. The overhaul is said to affect both key internal services and the top leadership, including director Alexander Bortnikov (74), who has headed the agency since 2008. The report was triggered by the departure of Sergei Alpatov as head of the FSB's Economic Security Service, officially for health reasons; no successor has been named yet, partly because officials are said to be waiting for the wider reshuffle. Meduza's English edition picked up the story on September 13-14, and The Moscow Times reported on it on September 12 as well. According to the two anonymous former FSB officers cited by iStories, Bortnikov has long been asking to retire and reportedly spends considerable time at sanatoriums, with First Deputy Director Sergei Korolev increasingly handling his duties. Three names are floated as potential successors: Korolev himself, Ivan Tkachev (head of the FSB's Military Counterintelligence Department, described as a protégé of Rosneft chief Igor Sechin), and Vladislav Menshchikov (head of the Counterintelligence Service). The Daily Beast adds that conflicting accounts exist about Alpatov's exit: alongside the health-reasons explanation, some sources suggest he was pushed out for investigating people connected to businessman Arkady Rotenberg. The FSB combines counterintelligence, domestic security, border control, administration in occupied Ukrainian territories, and a growing role in internet censorship and economic-security cases, making it Russia's most powerful security service. A leadership change at the top combined with a broader restructuring in the middle of the war against Ukraine would say something about internal Kremlin power dynamics. Crucially, the entire report rests on anonymous, unverified sourcing; neither the Kremlin nor the FSB has officially confirmed anything, and this should for now be treated as an unverified Kremlin rumor.

Ukraine hits UAV ground control stations and other military targets in Russia and temporarily occupied territories

2026-09-10

Ukraine's General Staff reports that on 9 September and during the night of 9–10 September, eight targets in Russia and temporarily occupied territory were struck, aimed at the launch and control infrastructure of Russian attack drones. In temporarily occupied Donetsk, a facility for storage, preparation and launch of Geran and Gerbera attack UAVs was hit. In addition, several UAV command posts were struck in the temporarily occupied Zaporizhzhia and Donetsk regions. The strikes fit a recognizable, ongoing pattern: Ukraine consistently targets ground control stations (GCS) and relay stations ahead of Russian strike waves. The General Staff itself frames this with cumulative August figures (17 Russian air defense systems, six bridges, more than 20 storage sites and over 30 command posts destroyed), indicating this is part of a systematic campaign against Russian drone and C2 infrastructure in depth.

Ukraine's commander-in-chief scraps 30-plus obsolete General Staff orders to cut field-command bureaucracy

2026-09-09

Ukraine's General Staff has rescinded more than thirty obsolete standing orders, along with dozens of interim amendment documents that had cluttered the regulatory framework. According to the 9 September 2026 announcement by Colonel Vitalii Sarantsev, communications chief of the 14th Army Corps, on Facebook, the cancelled material comprises old training programs, outdated firing courses, and physical-training concepts roughly ten years old. Much of it dated to the Anti-Terrorist Operation and Joint Forces Operation period of 2016-2020 — the years of trench warfare in Donbas before Russia's full-scale 2022 invasion. The move is attributed to Commander-in-Chief Mykhailo Drapatyi, who replaced Oleksandr Syrskyi in that post in July 2026. The stated rationale is reducing the bureaucratic burden on field commanders. Sarantsev described the scrapped instructions as "long-obsolete instructions that had existed only on paper for years, requiring formal reports, 'check boxes' and 'tables.'" Field commanders are no longer required to file reports or conduct activities against standards disconnected from the combat practice developed since 2022. A key practical effect is resolving legal conflicts between wartime-developed training standards and the outdated requirements inherited from the ATO/JFO era. Sarantsev called it "a good, big 'fall cleanup.'" The step fits a wider command-culture reform under Drapatyi, who is unwinding Soviet-era structures mid-war: on 2 September he published leadership principles placing the service member at the center, and he has previously pushed NCO reform toward Western models. Skeptical framing: the reporting rests entirely on a single Facebook post by a corps spokesman, with no independent verification, no specific order numbers cited, and no statement from Drapatyi or the General Staff itself. The sources draw no explicit link to mission command or drone-era operational tempo; for now this is administrative housekeeping of training and reporting regulations whose operational significance will only be visible in implementation. Its value as a signal of reform direction currently exceeds its demonstrable direct impact.

Defense Express: Germany only now seeks anti-satellite / GNSS-jamming capability, years into Russia's Kaliningrad spoofing

2026-09-08SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express reports that the Bundeswehr has tasked the German Space Agency with conducting market research for electronic warfare systems able to suppress satellite navigation and communications signals. The stated requirements cover jamming satellites in low, medium and geostationary orbits, coverage for a brigade-sized area of operations, and, as one countermeasure variant against satellite navigation, spoofing coordinates rather than fully suppressing the signal. The reference unit is Panzerbrigade 45, the brigade being permanently stationed in Lithuania; the timeline foresees initial operational capability around 2029 and Bundeswehr-wide fielding by 2035. Notably, the requirement calls for an "ITAR-free" approach, meaning independence from U.S. components. As background, Defense Express points to Russia's Tobol complex in Kaliningrad Oblast, which the article says has been attempting to suppress GPS across Europe for years and genuinely creates problems. The requirements do not name which satellite constellations are the intended targets, but Defense Express infers Russian military constellations, GPS and GLONASS, and civilian systems Russia might exploit such as Starlink, plus Russia's Rassvet satellite program. The implied logic is that Berlin is drawing on Ukraine's experience using Starlink for long-range strike assets and on Russia's efforts to jam it. The thrust of the commentary is critical: Defense Express expresses surprise that the German military is only now raising the question of effective satellite-navigation suppression, given that Russian GPS interference from Kaliningrad has persisted for years and affects all of Europe. This particular article contains no specific references to Baltic civil-aviation disruption incidents or airport closures. This is a single-source item: Defense Express is a Ukrainian outlet with a pronounced editorial slant, and its framing that Germany's response comes too late reflects that perspective, so treat the specifics as provisional pending corroboration from additional reporting.

Rybar ('A strike at the problem'): Russia uses Geran drones to hit Ukrainian long-range-drone launch crews before takeoff

2026-09-08SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

Milblogger channels such as Rybar are personal, editorial Telegram channels and do not represent a Russian Ministry of Defence position; milbloggers routinely contradict each other and the ministry, and none of this is independently confirmed. In this post ("Удар по проблеме" / "A strike at the problem"), Rybar argues that the "Geran" (Герань) strike drones stopped being mere kamikaze drones long ago and have become a universal weapon for strikes on infrastructure and mobile targets, with a use range the channel says widens every month. The main thrust is that Gerans are now also used against Ukrainian launch sites for long-range strike drones. Since ground troops overrunning launch positions is barely achievable, the launch crews can be hit with the same drones. Rybar contends that a hit before launch solves two problems at once: it removes the interception problem and avoids "falling debris" over friendly territory. As an example the channel cites a recent Geran employment near Alekseyevka (Belgorod region). Such strikes require careful preparation — the enemy can reposition or camouflage targets — but on a successful hit not only equipment but the crew is destroyed, and a trained crew is a heavier loss than the replaceable airframe. Rybar concedes that striking such "migrating groups" depends on reconnaissance capacity, which it says is often lacking. Scaling up will not work without improving the ISR portion of the "strike loop," including reliable UAV control channels; the development of Bureau 1440's "Rassvet" satellite constellation would help. The "problem" of the title is thus the Ukrainian long-range drone threat to Russian territory; the piece is about Russian targeting doctrine (pre-launch destruction, prioritising crews over airframes), not about effects on Ukrainian air defence.

Russia closes German consulate in St. Petersburg and Goethe-Institut branches in tit-for-tat over Leipzig drone attack

2026-09-07

Russia's Foreign Ministry announced on 7 September 2026 that Germany's consulate general in St. Petersburg must cease operations and that all its staff must leave the country by 18 September. Moscow simultaneously reaffirmed that every Goethe-Institut branch in Russia, including those in Moscow and St. Petersburg, must close by 13 September. Russia framed the move explicitly as retaliation, with the ministry stating that 'it was the German authorities who once again provoked a new round of escalation in bilateral relations.' The measure is a direct tit-for-tat response to Germany's decision earlier that week to shut the Russian consulate in Bonn and the Russian House cultural center in Berlin. Berlin took those steps after formally blaming Russia for the 4 August drone incident at Leipzig/Halle Airport, where an explosives-laden drone was found near a Ukrainian aircraft and a second drone reportedly struck a cargo plane. Leipzig/Halle is a major freight hub supporting logistics to Ukraine. Moscow denies any involvement and casts the incident as 'a provocation.' The consulate dispute broadens an existing sequence: Germany's 26 August formal accusation and consulate closure, its 3 September move to share targeting intelligence with Ukraine, and the 6 September announcement of a German counter-drone 'protective shield,' which prompted Lavrov to say the accusations 'incite a real war.' This latest step adds no military capability but marks a further downgrading of diplomatic relations and fits a broader pattern of Russian reprisals channelled through consular and cultural institutions. No specific personnel figures were disclosed.

Russian milbloggers push for networking mobile fire groups (MOG) as frontline counter-drone air defence

2026-08-30SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

MILBLOGGER — this rests on two personal/editorial pro-Russian Telegram channels (Rybar and WarGonzo), not an official Russian MoD position. Milbloggers routinely contradict each other and the ministry, inflate claimed results, and pursue their own agendas (Rybar openly promotes allied channels and a named manufacturer); the figures below are not independently verified. Rybar (30 Aug) argues that a significant share of the drones flying over Russian territory is being shot down by mobile fire groups (MOG — mobilnaya ognevaya gruppa): small crews with heavy machine guns or light anti-aircraft weapons on vehicles. Given the shortage of surface-to-air missiles, Rybar says these groups are often the only available air-defence asset; with good positioning, correct lead technique and a suitable gun mount, it claims a single crew can down "dozens of drones in one night." Rybar then makes the case for networking them: adding target cueing and coordination between MOGs and with regular air defence raises effectiveness, because a crew that knows the direction, timing and number of drones crossing its sector engages far more easily. The channel says the armed forces are fielding an "integrated information-interaction system" meant to link all assets — and eventually even civilian shooters — into one digital network, and cites technical solutions such as the "PPSh Laboratory" project with an automated turret, radar and its own interceptor. WarGonzo (30 Aug) trails a video report (made for Channel One) on the mobile fire group of the 49th Guards Anti-Aircraft Missile Brigade of the "Zapad" grouping and names specific kit: the "Utyos" heavy machine gun (NSV 12.7 mm), a "Zusya" (ZU-23-2 23 mm twin AA autocannon) and interceptor FPV drones. As a data point, the whole corroborates the Russian counter-UAS TTP of vehicle-mounted mobile fire groups using machine guns / ZU-23-2 plus emerging interceptor drones, and an acknowledged weakness — poor mutual coordination and cueing that Russia wants to fix with a C2 network.

According to Ukrainian defense outlet Defense Express, the United Kingdom is withdrawing its Challenger 2 tanks from Estonia and replacing the existing armored battlegroup with a new "Mobile Anti-Armour Force" (MAAF). The British contingent in the country would reportedly grow from roughly 800 to 1,200 personnel. The new force emphasizes mobility, survivability, and drone integration, and would be equipped with high-mobility gear, modern weapons, and advanced unmanned systems tailored to Estonia's operational environment. The restructuring is directly tied by the source to Ukrainian combat experience and to the NATO Hedgehog 2025 exercise held in May, which involved about 16,000 troops from twelve NATO countries plus Ukraine. During that exercise, Ukrainian drone operators — reportedly around ten personnel from the 412th Separate Unmanned Systems Brigade "Nemesis" — allegedly managed to "destroy" two notional adversary battalions in roughly half a day, mock-destroying 17 armored vehicles and conducting about 30 strikes on other targets. Defense Express frames this within a broader shift toward a more drone-centric British doctrine, describing an eventual unmanned force structure that would be roughly 80% drone-based across land, naval, and air domains. There is no official confirmation from the UK Ministry of Defence of this restructuring, the personnel figures, or the stated rationale.
