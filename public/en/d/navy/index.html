France conducts first submarine launch of the M51.3 ballistic missile from SSBN Le Vigilant

2026-10-06

According to the French Ministry of the Armed Forces, the nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarine Le Vigilant fired an M51.3 missile without a nuclear charge off Finistère on Tuesday 6 October 2026. It was the first launch of the M51.3 variant from a submarine; the earlier qualification flight in November 2023 was made from the DGA's land-based test site at Biscarrosse. The missile came down in the North Atlantic, several hundred kilometres from any coast, and was tracked throughout its flight by the DGA's test assets, including the measurement ship Monge. According to reports, the firing also carried experiments supporting the next version, the M51.4, whose development began in September 2025. The M51.3 entered operational service on 24 October 2025 and is gradually replacing the M51.1 and M51.2 on France's four Triomphant-class submarines, each of which carries 16 missiles. The ministry says the M51.3 offers greater range, precision and penetration capability and carries the new TNO-2 oceanic nuclear warheads. Its official range is classified. The test underlines France's strategic deterrent at a time when Russia's nuclear signalling towards Europe remains a central concern for NATO.

Danish intelligence (DDIS) sees 'low but growing risk' of a limited Russian military attack on a NATO border state in the coming months, alongside escalating hybrid warfare

2026-09-24

Denmark's Defence Intelligence Service (DDIS/FE) published an unclassified update, 'Assessment of the Threat from Russia', on 24 September 2026, supplementing its February 2025 assessment. It states that Russia will further escalate its hybrid war against NATO and the West in the coming months, citing intensified sabotage, destructive cyberattacks and drone attacks on European targets — including a foiled drone plot against Leipzig/Halle Airport that German authorities say Russia planned over several months — and Russian drones and in some cases missiles hitting NATO countries bordering Ukraine. It names the 14 September incident in which a Russian frigate off Gedser fired flares at a Danish military helicopter as the most serious recent example of aggressive Russian conduct towards NATO units in the Baltic; Denmark's security service PET has separately warned of sabotage planning against Danish defence companies. DDIS assesses a 'low but growing risk' that Russia will carry out a limited military attack on one or more NATO countries bordering Russia in the coming months — even while the Ukraine war continues and without having built the capability for a regional war — as a potential 'desperate move' to divert European resources from Ukraine and divide NATO. Possible forms it names: isolated long-range strikes on infrastructure supporting Ukraine (with Moscow possibly claiming the weapons strayed because Ukraine jammed them), a false-flag operation using Ukrainian-made drones, or a limited, possibly unmarked troop contingent — the latter requiring a months-long, hard-to-hide build-up. An outright invasion is judged 'highly unlikely' but 'cannot be ruled out' and would need at least six months of preparation. The February 2025 timelines remain valid according to DDIS: about six months for Russia to be able to fight a local war against a neighbour, about two years to pose a credible regional threat to the Baltic Sea NATO states, and about five years to be ready for a large-scale war in Europe — assuming the Ukraine war ends or freezes, Russian forces grow faster than the West's and Moscow judges the US would not back European allies. DDIS says Russia sees a 'window of opportunity' before European military capability grows towards 2030, and that its behaviour hinges on whether it believes it can stay below the threshold for a collective NATO response — a threshold Russia itself is likely unsure about.

An opinion piece on the Russian military portal Topwar (by Andrey Kolobov) argues that unmanned systems — both aerial UAVs and uncrewed surface vessels (USVs) — will not make the crewed surface fleet obsolete. English gloss of the title: 'On the ability of UAVs to "cancel" surface ships.' The author concedes that Ukrainian USVs scored notable successes against Russia's Black Sea Fleet and that the cost asymmetry is real, but concludes that drones 'have not become, and will not become, the ultimate weapon of naval warfare.' He rests the case on two core limitations. First, speed: cheap strike drones of the Shahed-136 class cruise at only 150-180 km/h and take nearly two hours to cover 350 km, versus minutes for missiles, so a large drone salvo gives the enemy long warning. Citing Soviet Cold War experience tracking US carrier strike groups, he argues the hard problem is, and remains, finding a moving squadron at all. He frames the change in naval warfare as evolutionary rather than revolutionary.
