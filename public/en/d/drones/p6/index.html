Operation Vivaldi: Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps takes Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka after suppressing Russian drones; ISW sees 'tactical drone overmatch' as mine-packed BTR-60s reach Russian fortifications, corps claims 250+ prisoners

2026-09-20

In the third 'season' of Operation Vivaldi, Ukraine's 3rd Army Corps says it liberated Nove, Ridkodub and Katerynivka in northern Donetsk Oblast near Lyman and regained control of Karpivka and Novomykhailivka, adding 51 sq km to the 176 sq km it says it has retaken since the offensive began. Corps commander Andrii Biletskyi calls Nove, a key Russian hub on the bridgehead ringed by commanding heights, the turning point; according to the corps, Russia expected the main thrust on the Shandryholove-Zelena Dolyna axis, burned its reserves counterattacking there and 'slept through' the real strike. Biletskyi claims about 2,000 Russian losses in this phase and more than 250 prisoners, and told Militarnyi that Russian units 'lost control and surrendered in groups', together with their officers, drone pilots and artillerymen. He lists the Russian formations engaged as the 20th Combined Arms Army (3rd and 144th Motorised Rifle Divisions) and the 25th Combined Arms Army (67th Motorised Rifle Division, 164th and 169th Motorised Rifle Brigades, 11th Tank Brigade). The 3rd Assault Brigade carried much of the fighting, with border guard detachments retaking Ridkodub. The strongest fortifications, notably at Nove, were broken with converted Soviet BTR-60 armoured carriers: Euromaidan Press reports they come from a batch of 110 donated by Bulgaria, and were filled with potentially dozens of TM-62 anti-tank mines, rigged for remote control, driven into Russian bunkers and detonated as unmanned vehicle-borne IEDs. The corps says they were used 'to demolish the enemy's strongest fortifications'. Euromaidan puts the cost of a conversion at perhaps a few thousand dollars, against tens or hundreds of thousands for a glide bomb, with no Ukrainian crew at risk. This adds to earlier robotic improvisations in Vivaldi: kamikaze UGVs air-dropped by heavy bomber drones more than 10 km behind the lines, and NC13 unit UGVs carrying two Bullspike grenade launchers that fire on positions and then detonate inside the shelters. What this says about the Russian defence: ISW assesses that Ukraine achieved 'tactical drone overmatch'. Old, slow armoured vehicles were driven up to heavy Russian fortifications without coming under Russian drone strikes, which ISW reads as Ukraine having neutralised Russia's drone defences in the sector, on a front where drones normally keep armour kilometres back. According to ISW this followed shaping operations and an intermediate-range strike campaign against the Russian drone and EW systems that would otherwise have hit the vehicles. Deputy corps commander Maksym Zhorin credits a 'Protective Drone' system of EW assets with neutralising around 20,000 Russian drones (no period given). ISW also notes that Russian EW runs on diesel generators, and the corps said on 17 September that strikes on logistics in occupied Luhansk Oblast had degraded fuel supply, straining Russia's use of drones and EW. Earlier strikes on mapped command posts and drone launch points (20th Army, 144th Division, Rubikon) and claimed partisan rail sabotage fit the same picture of a defence that leaned heavily on drones for surveillance and fires and gave way once they were blinded. All figures are claims by the 3rd Army Corps and Ukrainian media, a party to the conflict, partly relayed via ISW; none has been independently confirmed and the reporting does not describe Russian countermeasures.

2026-09-19

The U.S. Army has awarded a $10.6 million contract to Skyeton Inc., the American subsidiary of Ukrainian drone maker Skyeton, for the Raybird — a small, fixed-wing surveillance aircraft built for long-endurance missions rather than speed or firepower. It can stay airborne for close to 28 hours, fly more than 2,500 km without landing, operate at altitudes up to 5,500 meters, and carry a 5-10 kg sensor payload. The airframe is rated from -35°C to 55°C. Army Contracting Command at Redstone Arsenal, Alabama, issued the firm-fixed-price contract after an online solicitation drew only one bid, from Skyeton itself. Delivery location and exact funding will be set per individual order, with an estimated contract completion date of December 15, 2026. The Army has not disclosed how many systems will be bought, which unit will operate them, or where they will ultimately be delivered. Skyeton, founded in Ukraine in 2006 and originally a light sport aircraft builder, has logged more than 350,000 combat flight hours with Raybird since Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion, mostly on long-range reconnaissance missions. It is also expanding across NATO: a joint venture with UK firm Prevail Partners to build Raybird for NATO markets and compete to replace the British Army's aging Watchkeeper drone, a partnership with French sensor maker Harmattan AI, sales in Poland, and hydrogen fuel-cell variant testing in Ukraine since December 2025. This is reportedly among the first confirmed contracts putting Raybird directly into U.S. Army hands. Note: unit quantity is not disclosed, and the $10.6M figure is a contract ceiling, not a fixed total purchase price.

Ukraine Receives Australian Slinger 30mm Anti-Drone Systems

2026-09-17

Ukraine's armed forces have received Slinger anti-drone modules from the Australian company Electro Optic Systems (EOS), based in Canberra. The system is a remotely operated weapon station built around the 30 mm Bushmaster M230LF cannon, purpose-built to detect and destroy low-flying drones ranging from FPV kamikaze drones to reconnaissance UAVs. Slinger pairs an Echodyne 4D radar with active electronic beamsteering to a 4-axis optoelectronic sighting unit that tracks independently of the gun in both elevation and azimuth, aided by an integrated wind sensor for precision. The fully stabilised mount, weighing under 400 kg, can track and engage moving drones beyond 800 metres using specialised 30x113mm ammunition fitted with radio-frequency proximity fuzes, allowing effective kills against small, fast-moving targets without requiring a direct hit. In Ukraine the modules are being mounted on pickup trucks to form mobile counter-drone fire teams, and the system is also designed for integration onto light armoured vehicles and armoured personnel carriers. The delivery is part of a broader 2023 agreement covering 160 units, with 110 intended for integration onto M113 APCs and 50 ordered by the company Praktika. The systems add a mobile, precision-guided layer to Ukraine's counter-UAS defenses against the ongoing Russian drone and Shahed threat.

Ukraine combat experience shapes Redwire's new Penguin Mk3 drone

2026-09-12

US defense company Redwire has delivered more than 250 of its Penguin reconnaissance drones — a Group 3 fixed-wing UAS with roughly 14 hours of endurance and EO/IR sensors for target recognition — to Ukraine since 2022, largely through US aid packages. That deployment generated a large body of operational data, now feeding into a next-generation variant: the Penguin Mk3. The central lesson is that raw specifications — range, endurance, payload — do not determine whether a tactical drone stays useful. Having the right aircraft, in the right configuration, available at the right moment, along with maintenance speed, training quality and operator workload, proved just as decisive. Russia's heavily contested electromagnetic environment also forced Redwire to rethink navigation and communications assumptions: rather than building in one permanent EW-hardening fix, the company adopted a modular architecture that allows rapid adaptation as adversaries roll out new jamming or spoofing techniques. As a result, the Mk3 adds fully automated vertical takeoff and landing, multiple larger payload bays, winter-operating provisions, and deployability by a two-person crew. An undisclosed NATO country has since signed a multi-year contract for the Mk3, valued in the high eight figures and supported through Redwire's European maintenance and engineering operation. The development reflects a broader shift among Western manufacturers away from treating drones as finished products at delivery and toward continuous iteration — a direct response to the Ukrainian reality that drone tactics and countermeasures can become obsolete within weeks or even days.
