2026-10-20

A fire broke out at an oil pumping station near Michurinsk in Russia's Tambov Oblast after an overnight drone attack on 20 October, according to Telegram monitoring channels cited by Militarnyi and the Kyiv Independent. Governor Yevgeny Pervyshov confirmed that a drone attack had caused a fire "on the territory of one of the enterprises" in Michurinsky district without naming the site. It lies about 350-400 km from the front line. Militarnyi notes that the Nikolskoe and Nikolskoe-1 stations are a few kilometres apart, so the exact facility hit is not yet established. Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces (14th Regiment, according to commander Robert "Madyar" Brovdi) struck the same station on 18 August 2025, causing a fire and a temporary halt in oil transport through Druzhba. The reports do not say whether pumping was interrupted this time. Druzhba is the main remaining route for Russian crude to Hungary and Slovakia, which are exempt from the EU's ban on such supplies; earlier strikes on Druzhba nodes drew protests from Budapest and Bratislava. Ukrainian authorities have not claimed the attack, and neither outlet could independently verify the fire or its effects.

Jet-powered Shaheds push Ukraine's big cities into near-permanent air-raid alerts: Kyiv spent a third of September under alert, as a jet drone can cross the capital in eight minutes

2026-10-19

Since September 2026, following the large-scale appearance of jet-powered Shahed attack drones, Russia's strikes on Ukraine's rear have entered a new phase of escalation, Ukrainska Pravda writes in a survey compiled with local outlets in Odesa, Zaporizhzhia, Kharkiv and Dnipro. Targets now regularly include metals plants and other industry, retail warehouses, railway stations and trains, shopping centres, petrol stations and even kindergartens, and the major cities and their regions live under almost non-stop air-raid warnings. The key tactical effect is time: according to the article, a jet-powered UAV can cross the whole of Kyiv in eight minutes, so that even the city's new 'yellow' threat level (which lets public services keep running when there is no direct missile or drone threat) can hardly be called risk-free. The article does not give speeds, altitudes or flight profiles beyond this, and notes that the Air Force is still looking for ways to counter the jet-powered attacks. The alert figures illustrate the pressure. In September 2026 Kyiv had air-raid warnings every single day, totalling 242 hours 40 minutes (33.7% of the month). Odesa recorded 20 warnings on 18 September, a city record for the full-scale war; Dnipro had five times as many alerts in September 2026 as a year earlier; Kharkiv averages about 10 alerts a day lasting almost an hour and a half each, with one alert on 30 September lasting 10 hours. Zaporizhzhia, a frontline city, faces the full range of weapons: Russian FPV drones now reach the city centre and hit buses at junctions, Shaheds mainly target factories and energy sites, and guided bombs (four or five up to three times a day in summer) are now rare. In Dnipro, mobile and GPS signals are jammed when the threat is high so that electronic warfare can work, and on Kyiv's Pivdennyi Bridge a Shahed narrowly missed a metro train, ending an attempt to keep the overground metro section running during alerts. Civil defence is adapting through tiered threat levels rather than blanket shutdowns. Zaporizhzhia now distinguishes oblast-wide from city alerts and has a separate guided-bomb warning; Kyiv shortened its curfew to 01:00-05:00 on 10 September, restricts traffic on two bridges for 15 minutes after an alert, and closes shopping centres and petrol stations during major attacks; Dnipro hospitals cancel appointments only at the 'red' level. Cities are expanding underground and metro schools (10 underground schools in Zaporizhzhia, metro schools in Kharkiv), mobile shelters and protective netting, gabions and anti-drone cages on minibuses and petrol stations. At the same time, residents increasingly judge risk themselves: many ignore drone alerts, follow the 'two-wall rule' and only take shelter for ballistic missile threats, and shelter capacity remains uneven, notably in Dnipro.

Ukraine's CODE380 develops portable D·Vision SDR analyzer that detects drone signals and displays analog drone video

2026-10-19

Ukrainian company CODE380 has developed the D·Vision SDR, a portable radio-frequency analyzer that scans the airwaves for drone signals and lets the operator view video transmitted over analog channels, Militarnyi reports, citing the Ukrainian tech outlet DOU and the manufacturer. Unlike conventional analog video receivers, the device uses software-defined radio to search for and identify characteristic drone signals across a wide frequency range. It detects PAL and NTSC analog video as well as compatible digital signals from DJI Mavic drones, but it can only play back analog video: DJI digital video is detected, not decoded. According to the manufacturer, analog video can be received and viewed at up to 10 km at 540–6,000 MHz and at 3.5–5 km above 6,000 MHz; DJI Mavic digital signals are detected at up to 2.5 km. The unit has two SMA antenna inputs, a built-in screen, microSD recording, a waterfall display of spectrum activity, an EW-system testing function and USB remote control. Battery life is up to three or six hours depending on the version; it weighs about 290 g without antennas and measures 130 × 80 × 22 mm. Development took about a year and a half in total, and the developers say it was field-tested with Ukrainian service members, including on combat missions. These performance figures are manufacturer claims and have not been independently verified. Militarnyi places the device in a wider Ukrainian trend of portable electronic-reconnaissance detectors for early warning of enemy drones, such as BABAI's Vydun 7.5 (analog video up to 7.5 GHz), the SENSE-4 (up to 8.2 GHz) and the Chuyka 3.0 extended to the 6–8.8 GHz band.
