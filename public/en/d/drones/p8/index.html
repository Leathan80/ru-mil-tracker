Russia unveils 'Dan-T' loitering munition as export counterpart to the 'Banderol' (S8000) missile-drone

2026-09-09SINGLE SOURCE

At the El Alamein International Airshow in Egypt (8-10 September 2026), Russia's state corporation Rostec publicly displayed for the first time a full-scale mockup of the "Dan-T" (Дань-Т) loitering munition. Rostec says the weapon draws on design work in both cruise missiles and unmanned aviation, has completed all development stages, and has been tested on a range and in combat in Ukraine, claiming "hundreds" of launches against real targets — a developer claim with no independent verification. Russian coverage frames the item as sitting "between a missile and a drone." TASS, citing RANEPA analyst Alexander Stepanov, reports that what was shown is the export version of the system Russia already fields under the name "Banderol" (S8000). Separately, Ukraine's HUR deputy chief Vadym Skibitsky has suggested the Dan-T could replace Banderol-type munitions and that its component base is to be fully localized; Russian officials have not confirmed this. Russian sources (Rostec via RIA Novosti and TASS) give a launch weight of up to 270 kg and a range of up to 450 km, with unofficial estimates reaching 500 km. A compact turbojet provides a subsonic cruise, a ceiling of around 9 km and a speed of roughly 750 km/h. Guidance is an autopilot with combined satellite and inertial navigation; in its current form the weapon can only strike targets at known coordinates, functioning as a small cruise missile rather than a true loiter-and-search weapon. Analysts estimate a warhead near 135 kg, which the Topwar author considers implausibly high and which Rostec has not confirmed. Carriers named for the export version are the Yak-130, MiG-29, Mi-28N and Mi-8. Some sources also describe an existing ground-launched variant with range falling to about 250 km. The name echoes the earlier "Dan"/"Dan-M" target drones used to train air-defence crews and, from mid-2025, employed as decoys alongside Russian cruise missiles and UAVs. Ukrainian analysts argue the Dan-T is a direct conversion of the Dan-M target; Russia has not confirmed this and the developer remains undisclosed. The stated rationale is cost and mass: a cheap, simple small missile to saturate enemy air defences and force the expenditure of expensive SAMs — the same niche as Banderol. Ukrainian intelligence-based estimates cited in Russian coverage suggest Russia could build up to ~10,000 such missiles in 2027. All performance figures, the combat record and the export framing come solely from Russian state media (TASS) and a Russian pro-war analysis site (Topwar) relaying Rostec; none of it is independently verified. This entry relates to, and does not duplicate, tracker entries e-20260903-01 and e-20260908-10.

Ukraine develops RK-19 missile to sustain Soviet-era Osa-AKM (SA-8) air defence

2026-09-09

Ukraine has developed the RK-19, a new surface-to-air missile intended to replace the dwindling stock of original 9M33-series missiles for the Soviet-era Osa-AKM system (NATO designation SA-8 Gecko). Per the sources, the missile was designed by the Luch Design Bureau, part of the state conglomerate Ukroboronprom. It was first referenced in an April 2026 video address by President Zelensky and shown publicly at the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Poland. Production volumes and pricing have not been disclosed, indicating the weapon is in development or early production rather than fielded at scale. The stated specifications — all Ukrainian-side/developer claims, not independently verified — are: a range of up to 10 km, a maximum engagement altitude of up to 5 km, and a top speed of 600 m/s, versus roughly 500 m/s for the standard 9M33M3. Guidance remains radio-command with a proximity fuze, identical in principle to the original Soviet design, allowing the Osa vehicle's existing radar and fire-control suite to be retained unchanged. The warhead weighs 14.3 kg (high-explosive fragmentation); the transport-launch container with missile weighs 163 kg (against about 157 kg for the standard round), with dimensions of 3,255 x 320 x 303 mm. In engagement terms the RK-19 is essentially equivalent to the original, its main gain being higher speed — an incremental upgrade, not a redesign. The wider point is sustaining legacy Soviet short-range air defence. Ukrainian Osa systems performed well in 2022 and part of 2023, but it is the missiles, not the launchers, that are running out. A domestically built interceptor lets those dozens of existing vehicles keep engaging precisely the targets now saturating the airspace: jet-powered attack drones and cruise missiles, including — per the sources — Russia's Banderol. For a daily tracker this is an indicator both of Ukrainian weapons self-sufficiency and of the strain on air-defence munition stocks; its operational value depends entirely on whether the claimed performance and production capacity are borne out in practice.

Citing Soviet Cold War experience tracking US carrier strike groups, he argues the hard problem is, and remains, finding a moving squadron at all.
