Russian unit fired ~100 Lancets in three months for only three hits, pro-Russian figure Gubarev claims

2026-10-25

A Russian drone team fired about 100 Lancet loitering munitions over roughly three months from mid-June and hit only three targets, according to pro-Russian militant Pavel Gubarev, the self-declared 2014 'people's governor' of Donetsk who now comments on the war on social media. Defence Blog reports his account via the Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi. Gubarev said the drones were launched by a team from the 'Sparta' unit under Senior Lt. Kirill Ozerov (call sign 'Nevsky'), without naming the sector of the front. The three hits were a self-propelled artillery gun, an infantry dugout and a roadside shop. Most of the other drones were neutralised by Ukrainian air defences, and some were destroyed by Russian forces themselves because units coordinated poorly. Gubarev put the price of one Lancet at 3 to 5.5 million roubles ($37,000–67,000), so the three hits cost roughly 300–500 million roubles (about $3.7–6.1 million). The open-source project Oryx estimates a Lancet at around $35,000, which roughly matches. Gubarev blamed the commanders rather than the operators: in his account they used the expensive drones without proper preparation and sent reports of supposed successes up the chain of command. 'The questions are for the commanders,' he said. The claim comes from one partisan pro-Russian voice and has not been independently confirmed. Defence Blog adds background. The Lancet family is built by ZALA Aero, part of the Kalashnikov Group. It has X-shaped double wings, an optical seeker in the nose, an electric pusher motor, a catapult launch and a briefcase-style control station. Its stated range is over 40 km, and the distances to targets it has struck suggest a maximum of about 50 km. Some versions carry a KZ-6 shaped-charge warhead of about 3 kg, reportedly held in the fuselage with mounting foam. For much of the war the Lancet was one of Russia's most effective weapons against Ukrainian artillery and armour. Ukraine now shoots down growing numbers of them, including with interceptor drones that hunt them in the air.

Lithuania unveils X-12 jet-powered interceptor drone aimed at Russia's Geran-5, reportedly combat-tested in Ukraine

2026-10-25

Lithuania has presented the X-12, a turbojet-powered interceptor drone built to destroy fast aerial targets, according to Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi, which cites the Lithuanian news site Delfi. The primary target named is Russia's jet-powered Geran-5 attack drone. The project is linked to the Strategic Defense Alliance (SDA), an association of Ukrainian, Lithuanian and Latvian defence-technology companies. No single manufacturer is named. Its engines were assembled in Lithuania, and some components come from Latvia and Ukraine. An SDA representative says the drone contains no Chinese-made parts. The developers give a maximum speed of up to 700 km/h, a cruising speed of about 350 km/h, a range of up to 126 km, a maximum operating altitude of 10 km and an endurance of more than 12 minutes, depending on configuration and payload. Warhead, guidance and unit price were not disclosed. The stated goal is for the X-12 to cost less than the drones it hunts, such as a Shahed. According to Delfi, the X-12 has already been used in combat in Ukraine, and the Ukrainian personnel who tested it gave it a positive assessment. Lithuania itself lacks the conditions to test such systems. These performance and combat-test claims come from the developers and have not been independently confirmed. The X-12 is expected to be supplied to the Ukrainian armed forces, and Lithuania is considering adding it to its own air defence. SDA's Richardas Savickas claimed the system currently has no equivalent. Tadas Ruzhele, who coordinates Lithuanian-Ukrainian defence-industry cooperation at Lithuania's Ministry of National Defence, called such interceptors a critical part of layered air defence and stressed the need for trained operators and for lessons from Ukraine's combat experience. Russia's shift to jet-powered Geran variants is pushing up the speed that interceptors need, and Ukraine is pursuing its own jet interceptors as well, including one based on the Bars missile-drone that is planned for joint production with Germany.

Sources: Militarnyi

NYT: North Korea upgrades ballistic missiles and mass-produces attack drones with Russian help

2026-10-24

According to a New York Times report (23 October 2026), relayed by Ukrainska Pravda and SFG Media, North Korea is continuing to upgrade its ballistic missiles and has begun mass-producing attack drones, largely thanks to its military cooperation with Russia. Russia is said to share its own developments with Pyongyang in exchange for soldiers, workers and weapons. A key element is access to Russian data and feedback on how North Korean weapons perform in combat: Anton Ponomarenko of the Pacific Forum says this analysis of battlefield performance has helped improve warhead accuracy and missile manoeuvrability, making the threat to South Korea "more substantial". The reports do not name specific missile types such as the KN-23/KN-24 (Hwasong-11 family); this could not be checked in the original NYT article, which was not accessible. On missiles, the report says Pyongyang is working on more powerful warheads, missiles that can penetrate modern missile defences, shorter launch preparation, harder-to-detect launchers (including tunnel-concealed mobile launchers), submarine-based systems and a shift to solid fuel; some missiles can carry either a conventional or a nuclear warhead. In early October North Korea tested a tactical ballistic missile it claims is nuclear-capable: South Korea and Japan estimated a range of about 700 km, Pyongyang claimed 1,000 km. Ukraine carries out first known aerial casualty evacuation by heavy-lift drone: Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 extracts wounded soldier after 491 days in the grey zone near Kupiansk

2026-10-23

On 4 October 2026 Ukrainian forces evacuated a wounded infantryman from the grey zone on the Kupiansk front by heavy drone. Ukrainska Pravda and Reuters (republished by Meduza) both call it the first known operation of its kind. The soldier is Kostiantyn "Magura" Vlasenko of the 2nd Mechanised Battalion, 30th Separate Mechanised Brigade. He had held a position on the left bank of the Oskil River since 2 June 2025. On 14 February 2026 a Russian drone-dropped munition wounded him in both legs. He lost one foot and amputated the other himself. For months he could not be reached: the area was mined, Russian infantry had infiltrated around his position, and Russian FPV and reconnaissance drones attacked anything that moved. A ground robot sent to fetch him was destroyed on the way (Reuters says two), and infantry groups also failed. Drones resupplied him every four to five days. The aircraft was the Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 from Gurzuf Defence, a modular heavy-lift logistics drone with a cargo winch. Ukrainska Pravda gives its payload as up to 130 kg. Reuters reports roughly 90 kg over up to 25 km, so the sources disagree. The drone was adapted over about six months with help from the 92nd Separate Assault Brigade's unmanned ground systems company. The day before the flight it delivered a mountaineering harness (a Petzl Pitagor evacuation triangle) and a phone with instruction videos, so the soldier could clip himself to the winch cable. Reuters puts the cable height at about 70 m. The drone flew at first light without fog, at a time when Russian drone activity had been assessed as lowest. It used Starlink for control, and Ukrainska Pravda says no jamming was detected. Several measures protected it: Vampire heavy bomber drones flew decoy sorties in neighbouring sectors to draw Russian anti-aircraft FPV operators (one Vampire was shot down), an Autel quadcopter watched overhead, a Lucky Strike interceptor drone stood ready to ram Russian UAVs, and 10th Army Corps artillery fired on identified Russian drone-launch points. Units from the 14th and 43rd Mechanised Brigades also took part, with EW support from the 3rd Army Corps. The 16 km route needed a battery swap at a pre-positioned ground team. The soldier was then handed to a Peacemaker unmanned ground vehicle, which drove him another 8 km to a vehicle and a stabilisation point. Reuters gives different figures: a 15 km distance and a 15-minute flight. Ukrainska Pravda says the drone was airborne for under 1.5 hours in total. Vlasenko is now in rehabilitation in Kharkiv. The operation matters for tactics because ground casualty evacuation in the drone-saturated 'kill zone' has become nearly impossible, and the 'golden hour' is now rarely achievable. The developer itself warns against treating this as a new routine. Gurzuf co-founder Pavlo Feldblium calls aerial evacuation a last resort that needs unique, multi-unit planning (about 15 personnel, two months of planning, seven days of pre-flight preparation). Without decoys, escorts and counter-battery fire, he says, the slow drone with its underslung load would have been shot down by the first Russian interceptor. Gurzuf will nevertheless fit its mass-produced Ayudag-6 drones with evacuation mounting kits and is developing variants with higher payloads. The details come from Ukrainian military participants and the developer, and from Vlasenko's own account. They have not been independently verified.
