Ukrainian 'Nemesis' drone detachment caused about 70% of a US armored brigade's early casualties at Hohenfels, brigade commander writes

2026-10-20

Ukrainian drone operators playing the enemy in a US Army exercise caused about 70% of an American armored brigade's casualties in the first three days, according to an article in Military Review, the US Army's professional journal, reported by Defence Blog. The authors are Col. Michael Ziegelhofer, commander of the 3rd Armored Brigade Combat Team, 1st Cavalry Division ("Greywolf"), and six of his officers. They describe Combined Resolve 26-07, held in spring 2026 at the Hohenfels Training Area in Bavaria, where the opposing force was reinforced by a detachment of the 412th Regiment "Nemesis" of Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces. Over the roughly 10-day rotation the opposing force initiated contact with drones more than 750 times, either striking troops directly or spotting for artillery. By the end of day three the share of drone-caused casualties had fallen below 30%, without the enemy easing off: "Greywolf changed how it fought". According to the authors, roughly 60% of the brigade-level counter-drone fight is fieldcraft and drills. The brigade's counter-drone/EW systems jammed control links in protective bubbles up to 2 km wide, but had to be set up at fixed sites and moved forward in bounds, leaving units exposed on the move, and were less effective against the smaller number of unjammable fiber-optic drones. More than half the formation relied on cover, concealment, dispersion, smaller routes and fast battle drills. Survival had a price: crews switched off Blue Force Tracker, cut radio use and even dropped Starshield satellite links to reduce their electronic signature, and resupply took twice as long as planned because the enemy hunted supply lines. On the offensive side, the brigade brought in the 3rd Battalion, 504th Parachute Infantry Regiment (then an experimental drone battalion) and used more than 700 drones from platoon to brigade level; with relay drones, nearly all could fly 25-40 km, covering the whole ~14 x 27 km training area. That abundance initially overwhelmed the headquarters: collection-planning tools, the operations floor and per-drone airspace management could not keep up, and the staff never had a full picture of how many drones were airborne. The brigade adapted by giving drone units priorities and letting them hunt within the commander's intent, moving the coordinated fire line forward, making enemy drone ground control stations a top target and holding drone munitions in reserve; it ended the rotation with two simultaneous breaches about 6 km apart that the opposing force failed to stop. The officers recommend multifunctional drone strike troops in armored brigades (long-range surveillance to at least 40 km, medium/heavy bomber drones, strike drones with relays), one common operational picture for all drones modelled on Ukraine's Delta system, and counter-drone defences that work on the move, from hard-kill protection on every vehicle to brigade-level radar and defeat systems. This is a different, earlier exercise than the Stork and MV7 trials covered previously; it is one of the clearest first-hand accounts of how a NATO heavy brigade fares against Ukrainian-style drone TTPs, which mirror what Russian forces face and increasingly employ themselves. The figures come from the brigade's own after-action account and concern a training environment.

Ukraine's ANT upgrades Mercury VTOL reconnaissance UAS: up to 300 km range, 4-5 h endurance and FHSS backup link against Russian EW

2026-10-20

Ukrainian company ANT (Aviation New Technologies) has unveiled an upgraded version of its Mercury reconnaissance UAS, Militarnyi reports. According to the developer, maximum altitude rises from 3,000 to 4,000 m, rated endurance from 2.5 to 4 hours (up to five in practice), and maximum range from 170 to 250 km, reaching up to 300 km under operational conditions. Mercury, first shown in June 2025 and codified by Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, is used for tactical reconnaissance, target detection and artillery fire adjustment. The upgrade reflects how Ukrainian ISR drones are being redesigned around Russian jamming and GNSS denial (layered links, FHSS, non-satellite navigation). Militarnyi reports that it accelerated from 0 to 777 km/h in 27 seconds, held top speed for only a fraction of a second and then slowed down and landed. The figure is not yet an official record, because speed records must be flown in both directions to cancel out wind, and Karkour said the return leg is still to come. The demonstrator took about a year to develop and runs on special battery cells from a Porsche subsidiary; the company mentions a powerful electric motor, revised aerodynamics and a compact cooling system. Stability at high speed is the main engineering challenge. Quantum Systems links the project directly to the threat from Russia's jet-powered Geran drones, which Co-CEO Sven Kruck says can reportedly fly at 500–600 km/h, too fast for many of the small interceptor drones Ukraine uses against slower Shahed types. 'Russia is accelerating the threat. We are engineering the response,' Kruck said. According to the company, lessons on propulsion, energy management and high-speed flight feed into its counter-UAS development.
