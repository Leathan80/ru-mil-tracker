German investigation files name GRU officer and Belarusian criminal behind Leipzig bomb-drone attack

2026-09-12

On 4 August 2026, Leipzig/Halle Airport was targeted in an attack involving three explosive-laden drones aimed at a Ukrainian Antonov An-124 cargo aircraft carrying roughly 25,000 litres of kerosene. Two drones were destroyed at launch or failed; the third landed right next to the aircraft carrying about 1.8 kilograms of Semtex hidden inside a vegetable tin, but the detonator became detached, so it never went off. Roughly ten days later, investigators found a third drone and about 50 grams of Semtex near Kabelsketal, Saxony-Anhalt, pointing to a pre-planned operation with multiple launch points and a pre-positioned equipment cache. According to investigation files reported by Welt am Sonntag, German security services have identified two main suspects: Russian national Oleg Le., attributed to the environment of Russia's GRU military intelligence service and allegedly responsible for preparation and logistics, and Belarusian national Andrei Ka., a criminal with prior convictions in Finland and Lithuania, allegedly responsible for the operational role. Key evidence is DNA recovered from glove remnants found on the drone's antenna control equipment, reportedly matching DNA records already on file in Finland and Lithuania. Le. is said to have entered Germany via Turkey and left via Berlin-Brandenburg airport for Serbia and then Russia; German authorities were reportedly already aware of a possible GRU link before his entry, yet he was not placed under permanent surveillance. Polish prosecutors separately link Ka. to a 2024 arson at a hardware store in Łódź. The case fits a broader pattern of suspected Russian sabotage and drone incidents against European infrastructure in 2025-2026, and is politically sensitive because Leipzig/Halle is a hub in NATO's airlift mission supplying weapons to Ukraine. On 1 September 2026, German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul and Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt publicly held Russia responsible, after which Germany closed the Russian consulate-general in Bonn and the Russian House in Berlin; Russia responded by closing Germany's consulate in St. Petersburg and shutting Goethe Institutes, while continuing to reject the accusations. An important caveat: the specific names and the GRU attribution stem from leaked investigation files and journalistic reporting, not from an official German indictment or arrest — both suspects reportedly already left Germany and have not been detained. The facts have not yet been independently confirmed in a legal proceeding.

France Tests Tethered Balloon to Protect Nuclear Rafale Airbase From Drone Strikes

2026-09-12

The French Air and Space Force trialled a tethered balloon (aerostat, 12 by 7 metres) as a drone-detection sensor platform at 113th Airbase Commandant Antoine de Saint-Exupéry in Saint-Dizier-Robinson from 31 August to 4 September 2026. Hovering at roughly 200 metres, the balloon carried an EO/IR camera paired with an AI-based threat-detection algorithm feeding real-time imagery to base security; the manufacturer is not named in available reporting. Saint-Dizier is strategically sensitive: it hosts France's Strategic Air Forces, including the nuclear-tasked Rafale squadrons 1/4 Gascogne and 2/4 La Fayette (ASMP-A/ASMPA-R stand-off missiles, roughly 600 km range). The technical rationale is straightforward: fixed ground-based counter-UAS radar struggles against small, slow, low-flying drones because clutter and terrain masking create a blind zone close to the ground and behind obstacles. A sensor elevated to 200 metres gets a look-down angle that covers exactly that gap, and can stay on station continuously for days at a fraction of the cost of a manned or unmanned aerial patrol. The concept itself is not new — similar optronic captive balloons were used to secure French forward operating bases in Afghanistan and the Sahel — but this marks its first application to a home-station airbase with a nuclear mission. The trial fits a broader European pattern: following the wave of drone sightings over airfields and military sites across 2024-2026 (including Volkel airbase in the Netherlands and the Île-Longue submarine base in France, alongside earlier incidents in Belgium, Germany and Denmark), NATO members are seeking cheap, persistent complements to expensive radar networks. The French Air Force explicitly cites Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb — where FPV drones struck Russian strategic bombers on the ground — as the scenario it wants to forestall. Results are still under evaluation, and any operational fielding will depend on the trial's conclusions.

Почему танки перестали доминировать на поле боя? (Rybar video essay)

2026-09-05SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

Rybar published a short video essay (2:21) from its #ИсторияПодвига series, forwarded from the affiliated channel Тактикарь, asking why the tank no longer dominates the battlefield. Rybar first sketches the classic role: since the early twentieth century the tank was the "steel fist" of armies — it broke through defensive lines, supported infantry and served as an assault gun. Tanks entered the "SVO" in that role, with examples such as a T-80BV crew destroying Ukrainian armour and the "carousel" tactic used against Ukrainian infantry. Then, Rybar argues, everything changed because of "small, cheap products made from commercial components" — FPV drones — which he calls the most serious challenge to the tank since it first appeared on the battlefield. The factors cited are: mass-producible, very cheap FPV drones and anti-tank guided missiles that make any visible vehicle vulnerable; the loss of combined-arms cover (infantry, air defence, electronic warfare) so that tanks can no longer advance under protection; and a cost asymmetry in which a drone worth a few hundred dollars disables a vehicle worth millions. The consequence: both armies no longer use the tank for the breakthrough but as mobile artillery and standoff fire support — firing from cover, then withdrawing quickly. As countermeasures Rybar points to physical protection (cope cages, rubber and chain skirts, and more recently the heavier "hedgehog" cage armour), vehicle-mounted jammers against FPV control links, active protection systems, and above all adapted tactics that minimise exposure; he implicitly concedes that no single measure removes the drone threat. Caveat: this is a single Russian milblogger source (Rybar), a personal/editorial voice and not an MoD position; milbloggers contradict each other and the MoD.
