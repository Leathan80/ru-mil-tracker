Far fewer foggy days this winter deny Russian assault groups their cover from Ukrainian drones, analysis suggests

2026-11-27

In an analysis for Euromaidan Press, David Axe argues that Russian forces rely on fog to cross the drone-saturated gray zone, and that this winter offers much less of it. Last year, Russia's Center Group of Forces used an unusually long run of foggy days in Donetsk Oblast to push a large mechanized assault into Pokrovsk, then held by the 155th Mechanized Brigade, followed by the fall of Myrnohrad. Citing the mapper 'Vitaly' on X, the piece counts 37 foggy days from winter 2025 to spring 2026, including more than a dozen low-visibility days in November–December 2025, against just seven so far this winter. If the trend holds, the season would be roughly half as foggy. The article illustrates the TTP with a 13 November attack towards Hruz'ke, on one of the routes towards Kramatorsk, about 32 km north of Pokrovsk. Under fog, a Russian group of reportedly three tanks, three infantry fighting vehicles and more than ten light vehicles and motorcycles advanced; infantry dismounted near Shakhove while the tanks continued up the road. According to Vitaly, fog roughly halved Ukrainian drone sorties that day. The Ukrainian 1st Assault Regiment instead used fixed-wing Dart drones with thermal cameras, normally reserved for 'middle strikes' on supply convoys, and says it hit ten vehicles, apparently including two tanks south of Hruz'ke, and 30 dismounted infantry. Axe links the lack of fog and better Ukrainian drones to the slow Russian advance. He cites FPRI analyst Rob Lee as saying the Dobropillia axis, the only one where Russia is still committing armor, worsened in July–August but has been relatively stable since September, making capture of the city before the new year increasingly unlikely. The fog counts and strike results come from social media posts and the Ukrainian unit's own claims and have not been independently verified.

Russian senators submit bill to the State Duma criminalising the dissemination of information on the aftermath of drone and missile strikes, with up to five years in prison; pro-war bloggers unanimously criticise it

2026-11-26

A group of Russian senators has submitted a bill to the State Duma that would make it a criminal offence to "unlawfully" disseminate information about the aftermath of attacks on sites in Russia, Meduza reported on 26 November 2026; the bill has been published in the Duma's legislative database and would add a new article to the Criminal Code. Covered would be information about the "aftermath of the enemy's use" of drones and missiles, about the locations of military equipment and weapons storage sites, and about the aftermath of terrorist attacks, sabotage and assassination attempts against government and public figures. The first part of the article, with a maximum of three years in prison, applies to people who already have an administrative violation on record for a similar offence; the second applies to anyone, but only if the dissemination led to serious consequences such as accidental death, harm to health or property, or mass violations of public order, and carries a maximum of five years. According to the explanatory note, the aim is to protect information whose unauthorised dissemination "could endanger the state's defense capability", the safety of the population, and strategically important and military sites. Meduza does not name the senators, and the bill does not define what counts as "lawful" dissemination. The bill would turn a patchwork of regional rules into federal criminal law. By May 2026, when Moscow introduced its own ban on publishing photos and videos of the aftermath of terrorist attacks and drone strikes, more than 30 regions already had similar bans; in Moscow, violations are punished only with fines (3,000-5,000 rubles for individuals, 30,000-50,000 for officials and 50,000-200,000 for legal entities). In military terms, the measure targets the open-source imagery that both the public and the attacking side use to assess where drones and missiles hit and how well Russian air defence performed. The bill is at the submission stage; it has not been debated or adopted. The Kremlin defended it, saying, as quoted by Meduza, that "there is a war going on" and that wartime decisions "must be made to ensure security". In a follow-up on 27 November, Meduza reported that authors of pro-war Z-channels and "military correspondents" reacted with unanimous criticism. Meduza published their views as a single combined, lightly edited text, without attributing points to individual named bloggers. In it, they argue that almost anything could count as unlawful dissemination, from forwarding a video to one's mother to a screenshot from a channel registered with the media regulator Roskomnadzor, and that merely saying the word "strike" could land someone in court: "No reports, no strikes." They say nobody should be jailed over poorly organised regional air defence, warn that the result will be "total censorship" pushing Russians towards Ukrainian social media, and propose prompt official reporting on strikes right after they happen instead of a week later. In their view the law is meant to "shut up" critics and eliminate "unnecessary questions" about how the state allows the attacks to happen, and is "either legally incompetent, or deliberately written so that anyone could be imprisoned".

Ukraine steered FP-5 Flamingo cruise missiles into the Kerch Bridge via a live camera feed, as Kyiv reportedly weighs shifting funds to the Neptune

2026-11-26

In the combined drone and missile strike on the night of 17 November 2026 that brought down one of the Kerch Bridge's two parallel spans, Ukrainian operators steered at least one Fire Point FP-5 Flamingo cruise missile to impact through a live camera feed, much like an FPV drone. That is the assessment of the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) and Ukrainian aviation expert Kostiantyn Kryvolap, based on footage released by President Zelenskyy, who said the missile's "communications uplink worked flawlessly until the very last seconds". According to ISW, a mesh network of drones carrying signal repeaters relayed the link more than 250 km from the front line; a Ukrainian officer quoted by The Times (via Meduza) said the operation showed Ukraine can do this without Starlink. Fire Point itself says two Flamingos hit the bridge: one struck the road-span arch, collapsing it with the roadway, the other the railway section. A satellite image from 19 November shows one arch down. Neptune missiles also took part. Neither Kyiv nor Fire Point has described the guidance system, so the operator-in-the-loop reading rests on the video and the unbroken link. Kryvolap told Euromaidan Press the target demanded "neurosurgery"-level accuracy: the 207-metre rail arch rests on four bearings held only by its own weight, and the aim was to knock it off them, which a missile flying to coordinates could not reliably do. With the 1,150-kg warhead plus roughly five tonnes of missile at impact, the Flamingo had the mass for the job. Open questions remain: video-based estimates put final-approach speed at 400-500 km/h (Fire Point claims a 950 km/h top speed), and Kryvolap does not rule out that the missile homed autonomously with the operator only correcting. Steering also only works within reach of the relay chain, well short of the claimed 3,000-km range. Previously the Flamingo flew on inertial guidance to a preset point and most known launches missed; Euromaidan Press cites Dnipro Osint's count of 12 hits in 53 publicly traced launches. The upgrade surfaces amid reports that Kyiv may curtail the programme. The Telegram channel Ukraine Context claimed Zelenskyy told Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara to "shift the priority" from new Flamingos to Luch's R-360 Neptune, which had "proven themselves much better"; presidential communications adviser Dmytro Lytvyn denied any shift (per LIGA.net), and Fire Point denied similar rumours in late October. Analyst David Axe notes that tracker estimates (Oko Gora, Thorkill) put Flamingo hits at as few as 13 of about 150 produced (around 9%) versus roughly 13% for the Neptune, but argues the two are complementary: the roughly $600,000 Flamingo, now operator-steered, as a very heavy medium-range missile, and the Neptune (warhead no more than 260 kg, 1,000-km range, likely multi-mode seeker) as the more reliable long-range weapon. The funding-cut reports and hit-rate figures are unconfirmed estimates.
