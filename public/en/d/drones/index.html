Russian state-linked entities have spent about 17 billion rubles on anti-drone systems since 2022, Novaya Gazeta Europe finds

Russian state-owned and state-linked organisations have spent about 17 billion rubles on counter-drone systems since the start of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The spending fits a wider push to make infrastructure operators responsible for their own defence against Ukrainian long-range drone strikes. In late August, President Putin signed a decree allowing the state to impose temporary management on critical infrastructure whose owners fail to protect it against security threats, including drone attacks. Rosimushchestvo, the federal property agency, or another body named by the government would then take over.

RUSI: Russia's disposable sabotage networks could be joined by autonomous AI agents that adapt after each failed attempt, making hybrid pressure on NATO cheaper and more persistent

2026-10-29

In a RUSI commentary published on 29 October 2026, guest contributor Dr Ahana Datta Fasel argues that Russia's hybrid campaign in Europe already relies on disposable human proxies and may next draw on autonomous AI agents. She cites the failed sabotage attempt at Leipzig airport in early August, after which The Insider alleged a GRU-linked network (naming Oleg Levushkin as organiser and Andrei Karshakov as behind a 'dud drone'). In her reading, Moscow now accepts operations whose state involvement is hard to deny: untrained operatives are replaceable, and exposure is tolerated because it helps probe NATO's response threshold. The author points to an 'adaptive loop' already visible in AI misuse. Combined, defensive responses would no longer force an attacker to stop and redesign manually. MI5 Director General Ken McCallum said on 13 October that Russia is turning to criminal and volunteer intermediaries as its traditional covert network degrades. The author stresses there is no evidence yet that Russia uses autonomous agents to run a coordinated campaign against NATO, that Moscow has not adopted this as deliberate strategy, and that agents can fail or produce little strategic value. She recommends NATO assess persistent patterns rather than discrete incidents, accept that disruption alone may not deter if adaptation becomes cheap, link technical disruption with intelligence, law-enforcement and financial measures against enabling networks, and align allied views on attribution and response.

Russia is fitting Geran drones with a US-made magnetometer compass that holds course under GPS jamming, but Western electronics remain at their core, Ukrainian officials say

2026-10-28

Russia has begun installing magnetometers in the tails or tail fins of its Geran attack drones, Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's presidential commissioner for sanctions policy, said in a Facebook post on 28 October 2026. A magnetometer acts as an electronic compass: it lets the drone determine its heading without satellite signals, which makes Ukrainian GPS jamming less effective. Vlasiuk did not say which Geran models carry the sensor. He posted photos from a site where Ukraine examines components recovered from Russian weapons, and said that "Western electronics are at the core everywhere". He also said Ukraine is studying a new Russian jamming device, without saying where it was found or what it targets. Vlasiuk's statement matches an earlier finding by Ukrainian Defence Intelligence (HUR). On 5 October HUR reported that the upgraded Geran-4 carries a new navigation unit called "MOKh". It is built around a PNI5GA magnetometer from the US firm PNI Sensor and works with the SADRA inertial navigation module to reduce gyroscope drift and make autonomous navigation more accurate. According to HUR, the upgraded Geran-4 also carries the Susanin optical-correlation system, which compares camera images with a stored reference image to keep the drone on course. HUR says the new K5 version of the Geran-5 uses a similar system. The magnetometer adds a further layer to Russia's efforts to make the Geran family resistant to jamming. Earlier measures included adaptive multi-element (CRPA) antennas and camera-based terrain navigation. Presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov warned on 28 October that if Susanin is used widely at night, Ukraine may have to resume switching off lights, especially on roads. Apart from the PNI5GA, the article does not say where the other components come from. These are claims by Ukrainian officials and HUR that have not been independently verified.

China's new drone export-licensing rules cover Geran engines, turbojets, autopilots and anti-jam navigation, but licensing is not a ban, Ukraine's sanctions commissioner says

2026-10-28

China has updated its drone export controls in Announcement No. 49, issued jointly by the Ministry of Commerce, the General Administration of Customs and the Equipment Development Department of the Central Military Commission. From 18 November, exporters need a licence for listed drone items, including specially designed autopilots and certain drone engines. Brushless motors are covered only if they meet all of these thresholds: at least 48 volts, at least 3 kW, a power density of at least 3 kW per kilogram, and no more than 3,000 rpm. According to Militarnyi, matching speed controllers are covered too. According to the article, FPV motors and controllers, batteries, cameras and optical fibre fall outside the thresholds. A Commerce Ministry spokesperson said China will approve applications that comply with the rules. Some drone engines have required permits since September 2023, so licensing as such is not new. Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's sanctions commissioner, said on 27 October that the rules cover components found in Russia's Geran drones (the Russian version of Iran's Shahed): their piston engines, the turbojets of the jet variants, autopilots, and jam-resistant navigation, which the announcement lists as a technology rather than hardware. According to HUR deputy chief Vadym Skibitskyi, Russia's jet-powered Gerans rely entirely on Chinese turbojets. Reuters earlier reported that some Chinese engines reached Russian drone plants declared as refrigeration units. Vlasiuk stressed that licensing is not a ban. Exporters that Beijing considers reliable can get general permits valid for up to three years, and the rules leave broad discretion to refuse. He nevertheless sees an opening: Beijing now formally takes responsibility for these categories, and Kyiv will push for the rules to be applied to known suppliers of Russia's defence industry, and with partners will track licences, transit routes and re-exports. Militarnyi warned that Russia could keep getting parts with Beijing's approval, while Ukraine, lacking such close ties, could face delays, inspections and higher costs. The article concludes that the update's value lies in enforcement rather than in the list itself.

Russia hits Kyiv with ballistic missiles in two waves on 26–27 October, striking a university campus and again the Pivdennyi (Southern) Bridge; only two missiles reported downed

2026-10-26

Russia struck Kyiv in two ballistic missile waves a few hours apart. The first came on the evening of 26 October. At 21:41 Ukraine's Air Force warned of a ballistic threat from the Bryansk area, and monitoring channels reported missiles approaching through Chernihiv Oblast. Within minutes they hit the left-bank Desnianskyi district: a university campus and its student hall of residence, Kyoto Park, residential buildings and the area near a metro station. Blast damage also reached medical facilities in Dniprovskyi district. Zelensky said there was no military facility nearby. He also said Russia launched ballistic missiles again while emergency services were still working at the scene. An ambulance crew that had arrived was hit and two medics were injured. The second wave came before dawn on 27 October. It hit an administrative building in Podilskyi district and, at about 05:04, the Pivdennyi (Southern) Bridge and the metro infrastructure on it. Euromaidan Press notes that the same bridge's metro infrastructure had already been damaged on 22 October. According to the Air Force, Kyiv was the main target of an overnight attack with Iskander-M/S-400 ballistic missiles (the Air Force reports the two as one category) and about 280 drones: Shahed (69 of them jet-powered), Gerbera and Parodiya decoys. Air defence destroyed or jammed only two of the ballistic missiles. It did the same to 255 drones, 56 of them jet-powered. The Air Force recorded hits at 25 locations and falling debris at 16. Zelensky put the night's total at 16 ballistic missiles and more than 280 drones. Strikes also hit at least ten other oblasts, mainly energy, logistics and urban infrastructure. He called the attack 'a serious escalation in Russia's operation against our logistics.' Police closed the Pivdennyi Bridge to all traffic. Green-line metro trains stopped crossing it and now run only between Syrets and Vydubychi. In Kyiv, one person was killed (a 46-year-old woman) and about 15 were injured, many of them students. Prosecutors counted at least 12 damaged apartment blocks. In his evening address on 27 October, Zelensky said Russia had launched more than 100 ballistic missiles at Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast since 14 October. He said Ukraine does not have enough Patriot missiles. He blamed 'artificial and prolonged delays' in deliveries of Patriot and NASAMS interceptor packages and asked for the means to destroy ballistic missile launchers. Euromaidan Press writes that Patriot is the only system in Ukraine that reliably stops the Iskander-M, and that the interceptor package has waited almost a month for a US signature. The attack and interception figures come from the Ukrainian Air Force and the president. They have not been independently verified.
