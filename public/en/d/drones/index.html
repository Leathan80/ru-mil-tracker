Russian senators submit bill to the State Duma criminalising the dissemination of information on the aftermath of drone and missile strikes, with up to five years in prison

2026-11-26

A group of Russian senators has submitted a bill to the State Duma that would make it a criminal offence to "unlawfully" disseminate information about the aftermath of attacks on sites in Russia, Meduza reported on 26 November 2026; the bill has been published in the Duma's legislative database. It would add a new article to the Criminal Code. Covered would be information about the "aftermath of the enemy's use" of drones and missiles, about the locations of military equipment and weapons storage sites, and about the aftermath of terrorist attacks, sabotage and assassination attempts against government and public figures. The proposed article has two parts. The first, with a maximum of three years in prison, applies to people who already have an administrative violation on record for a similar offence. The second applies to anyone, but only if the dissemination led to serious consequences such as accidental death, harm to health or property, or mass violations of public order; it carries a maximum of five years. According to the explanatory note, the bill aims to strengthen the protection of information whose unauthorised dissemination "could endanger the state's defense capability", the safety of the population, and strategically important and military sites. Meduza's report does not name the senators who submitted it, and it does not define what counts as "lawful" dissemination. The bill would turn a patchwork of regional rules into federal criminal law. Meduza notes that by May 2026, when Moscow introduced its own ban on publishing photos and videos of the aftermath of terrorist attacks and drone strikes, more than 30 regions already had similar bans. In Moscow, violations are punished only with fines: 3,000-5,000 rubles for individuals, 30,000-50,000 for officials and 50,000-200,000 for legal entities. In military terms, the measure targets the open-source imagery that both the public and the attacking side use to assess where drones and missiles hit and how well Russian air defence performed. The bill is at the submission stage; it has not been debated or adopted.

Ukraine steered FP-5 Flamingo cruise missiles into the Kerch Bridge via a live camera feed, as Kyiv reportedly weighs shifting funds to the Neptune

2026-11-26

In the combined drone and missile strike on the night of 17 November 2026 that brought down one of the Kerch Bridge's two parallel spans, Ukrainian operators steered at least one Fire Point FP-5 Flamingo cruise missile to impact through a live camera feed, much like an FPV drone. That is the assessment of the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) and Ukrainian aviation expert Kostiantyn Kryvolap, based on footage released by President Zelenskyy, who said the missile's "communications uplink worked flawlessly until the very last seconds". According to ISW, a mesh network of drones carrying signal repeaters relayed the link more than 250 km from the front line; a Ukrainian officer quoted by The Times (via Meduza) said the operation showed Ukraine can do this without Starlink. Fire Point itself says two Flamingos hit the bridge: one struck the road-span arch, collapsing it with the roadway, the other the railway section. A satellite image from 19 November shows one arch down. Neptune missiles also took part. Neither Kyiv nor Fire Point has described the guidance system, so the operator-in-the-loop reading rests on the video and the unbroken link. Kryvolap told Euromaidan Press the target demanded "neurosurgery"-level accuracy: the 207-metre rail arch rests on four bearings held only by its own weight, and the aim was to knock it off them, which a missile flying to coordinates could not reliably do. With the 1,150-kg warhead plus roughly five tonnes of missile at impact, the Flamingo had the mass for the job. Open questions remain: video-based estimates put final-approach speed at 400-500 km/h (Fire Point claims a 950 km/h top speed), and Kryvolap does not rule out that the missile homed autonomously with the operator only correcting. Steering also only works within reach of the relay chain, well short of the claimed 3,000-km range. Previously the Flamingo flew on inertial guidance to a preset point and most known launches missed; Euromaidan Press cites Dnipro Osint's count of 12 hits in 53 publicly traced launches. The upgrade surfaces amid reports that Kyiv may curtail the programme. The Telegram channel Ukraine Context claimed Zelenskyy told Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara to "shift the priority" from new Flamingos to Luch's R-360 Neptune, which had "proven themselves much better"; presidential communications adviser Dmytro Lytvyn denied any shift (per LIGA.net), and Fire Point denied similar rumours in late October. Analyst David Axe notes that tracker estimates (Oko Gora, Thorkill) put Flamingo hits at as few as 13 of about 150 produced (around 9%) versus roughly 13% for the Neptune, but argues the two are complementary: the roughly $600,000 Flamingo, now operator-steered, as a very heavy medium-range missile, and the Neptune (warhead no more than 260 kg, 1,000-km range, likely multi-mode seeker) as the more reliable long-range weapon. The funding-cut reports and hit-rate figures are unconfirmed estimates.

Germany plans first Bundeswehr drone regiment from 2027, with Ukrainian systems including the Flamingo cruise missile in its deep-strike arsenal

2026-11-25

Internal German government documents obtained by POLITICO, and reported by Militarnyi, show the German Army plans to begin forming the Bundeswehr's first dedicated drone regiment in 2027. It would start with about 350 personnel and grow to about 600 by 2029. The planned structure has six companies: one for reconnaissance and strikes close to German forces, one for protection against enemy drones, two operating deeper behind enemy lines, a Deep Strike company for the most distant targets, and an in-house unit that tests new technology and brings it into service quickly. Berlin estimates that buying thousands of drones for the regiment will cost about €150 million. The German Army would not confirm the details; it said the final structure is still being worked out and that the unit will keep adapting to changes on the battlefield. The planned arsenal mixes Ukrainian, German and US systems; according to the documents, seven of the nine are already in operational use in Ukraine. For close range: the German Quantum Systems Vector reconnaissance UAV, the Bumblebee small strike drone, the Termit unmanned ground vehicle with a heavy machine gun, and the Merops counter-drone system with an interceptor drone, originally developed for Ukraine against Shaheds. For greater depth: the Ukrainian Raybird reconnaissance UAV and the Hornet strike drone, of which Militarnyi says Ukraine used more than 10,000 in strikes in three months this summer. Bumblebee, Merops and Hornet are made by the US company Perennial Autonomy. The Deep Strike company would get an unnamed long-range reconnaissance UAV with a reported range of up to 2,000 km and Fire Point's Ukrainian Flamingo (FP-5) cruise missile, which the German document credits with a range of more than 2,000 km. Defence Minister Boris Pistorius said supporting Ukraine "has long ceased to be a one-way street", because the Bundeswehr is drawing on Ukraine's experience with unmanned systems. In ArmyInform (Ukraine's Defence Ministry outlet), analyst Valentyn Badrak links the regiment to new agreements: in Berlin, Ukrainian Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara and Pistorius signed an implementation agreement for a joint defence-production project giving Ukrainian manufacturers access to German software and the Bundeswehr access to combat-tested Ukrainian systems, and Germany is the first partner country to get access to Ukraine's DELTA battle-management ecosystem.
