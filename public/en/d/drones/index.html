Ukraine's CODE 9.2 regiment flies two wounded soldiers out of the grey zone with SkyFall's StarGate drone, one leg nearly 30 km each way under GPS jamming

2026-10-26

Ukraine's 475th Separate Assault Regiment (CODE 9.2) says it evacuated two wounded soldiers by air within a single week using the StarGate casualty-evacuation drone built by the Ukrainian company SkyFall. The casualties, Yevhen (call sign Izium, 25) and Artem (call sign Kolobok, 24), had severe limb injuries and were in the grey zone about 5 km from the Russian border. According to the regiment, difficult terrain, water obstacles and Russian FPV drones made ground evacuation impossible. The missions took a week of preparation with SkyFall and other units; heavy Vampire strike drones suppressed Russian positions during the operation. On one mission StarGate flew almost 30 km to the soldier's position and roughly the same distance back with him on board, while Russian electronic warfare tried to jam satellite navigation completely. The regiment and SkyFall say the drone kept flying on an AI-based navigation system without GNSS. SkyFall gives StarGate a payload of up to 150 kg, a top speed of 105 km/h and a range of up to 30 km; for CASEVAC it carries a medical capsule with a certified stretcher. Regiment commander Oleksandr 'Flint' Nastenko says the unit will set up a dedicated medical aviation element within its medical company to evacuate wounded by air. This is a second system and a different unit from the first known drone CASEVAC that the tracker reported on 24 October (a Heavy Shot drone made by Gurzuf Defence, flown by soldiers of the 92nd and 30th Mechanized Brigades). Militarnyi calls the CODE 9.2 flights the second and third known instances worldwide. The details come from the regiment and the manufacturer and have not been independently verified.

2026-10-26SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

In comments the author of the Fighterbomber Telegram channel gave to the Russian state outlet Sputnik (reposted on his channel), he says weather balloons have been flying in both directions since the first day of the war. Initially they served as decoys for air defence, carrying reflectors to imitate targets; later they were used as relays to extend the range of radio communications and of control links for drones of all types. Now, he says, they are used to drop payloads behind enemy lines — reconnaissance equipment, SIM cards, phones, explosives, weapons, detonators — and balloons carrying drones have also appeared. According to Fighterbomber, balloons are fitted with GPS trackers and steered by launching into known wind flows at altitude, but the arrival time at a target cannot be calculated precisely; even a satellite dish could be attached. He argues no effective counter has been found: balloons are hard to see visually or on radar and fly higher than most surface-to-air systems, some even above fighter ceilings, citing the Chinese balloon over the US. Shooting one down with an infrared-guided air-to-air missile is possible because the balloon heats in the sun, but very expensive. What remains is tracking launch and likely landing or drop points, and aircraft cannon rounds designed for firing at balloons that have not been used for many years. Helsing unveils AD-2 'Ambush' counter-UAS interceptor missile (engagement altitude above 9 km) as part of its Contra air-defence suite, aimed at Russia's fast jet-powered Geran drones

2026-10-26

German defence company Helsing presented its Contra air-defence offering on 26 October 2026: two new interceptor missiles plus AI software that automatically calculates the order and distance of intercepts. According to Defense News, the company says Contra can down fast attack drones for under $100,000 per shot; it is still in testing, is expected to be market-ready in the second half of 2027, and one contract has already been signed. CEO and co-founder Gundbert Scherf framed the design as a direct response to Russia's Geran drones: the early, propeller-driven types were intercepted at rates of about 90%, but Russia now fields thousands of Geran-4 and Geran-5 drones flying at 400–700 km/h, and according to Scherf almost half of them now get through. The AD-2 'Ambush' is the solid-rocket-motor half of the pair. Militarnyi reports a speed above Mach 2.5, a combat radius of more than 20 km and a maximum engagement altitude of over 9 km (company president Niklas Köhler spoke of fire superiority at altitudes up to 10 km); Defense News describes it as more than twice the speed of sound, almost 3,000 km/h, intended as a last-ditch weapon against 'leakers' about to hit the ground. It uses an AI-enabled onboard active radar seeker, receives mid-course command updates from ground radars, and is designed to work at night and in bad weather. Helsing says the seeker is built from widely available components, free of Chinese supply-chain or US export-control dependencies, using two independent European supply chains. The companion AD-1 'Inferno' is jet-powered, flies at about 700 km/h with a 20 km range and is meant as the standard countermeasure. Ambush missiles are launched from transport-launch containers holding 20–40 missiles, mountable on vehicles or rail platforms, and will be built in Helsing's 'Resilience Factories'; a company animation showed 20 Inferno and 40 Ambush interceptors leaving shipping containers to engage a swarm. All performance figures are company claims from the product presentation and have not been independently verified.

Russia's Digital Development Ministry recommends underground or bunkered strategic data centres after Ukrainian drone strikes; Yandex relocates all three supercomputers

2026-10-26

Russia's Ministry of Digital Development has approved recommendations for protecting data centres against drone attacks and other aerial threats, business daily Kommersant reported, citing a document it reviewed. Critical data centres are to be split into three categories by the consequences of an attack; category K1 covers strategic facilities whose damage could disrupt key government functions for an hour or more. For these, the recommendations call for underground or bunker placement. They also call for reducing data centres' visibility from the air and combining 'physical reinforcement' with electronic-warfare equipment and counter-air systems, and apply to facilities being designed, built or already operating, regardless of owner. The move follows a Ukrainian campaign against Russian data centres that began in October in response to Russian strikes on data centres in Kyiv. Within a week, Ukrainian drones hit three of Yandex's six data centres — in Sasovo (Ryazan region), Kaluga and Vladimir — and later a Sberbank data centre in the Saratov region. IT-market sources cited by Kommersant estimate that replacing equipment knocked out at Yandex will take up to six months. Separately, Yandex said it has moved all three of its supercomputers — Lyapunov, Galushkin and Chervonenkis — out of their previous locations to new computing sites, and that they were not damaged in the drone attacks. Two of them were housed at Sasovo; Chervonenkis ranked 19th in the world TOP500 list in 2021. A source cited by Forbes Russia said the three machines are independent nodes rather than one supercluster, so they can be placed separately. The claim that they were undamaged comes from Yandex itself and has not been independently verified.

Kyiv's air defence crews struggle to keep pace with Russia's jet-powered Geran drones

2026-10-26

A Kyiv Independent field report (26 October 2026), based on two days with Ukraine's 112th Separate Territorial Defense Brigade outside Kyiv, describes how jet-powered Geran drones, used against Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast since late summer, are straining the capital's layered air defence. Waves of jet drones now arrive day and night between larger mass assaults. Some variants fly at about 600 km/h and at 5–6 km altitude, beyond the reach of mobile machine-gun crews. Soldiers say drones change route and manoeuvre when interceptors approach, and that some newer Geran variants carry cameras and mesh radios, giving operators a live video feed and the ability to redirect them in flight. The report details each layer. Radar and other sensors pass direction, altitude and distance via combat command posts to three-man mobile fire groups armed with a truck-mounted Browning M2HB, a SkyLock targeting system and thermal imagers. They fire only within assigned sectors and must calculate the lead themselves. One gunner says he has about five seconds to engage, and another says a high-flying jet drone was barely visible to him. An Igla (9K38) MANPADS operator says that, unlike propeller Shaheds, jet drones and cruise missiles give almost no audible warning, and some cross his engagement area in about 20 seconds. Operators of Sting interceptor drones (about 320 km/h top speed) say they struggle to catch the faster jet drones. On 18 October, Brave1's then-head Andrii Hrytseniuk said seven Ukrainian interceptor types had already downed jet drones, that 22 teams were developing high-speed models (some with miniature jet engines), and that more than 1,000 had been ordered, though production is still being scaled up. NASAMS and IRIS-T remain the main systems against cruise missiles and aircraft, and Patriot against ballistic missiles. The brigade's EW/ELINT chief (call sign "Secret") says jamming and spoofing can only complement kinetic interception, because some drones fly on to pre-programmed coordinates without a datalink. His unit analyses wreckage to track how Russia keeps changing drone communications. He says the "fashion" changes every three to six months, so new equipment must be added while old systems stay in service. An officer describes Russian probing with small salvos from all directions to map routes ahead of a mass attack. Crews have worked 20-hour shifts during intense periods, which one commander calls a tactic of "constant exhaustion". All of this is the brigade's own account, as reported by the Kyiv Independent.

In an opinion piece, Topwar columnist Yevgeny Fedorov argues that slow piston-engined long-range strike drones are losing effectiveness on both sides. Russia's answer was jet-powered strike drones, culminating in the Geran-5, which he says Ukrainian propeller interceptors cannot catch and which leaves Ukraine relying on MANPADS. He claims Russia produces 'at least 3,000' jet kamikaze drones a month with localised engines, while Ukraine lacks small-turbojet technology and has only the jet-powered 'Palianytsia' in small numbers. On defence, he credits systematic protection of Russian refineries against slow drones for ending this summer's fuel shortages and lifting rationing in some regions. He cites a 2026 CIAM (Central Institute of Aviation Motors) study, published in the journal Aviatsionnye Dvigateli by Lukovnikov and Zinenkov. It modelled an 8,000 kg UAV with turbojet, turboprop, piston and electric power: piston engines gave the longest range at low altitude, while turbojets perform best at 11-13 km. From this he argues that a Ukrainian deep-strike jet drone would have to fly high and would be intercepted. On the latest raid on St Petersburg, he says Ukraine launched 'up to a thousand' drones but only 15 reached the city. He reports one person killed and about 15 injured, a VK data centre and an Ozon warehouse that kept operating, and Pulkovo airport closed for six hours. He puts the cost of the attack to Kyiv at $60-80 million. He warns that Ukraine could pick unprepared targets such as Yandex (he says rooftop chiller units at data centres were left uncovered), shift its mass launches to a 200-500 km band, or turn to attacks on crowded places. The piece is strongly polemical, including language about the 'Zelensky regime', and gives no sources for its production and raid figures. NYT: North Korea upgrades ballistic missiles and mass-produces attack drones with Russian help

2026-10-24

According to a New York Times report (23 October 2026), relayed by Ukrainska Pravda and SFG Media, North Korea is continuing to upgrade its ballistic missiles and has begun mass-producing attack drones, largely thanks to its military cooperation with Russia. In early October North Korea tested a tactical ballistic missile it claims is nuclear-capable: South Korea and Japan estimated a range of about 700 km, Pyongyang claimed 1,000 km. Five days later South Korea test-fired its own hypersonic ballistic missile for the first time, observed by President Lee Jae-myung, which the NYT reads as growing unease in Seoul. Russia has also helped North Korea develop and mass-produce its own unmanned systems; Kim Jong Un visited a drone factory last month and photos suggest series production has started. In a September drill a drone swarm attacked a target island while cruise and ballistic missiles struck simultaneously from different directions — a tactic that, according to the NYT and Hong Min (KINU), could force South Korea and its allies into very costly air-defence upgrades. Background: in a May 2026 Lowy Institute analysis, Ponomarenko noted that North Korean troops fought in the Kursk region and remain active in Kursk Oblast, that Russian Defence Minister Belousov decorated them for the Kursk operations and announced a 2027–31 cooperation plan, and estimated North Korea's earnings from arms deals with Russia at about US$14.4 billion, alongside technology transfers in drones, loitering munitions, ballistic and air-defence systems.
