««Бандероль» на «Иноходце»: против разных целей» (Topwar: 'Banderol on the Inokhodets: against various targets') — Izvestia claims Inokhodets/Orion UAVs strike Ukrainian drone control points, with Banderol cruise missiles in the mix

2026-11-06SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar (author Kirill Ryabov) builds on a 5 November report by the Russian daily Izvestia, which cites unnamed sources familiar with the combat zone. According to Izvestia, heavy Inokhodets (export name Orion) reconnaissance-strike UAVs are being used against Ukraine's drone forces by hunting down the ground sites from which Ukrainian UAVs are based and controlled. As an example, it describes a recent episode in the area of the 'Centre' group of forces: scouts of a motor-rifle formation used an electro-optical station to spot antennas and cable runs of Ukrainian drone systems, located a control post in a concrete shelter, and passed the coordinates to a unit of the Unmanned Systems Forces. An Inokhodets crew then struck it with a guided missile of unspecified type; Izvestia's sources say the fortified post was put out of action together with its duty crew and its communications and control equipment. Topwar stresses that detection and strike were carried out by different units. The weapon used in that episode is not named, but Izvestia points to the use of Banderol air-launched cruise missiles, linking them to the Inokhodets' growing ability to hit distant and fortified targets; a dedicated concrete-piercing warhead for the Banderol is not confirmed. Per Izvestia, the Banderol has a warhead of up to 150 kg, a small turbojet, a speed of up to 650 km/h and a range of 500 km; Ukraine's HUR has estimated its length at about 5 m and wingspan at about 2.2 m. It is described as navigating by autopilot with satellite and inertial guidance against targets with known coordinates; whether terminal homing will be added is unknown. First reports of Banderol launches from the Inokhodets date from spring 2025, and in July 2026 Russian television showed the UAV being prepared for a sortie with a large munition that a serviceman called a 'Banderol'. The Mi-28N attack helicopter has also been named as a prospective carrier. For the platform, Topwar cites the published Orion-E export data: 8 m length, 16 m wingspan, 1,150 kg maximum take-off weight, 250 kg payload, 200 km/h cruise speed, 7,500 m ceiling, at least 24 hours endurance with a standard payload and 250 km line-of-sight radius, with an optional satellite link; the configuration of front-line aircraft may differ. Topwar notes that limited speed and a small munition load remain constraints. Euromaidan Press reports that Ukraine's 237th Unmanned Systems Battalion ("Apachi", 7th Air Assault Corps), which operates west of Huliaipole in Zaporizhzhia Oblast, appears to be testing a new anti-vehicle tactic. A video montage the battalion published on 30 October shows strikes on trucks, armoured vehicles and artillery; most were carried out by ordinary FPV drones, but a few by much heavier fixed-wing drones. According to the article, Ukrainian units normally reserve fixed-wing types such as the Fire Point FP-1 and FP-2 for deep strikes hundreds of kilometres behind the line, or for "middle strikes" on Russian supply routes. The driver is the Russian response to FPVs. Russian assault vehicles are now fitted with layers of anti-drone protection (cages, plates, mesh and "spines") that detonate an FPV warhead at a safe distance from the hull; some of these "turtle", "hedgehog" and "porcupine" vehicles have reportedly absorbed dozens of FPV hits before being knocked out. The article contrasts costs: an FPV costs about $500 and carries a warhead of around 1 kg, while an FP-1/FP-2 may cost about $50,000, weighs hundreds of kilograms and carries a warhead of up to 158 kg. If dozens of FPVs are needed for one up-armoured vehicle, a single fixed-wing drone that virtually guarantees a catastrophic kill could be the more cost-effective option. The outlet quotes observer Moklasen, who predicts that renewed Russian armoured assaults would be met with "middle strikes" by large fixed-wing drones. The battalion faces the Russian 127th Motor Rifle Division and adjacent units. According to the article, Ukrainian brigades counterattacked on this axis in February, helped by the sudden disabling of thousands of Starlink terminals used by Russian forces, and recovered months of Russian gains. Mapper Clément Molin is quoted as saying that Ukraine has stabilised the southern front in 2026 and regained more territory than it lost, while Russian forces continue attacking from Huliaipole along a front about 30 km wide with limited progress. The article itself presents the switch to fixed-wing drones as a possibility, not yet an established practice.

Ukraine lets private air defence groups protect other companies' sites and share systems

2026-11-05

The Ukrainian government has expanded its experimental private air defence project: companies that run their own air defence groups may now protect the facilities of other companies under contract, not only their own sites. Ukrainska Pravda reports this citing the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence. The amended rules also allow air defence systems to be transferred for use between authorised companies, on the basis of a joint decision with Air Force Command. The entry criteria now include status as a resident of Defence City (Ukraine's special regime for defence firms) and/or designation by the Ministry of Defence as a critically important business. Private groups are now allowed to use manned aircraft, the government has regulated companies' use of warheads and electric detonators for interceptor drones, and the minimum age for group members has been lowered from 21 to 18. The private groups remain under the control of the military command and operate within the unified air defence system of the Armed Forces of Ukraine. According to the same report, by 14 October almost 100 companies were taking part in the project and about 50 air defence groups had been formed; groups from six companies had flown combat missions and destroyed more than 60 aerial targets. The step follows an earlier simplification, at the end of October, of the General Staff's rules for radars and EW used by private air defence, and comes as firms such as Nova Post already field their own private air defence unit.

Large-scale GPS jamming mapped along NATO's eastern flank, from the Baltic to the Black Sea

2026-11-04

Large-scale interference with GPS satellite navigation has been recorded along almost the entire eastern flank of NATO, from the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea, Militarnyi reports. The finding comes from the OSINT project ItaMilRadar, which published a map of navigation interference as of 3 November 2026 based on data from the AirNav Radar flight-tracking service. The heaviest interference was concentrated along NATO members' borders with Russia, Belarus and Ukraine: near Russia's Kaliningrad region, in Lithuania, eastern Poland, Romania and Bulgaria, and over the western Black Sea. Citing Defence Blog, Militarnyi notes that the geographic pattern partly overlaps with areas crossed by the flight paths of Russian attack drones. The map is a snapshot built from aircraft navigation data; it does not by itself attribute each interference zone to a specific emitter. The report places the snapshot in a longer pattern of Russian navigation interference in the Baltic region. In May the Finnish Border Guard reported an increase in Russian GPS jamming in the Baltic, notably in the Archipelago Sea and the Åland Sea. In August Denmark recorded a record number and duration of satellite-navigation disruptions; the crew of the cargo vessel Saturn reported losing GPS for about 24 hours while sailing between Gdańsk and southern Gotland.
