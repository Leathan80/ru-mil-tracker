Latvian firm Rannon prepares small unguided anti-drone missiles, launched from drones, for testing in Ukraine

Latvian defence company Rannon is developing compact, unguided air-to-air missiles to counter aerial targets and is finalising the system for testing in Ukraine, according to Militarnyi, which spoke to a company representative at the Defence Innovation Forum 2026 in The Hague. The launchers can be mounted on fixed-wing drones and quadcopters. The number of missiles is limited only by the carrier's payload; a typical set-up carries 4 to 12, and Rannon has tested two launchers operating at once. According to the representative, mounted on a jet-powered fixed-wing UAV the missile could even intercept new drones such as the Shahed. Aiming relies on a ballistic computer and AI target recognition, and the system can be integrated with air-defence command-and-control systems. The operator places a crosshair on the target using a standard remote control, confirms lock-on and fires. Rannon says the missile can be used both surface-to-air and air-to-surface. No price was given. Rannon is also developing solid-fuel rocket boosters for fixed-wing UAVs and interceptor drones, so they can be launched without catapults or other special infrastructure. First booster tests are planned for January, and the unguided rockets are expected to be finalised by mid-2027. The company says the system is not yet in mass production and has not been used in Ukraine; it wants to complete testing before sending it for combat trials. Rannon was one of the winners of the first phase of BraveTech EU, the EU-Ukraine defence innovation initiative. Stockholm-based start-up Nordic Air Defence (NAD), founded in 2023, on 20 November unveiled the K200, an autonomous counter-drone interceptor designed to destroy faster, jet-powered attack drones, including Russia's Geran-3/3A and, according to Euromaidan Press citing Tectonic Defense, the Geran-4. It is not currently designed to intercept the newer Geran-5. The company says the system is deploying to Ukraine for test and evaluation. NAD has opened a staffed office in Ukraine and has operations and production partnerships in Ukraine, Poland and the United Kingdom; it says it can build up to 1,000 interceptors a month at its own facilities and plans to expand through international production partners. Testing in Ukraine is to inform further development as it prepares to offer the K200 in multiple markets. The K200 succeeds NAD's Kreuger 100 family (the battery-powered K100 reaches up to 270 km/h, the upgraded 100XR up to 354 km/h, according to Tectonic Defense). The K200 is about twice the size, faster according to the company, and carries a 500-gram warhead instead of 250 grams, yet NAD says it costs about the same to build. According to CEO Karl Rosander it was developed in 70 days and hit nine of 10 targets in testing. NAD gave no top speed, range, unit price or test date. It is a fixed-wing aircraft with a single propeller. External radars and electro-optical/infrared cameras steer it toward the target, and its own infrared camera takes over for the final approach. It can reportedly detonate near a target rather than needing a direct hit, with an estimated blast radius of 10 to 12 metres, and if it misses it can automatically go around and re-engage. NAD has teamed up with Volvo Defense on a truck-mounted counter-drone system using the 100XR. Rosander said defenders cannot keep using expensive missiles against drones that cost a fraction of the price, and that Europe needs interceptors that can keep pace and be produced in volume.
