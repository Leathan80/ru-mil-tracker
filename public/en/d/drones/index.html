Topwar (Voennoye Obozreniye) describes the 'Depesha-MK', a new unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) from the High Precision Systems holding (Vysokotochnye Kompleksy), part of Rostec. Rostec first announced the Depesha family of ground robots on 1 June 2024. The MK version was shown in cargo configuration, with a mesh load compartment, at the 'Army Rationalisers' exhibition on 22-23 July 2026, where the holding displayed seven ground robots; Rostec announced its development on 16 November with demonstration footage of the robot carrying boxes over dirt and climbing a sloped test track. It is a remotely controlled tracked platform with a metal hull and two electric motors, which Rostec says are more powerful than before, allowing it to tow heavier trailers (no trailer mass was given). The exhibition placard lists a payload of up to 400 kg, a top speed of 15 km/h, a range of up to 40 km and up to 6 hours per charge, each stated separately; range when fully loaded was not specified. For comparison, Rostec gave 150 kg for the basic tracked Depesha in 2024, and Za Rulyom cites 150-180 kg for the Depesha-3. The article frames the MK as a larger complement to the compact Depesha-3, so the family now offers cargo platforms of different capacities; the practical gain is fewer trips per batch of supplies. Rostec says vehicles of the family can be fitted for casualty evacuation, but gave no stretcher count for the MK. The exhibition sample and the demonstration vehicle differ in running gear (more road wheels on the latter), without explanation. High Precision Systems reported two deliveries from the family in 2025 (wheeled Depeshas with special modules in January, tracked vehicles in May), and Rostec has reported Depesha-3 use for cargo delivery and evacuating wounded. According to the article, as of 23 November 2026 there was no open confirmation of serial production, deliveries or combat use of the MK version, and Rostec published no test results. Caveat: single-source reporting (Topwar, Russian defence outlet), based largely on Rostec's own announcements and exhibition placards; the performance figures are manufacturer claims and are not independently verified.

Ukraine's Defence Ministry says over 4,000 Russian ground robots hit in 2026, forcing Russia to put troops and armour into supply runs

2026-11-24

Ukraine's Defence Ministry said on 23 November that its unmanned systems units had destroyed or disabled more than 4,000 Russian ground robots (UGVs) since the start of 2026. It says each strike was filmed, logged in the DELTA combat system and verified by specialists from Brave1, the state defence-innovation cluster. The ministry had reported 3,000 on 17 September. Its monthly figures run from 460 in January, through lows of 215 in March and 223 in April, to a peak of 511 in August. They stayed above 450 through October. In the first 23 days of November there were 258, about 11 a day against roughly 15 a day in October. The ministry gave no reason for the slowdown. According to the ministry, destroying Russian robots disrupts supplies and assault preparation. It says this forces Russia to bring armoured vehicles and troops back into logistics runs, which increases Russian losses. Both sides use ground robots to replace people on the most dangerous supply runs in front-line zones where vehicles cannot move safely. In February a press officer of Ukraine's 79th Kherson Border Detachment told ArmyInform that Russian troops were turning to robots because Ukrainian units were systematically disrupting Russian logistics. He said the robots were hard to spot because of weather, camouflage and their use of field roads. On the Ukrainian side, the ministry says its own ground robots flew 31,398 supply and evacuation missions in October, more than four times the January figure. The figures are Ukrainian official claims and have not been independently verified. Euromaidan Press notes an unexplained inconsistency: the General Staff's running tally of Russian losses since February 2022 lists only 3,213 ground robotic systems as of 23 November, fewer than the ministry's count for 2026 alone. The ministry also did not say how many ground robots Russia fields, so the share of the fleet hit is unknown. The claim that the losses push Russian troops and armour into supply runs is the ministry's own assessment.

Ukrainian-Norwegian DESNA air defence system, built around cheap Ukrainian missiles and Kongsberg fire control, already in use against Russian drones; Norway plans at least $7bn in aid for 2027

2026-11-24

Ukraine is already using DESNA against Russian drones. It is a new air defence system developed jointly by Ukrainian industry and Norway's Kongsberg, the maker of NASAMS. Norwegian Defence Minister Tore O. Sandvik announced this on 24 November 2026 after meeting his Ukrainian counterpart Yevhenii Khmara in Oslo. According to Kongsberg and the Norwegian side, the system combines Ukrainian-made missiles and launchers with a Norwegian fire-control centre, and complements the NASAMS systems Ukraine has operated since 2022. Militarnyi, citing NRK, reports Sandvik as saying that Norway bought and delivered several systems in recent months and is working to expand the project to cover more of Ukraine. Sandvik stressed that the Ukrainian missiles are cheaper than traditional interceptors and can be produced in large batches, at a time of global shortage of air defence missiles. Khmara said DESNA has proven effective against Russian drones, including jet-powered ones, and that both sides discussed scaling it up. Key technical details remain unconfirmed. No official has named the missile. Militarnyi links it to the 130-mm RK-2PZ, which Ukroboronprom presented in September at the MSPO fair in Poland for DESNA and upgraded Strela-10 systems, and which is derived from the Barrier anti-tank missile. Euromaidan Press notes that this link has not been officially confirmed and that the Ukrainian industrial partner has not been named. No figures have been published on systems delivered, drones downed or cost per missile, and the effectiveness claims come from the Ukrainian minister, not from independent data. The project builds on earlier steps: in 2025 Kongsberg announced plans for Ukrainian interceptors and agreed with Ukroboronprom to connect Ukrainian air defence systems to NASAMS command modules. The meeting also produced wider agreements. According to the Ukrainian Defence Ministry, Norwegian bilateral aid in 2027 will total at least $7 billion, focused mainly on air defence (including interceptor missiles) and unmanned systems. The ministers discussed integrating other missile types into Ukraine's NASAMS, additional combat aircraft and ammunition, joint production and the exchange of combat experience. They also signed a letter of intent under which Norway's armed forces will test and adapt Ukraine's DELTA battle management system through the DELTA Lab initiative; both sides say no combat or operational data will be transferred. The Kyiv Independent places the deal against a severe interceptor shortage: Zelensky said in October that Ukraine receives on average only 12 Patriot missiles a month, while Russia launched about 2,700 drones, 165 missiles and nearly 1,460 glide bombs in the week before 22 November.

German Lynx KF41 fighting vehicles arrive with Ukraine's 21st Mechanized Brigade, which plans to use their airburst cannon against Shahed drones

2026-11-24

Ukraine has received its first Rheinmetall Lynx KF41 infantry fighting vehicles from Germany, the 18th Army Corps and the 21st Separate Mechanized Brigade announced on 24 November. Neither gave the number of vehicles or the date of transfer. In January 2026 Rheinmetall said the first batch, under a contract signed in December 2025, covered five vehicles worth a mid double-digit million euro sum, paid for by the German government. They have the two-person Lance turret in a configuration built for Ukraine. Rheinmetall says further batches will follow, including production in Ukraine, where it has opened a factory. The brigade says it will also use the Lynx against Russian drones, Shahed-type strike drones included. A senior technician called 'Bankir' described the method: when a Shahed approaches, the crew locks on and fires five programmable rounds ahead of the target. The rounds detonate in front of it and form a cloud of fragments. The 30 mm autocannon fires Rheinmetall's AHEAD airburst ammunition, with the fuze timing set by the fire-control computer. A 12.7 mm machine gun sits in a remote weapon station. The 18th Corps says the fire-control system and programmable ammunition let the vehicle engage both ground and air targets. The brigade will also use the Lynx for its normal role: carrying up to eight troops and engaging infantry and light armor. The War Zone cautions that the Lynx is not an optimised air-defence system. It has no visible radar, so it would rely on passive sensors, possibly cued by external systems, and on crews acquiring targets through narrow optical feeds. Its counter-drone effectiveness is therefore unproven, and the vehicles have not yet been seen in combat. Rheinmetall does make a dedicated air-defence variant, the Skyranger 30, with its own radar and short-range missiles, but that is not the version Ukraine received. The 21st Brigade was also the first Ukrainian unit to operate Swedish CV90s, in 2023.

Russian drone strikes on Ukraine, 23-25 November: Korosten railway station hit as Russia widens its campaign against the railways; FPV drones hit civilian vehicles, a bus and energy workers near the front

2026-11-23

On 24 November a Russian drone attacked the railway station in Korosten, Zhytomyr Oblast, while an electric train was standing there. According to Ukrzaliznytsia (Ukrainian Railways) and Zhytomyr Oblast head Vitalii Bunechko, debris killed one person and injured two. After a warning from the railway's monitoring centre, the train crew declared a heightened alert, and staff moved passengers into a large shelter at the station; no railway staff were hurt. In the same attack a Nova Poshta branch and commercial warehouses in Korosten caught fire, and strikes in Zhytomyr city damaged two businesses, residential buildings, two educational institutions and 36 vehicles. An energy facility in Zhytomyr district was also hit. For the night of 24-25 November Ukraine's Air Force said Russia launched 112 Shahed (66 of them jet-powered) and Gerbera attack drones and Parodiya decoys. It said 88 had been shot down or jammed by 08:00, with hits at 18 locations and debris at 13. In Kyiv a drone fell on a warehouse in Holosiivskyi district during a short daytime alert on 24 November, and that evening debris set fire to the two lowest floors of a 16-storey residential building in Shevchenkivskyi district, injuring two people. In Volyn Oblast's Kovel district three jet-powered Shaheds were reported on the evening of 24 November; debris and impacts injured five people and set lorries, cars and a building on fire. Alongside the long-range raids, Ukrainian officials reported short-range drone attacks on civilian traffic near the front. On 23 November a Russian FPV drone hit a vehicle of DTEK energy workers inspecting power lines near a frontline village in Dnipropetrovsk Oblast; the vehicle was damaged and nobody was hurt, according to DTEK. On 24 November an FPV drone seriously injured a 39-year-old woman in Zaporizhzhia city. The same day a fibre-optic-controlled FPV drone struck a civilian car in Sumy, killing a 76-year-old passenger and injuring the driver, his son, according to the head of Sumy's military administration. On the morning of 25 November a drone hit a bus on a road near Fedorivka in Chuhuiv district, Kharkiv Oblast, killing one person and injuring seven, according to oblast head Oleh Syniehubov. Fibre-optic control makes such drones immune to radio jamming. These are reports from Ukrainian officials and companies; casualty figures and the Air Force's interception figures cannot be independently verified. Euromaidan Press (23 November) puts strikes such as Korosten in a wider pattern: with Ukraine's airspace closed to civil flights since 2022, trains carry about 60% of its freight and 37% of its passengers, and Russia is now hunting that network. According to the outlet, Russia struck the railways about 1,200 times in 2025, more than in 2023 and 2024 combined, and Russian fire damaged 209 locomotives and 239 passenger carriages in 2025 and early 2026. It says the focus has shifted from depots, substations and bridges to trains carrying people. Examples include a jet-powered drone strike at Dnipro station on 29 October where a commuter train stood, which killed 15; a drone attack on a passenger train in Kharkiv Oblast in January that killed five; and a hit on the locomotive of a Kyiv-Warsaw train at Yahodyn, about 2 km from Poland, on 13 September. Drone and EW specialist Serhii Beskrestnov, now an adviser to the president, is quoted as saying Russian operators have repeatedly redirected hand-flown drones after spotting trains. Since 17 September Ukraine has run a two-level rail alert system: under yellow (drone threat) trains keep running and stop only when a threat nears a specific train; under red (missiles or mass attack) boarding stops and people take shelter. Passengers move at least 200 m away from the train, and one conductor reported up to 13 evacuations on a single trip.
