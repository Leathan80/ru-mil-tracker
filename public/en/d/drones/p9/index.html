Ukraine's 44th Artillery Brigade Claims Destruction of Three Russian Msta-B Howitzers, Strike on Troop Position

2026-07-22SINGLE SOURCE

According to a video release by Ukraine's 44th Separate Artillery Brigade, the unit claims to have destroyed three Russian 2A65 Msta-B 152mm towed howitzers and struck a Russian troop position during combat operations on the Huliaipole and Orikhiv axes in the Zaporizhzhia sector. The footage was reportedly released around 20 July 2026, per Defense Express. Per the brigade's account, drone operators first identified the concealed Russian artillery positions and relayed precise targeting data to artillery crews, who then engaged the howitzers — a combined reconnaissance-strike method pairing drone spotting with indirect artillery fire, rather than a direct drone strike. The brigade further states that Russian forces in the area are relying heavily on artillery while massing personnel in preparation for further combat operations, which it says explains the reported strike on an assembled Russian troop position; no casualty figures for that strike are given, and the video itself is not embedded in available reporting. This is a one-sided Ukrainian-sourced claim based solely on footage released by the brigade itself; there is no independent (e.g., Russian or third-party) confirmation of the destruction of the three howitzers or of the troop-position strike, so the information should be treated as single-source and unverified.

Ukraine Destroys Tor-M2 System, Hits Buk-M1 Units, and Five russian Radars in Coordinated Drone Strikes

2026-07-22SINGLE SOURCE

According to Defense Express, operators from Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces — specifically the 1st Separate Center working with the Deep Strike Center — carried out a coordinated overnight drone-strike campaign on 20 July 2026 against Russian air-defense and radar infrastructure. The report claims a Tor-M2 short-range air-defense system was destroyed near Yeysk in Russia's Krasnodar region, while a Buk-M1 medium-range SAM system was struck in the Russian-occupied part of Zaporizhzhia oblast. In the Krasnodar sector, five radar systems were reportedly hit as well: a Kasta-2E2, a Gamma-S1M, two Nebo-SV units, and a Nebo-U long-range surveillance radar. These radar types are used to detect aircraft, cruise missiles, and in some variants stealth targets, so their reported loss alongside the Tor-M2 and Buk-M1 would suggest an attempt to degrade layered Russian integrated air-defense coverage supporting operations in the south. The report does not specify which drone types were used in the strikes. This is a one-sided, unverified claim based on drone footage from a single Ukrainian source (Defense Express); there is no independent confirmation of the reported destruction or of the scale of damage to Russian air-defense coverage.

Ukrainian Drones Destroy Warehouses Near Moscow Despite Dozens of Pantsir Systems and Three S-400s

2026-07-22SINGLE SOURCE

Ukrainian long-range attack drones reportedly struck a cluster of logistics and fuel facilities in Moscow Oblast around 20 July 2026, hitting Wildberries' largest sorting center in Koledino, the 'South Gate' (Yuzhni Vorota) industrial park near Metkino, and an oil depot in Lvovskoye, all located 20-30 km south of central Moscow near Podolsk. Defense Express frames this as a follow-on wave after an earlier drone strike destroyed a separate Wildberries warehouse in Elektrostal, pointing to repeated Ukrainian targeting of Russian e-commerce and logistics infrastructure that supports the war effort. Citing OSINT analyst Mark Krutov, the outlet reports that roughly two dozen Pantsir gun-missile systems are deployed around Podolsk alone, with five positioned in the immediate vicinity of the struck sites, alongside three S-300/S-400 battalions nearby. Across the wider Moscow region, Russia has reportedly massed around 90 Pantsir systems and about 25 S-300/S-400 battalions in total — one of the densest air-defense belts in the country. Despite this concentration, the drones reportedly flew more than 750 km, crossing additional air-defense positions strung along Moscow's A108 outer ring road before reaching and destroying their targets. This account originates solely from the Ukrainian outlet Defense Express and has not been independently verified by Russian or third-party sources; Russian officials have not confirmed the strikes or the extent of the damage, and the cited air-defense figures cannot be independently checked at this time.

Real Cost of Ukraine's Hornet Drone Revealed: Far Cheaper Than Russia's Lancet, Now Also Hitting Aircraft

2026-07-21SINGLE SOURCE

According to Ukrainian defense outlet Defense Express, the real production/purchase cost of Ukraine's Hornet strike drone (also known as "Hornet One Way") has become known: $10,000-$11,000 per unit. Defense Express compares this to the Russian Lancet loitering munition: the Lancet X-52 variant reportedly costs Russia around $40,000 per unit, while the heavier X-51 variant costs about $68,000 — making the Hornet roughly four to seven times cheaper than its Russian counterpart. For further comparison, the article also cites the German HX-2 by Helsing, which reportedly costs Ukraine about €17,000 (roughly $19,500) per unit — likewise considerably more expensive than the Hornet. Per Defense Express, the drone is manufactured by US company Swift Beat LLC, weighs about 15 kg at takeoff, has a 2.2-metre wingspan and 1.4-metre length, and reaches a cruising speed of 100-120 km/h with a top speed of up to 200 km/h. The Hornet can carry different payloads — a 1.5 kg or 4 kg armor-piercing core, or a high-explosive fragmentation warhead — and, according to the article, features fairly advanced hardware and software for target detection, lock-on, and visual navigation, with an effective strike range of 100-150 km. The type has long been used against Russian fuel tankers behind the front line, and Defense Express now also credits it with strikes on aircraft — an apparent reference to the previously reported Omega Special Purpose Centre strike on a Su-24M bomber at Saky air base (see the separate entry on that incident), though this article itself does not cite any new, additional aircraft incidents. SINGLE SOURCE — this report comes solely from Defense Express (a Ukrainian outlet) and has not been independently confirmed. Notably, the manufacturer named here (Swift Beat LLC) and the stated price ($10,000-$11,000) differ from earlier, Militarnyi-sourced specifications in our own tracker, which attributed the Hornet to a company called Perennial Autonomy at a price of under €5,000; the 100-150 km range cited here also differs from the previously reported strike distance of over 185 km in the Saky attack. These discrepancies underline that the Hornet drone's specifications and provenance are not yet consistently established and should be treated with caution.

Стрелок под бронёй: что турецкий SARP меняет в войне дронов, а что нет

2026-07-19SINGLE SOURCE

SINGLE SOURCE — Russian publication (Topwar.ru). The article analyzes the Turkish ASELSAN SARP remote weapon station (RWS) and concludes it represents an evolutionary, not revolutionary, change in drone warfare. RWS technology predates quadcopters by decades — the concept traces back to remotely-operated turrets on the B-29 bomber — and modern iterations such as Norway's Kongsberg Protector and Israel's Typhoon became standard equipment in the 1990s, widely fielded in Iraq and Afghanistan. Technically, the SARP family spans variants of 80 to 400+ kg, in calibers from 5.56mm to 35mm, with a traverse speed of up to 60 degrees per second, an elevation range of -30° to +65°, and optics comprising a daylight camera, thermal imaging, laser rangefinder, ballistic computer, and auto-tracking; ammunition capacity runs up to 400 rounds (12.7mm) or 1,000-1,500 rounds (7.62mm). The author stresses that hitting small, maneuvering FPV drones with bursts remains difficult despite stabilization systems — an RWS improves crew survivability but does not guarantee a drone kill. For comparison, the article notes Ukraine already fields the Spanish Guardian 1.5 and its own “Tavria” RWS module, while Russia produces the “Arbalet-DM” and “Okhotnik” variants — with production, per the author, remaining fragmented across multiple design bureaus without consolidated manufacturing lines, limiting economies of scale. The piece closes with a political aside on Turkey's dual role as an arms supplier to both belligerents and self-styled mediator.
