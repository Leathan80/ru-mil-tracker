Oklahoma National Guard Trials Ukraine-Style Cage Armor on Convoy Vehicles

2026-09-15

During Exercise Thunderstruck 3.0, held September 12 at Camp Gruber Training Center, Oklahoma National Guard convoy vehicles were photographed and filmed fitted with welded wire-mesh cage screens against first-person-view (FPV) drones. This is the same basic principle behind the improvised 'cope cages' that Ukrainian forces began welding onto tanks and trucks as early as 2022, a practice that later spread to Russian forces: the cage is meant to detonate or deflect a drone's shaped-charge warhead before it reaches the vehicle's hull. It is thus a direct, visible instance of a Western unit adopting a defensive technique that originated on the Russia-Ukraine battlefield. Imagery shows a Humvee carrying a mesh screen mounted over the vehicle, and cargo trucks fully enclosed in boxy, welded cage frames. These vehicles moved as part of a tactical convoy that also included a marked ambulance, suggesting the Guard tested the added protection on logistics and medical-evacuation vehicles as well as combat platforms. Neither the Oklahoma National Guard's public release on the exercise nor its participating unit commanders specifically addressed the cage armor seen in the imagery, though the release did describe Thunderstruck as built around lessons from 'evolving threats,' where drones provide persistent surveillance, track troop movements, and influence how formations maneuver. Thunderstruck 3.0 folded friendly and simulated adversary drones into training alongside UH-60 Black Hawk operations, convoy movements, and medevac drills, under the Guard's Launched Effects Program, which has brought together military, industry, academic and government partners since 2022 to develop drone and counter-drone capabilities. Brig. Gen. Colby Wyatt described the goal as testing systems, tactics and procedures; retired Col. Shane Riley of RXR Worldwide spoke of using the drones for 'threat replication' to prepare units for what they might face in a future conflict. It remains unclear whether the cage screens reflect a formal equipment decision by the Oklahoma National Guard or a field-expedient addition specific to this exercise.

EU-Ukraine Drone Alliance Holds Inaugural Meeting, Uniting 18 Drone and Defense Firms

2026-09-14

The EU-Ukraine Drone Alliance held its inaugural meeting in Brussels in September 2026, months after being announced by European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen in Kyiv on 15 July 2026. It brings together 18 companies — nine Ukrainian and nine European — selected from roughly 400 applicants, marking the first time Ukrainian drone manufacturers and European arms firms have joined a single formal body. Ukrainian members include Tencore, Athlon Avia and Vyriy Industry; European participants include Germany's Quantum Systems, Italy's Fincantieri and Denmark's Terma. This distinguishes it from earlier EU-Ukraine drone cooperation announcements, which were typically bilateral funding pledges or procurement deals rather than a standing joint industrial body; first concrete results are expected by the end of 2026.

2026-09-12

On September 12, 2026, Ukraine's Navy announced that one of its uncrewed surface vessels (USVs), the Sargan-3000, had for the first time in history destroyed another uncrewed vessel in combat — a Russian unmanned boat in the Black Sea. The Sargan-3000 is a Ukrainian multipurpose naval strike drone that entered service in April 2026 after passing combat trials; it can reach speeds of 40-55 knots, has an operational range of up to 1,600 km, and can carry a payload of up to 450 kg. Ukraine combat experience shapes Redwire's new Penguin Mk3 drone

2026-09-12

US defense company Redwire has delivered more than 250 of its Penguin reconnaissance drones — a Group 3 fixed-wing UAS with roughly 14 hours of endurance and EO/IR sensors for target recognition — to Ukraine since 2022, largely through US aid packages. That deployment generated a large body of operational data, now feeding into a next-generation variant: the Penguin Mk3. The central lesson is that raw specifications — range, endurance, payload — do not determine whether a tactical drone stays useful. Having the right aircraft, in the right configuration, available at the right moment, along with maintenance speed, training quality and operator workload, proved just as decisive. Russia's heavily contested electromagnetic environment also forced Redwire to rethink navigation and communications assumptions: rather than building in one permanent EW-hardening fix, the company adopted a modular architecture that allows rapid adaptation as adversaries roll out new jamming or spoofing techniques. As a result, the Mk3 adds fully automated vertical takeoff and landing, multiple larger payload bays, winter-operating provisions, and deployability by a two-person crew. An undisclosed NATO country has since signed a multi-year contract for the Mk3, valued in the high eight figures and supported through Redwire's European maintenance and engineering operation. The development reflects a broader shift among Western manufacturers away from treating drones as finished products at delivery and toward continuous iteration — a direct response to the Ukrainian reality that drone tactics and countermeasures can become obsolete within weeks or even days.

France Tests Tethered Balloon to Protect Nuclear Rafale Airbase From Drone Strikes

2026-09-12

The French Air and Space Force trialled a tethered balloon (aerostat, 12 by 7 metres) as a drone-detection sensor platform at 113th Airbase Commandant Antoine de Saint-Exupéry in Saint-Dizier-Robinson from 31 August to 4 September 2026. Hovering at roughly 200 metres, the balloon carried an EO/IR camera paired with an AI-based threat-detection algorithm feeding real-time imagery to base security; the manufacturer is not named in available reporting. Saint-Dizier is strategically sensitive: it hosts France's Strategic Air Forces, including the nuclear-tasked Rafale squadrons 1/4 Gascogne and 2/4 La Fayette (ASMP-A/ASMPA-R stand-off missiles, roughly 600 km range). The technical rationale is straightforward: fixed ground-based counter-UAS radar struggles against small, slow, low-flying drones because clutter and terrain masking create a blind zone close to the ground and behind obstacles. A sensor elevated to 200 metres gets a look-down angle that covers exactly that gap, and can stay on station continuously for days at a fraction of the cost of a manned or unmanned aerial patrol. The concept itself is not new — similar optronic captive balloons were used to secure French forward operating bases in Afghanistan and the Sahel — but this marks its first application to a home-station airbase with a nuclear mission. The trial fits a broader European pattern: following the wave of drone sightings over airfields and military sites across 2024-2026 (including Volkel airbase in the Netherlands and the Île-Longue submarine base in France, alongside earlier incidents in Belgium, Germany and Denmark), NATO members are seeking cheap, persistent complements to expensive radar networks. The French Air Force explicitly cites Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb — where FPV drones struck Russian strategic bombers on the ground — as the scenario it wants to forestall. Results are still under evaluation, and any operational fielding will depend on the trial's conclusions.
