Russia's Dan-T munition blurs line between UAVs, cruise missiles — Rostec claims S-71M, S-71K and Dan-T jet drones form a new drone-missile class

2026-09-30

Russian state corporation Rostec, in comments to the Defence Ministry newspaper Krasnaya Zvezda (30 September) and to TASS, claims that three of its newest jet-powered strike drones — the S-71M, S-71K and Dan-T — form a new category between attack drones and cruise missiles. Rostec's reasoning: a classic cruise missile is already a fixed-wing UAV, but not every fixed-wing UAV is a cruise missile, and with these three types 'this line is being erased'. According to Rostec they offer the accuracy, manoeuvrability and range of cruise missiles at significantly lower cost, are already used against Ukraine, and are expected to become standard weapons of Russian frontline aviation, alongside glide-bomb kits. Rostec gave no prices, production numbers or performance figures. The S-71M and S-71K are designed for launch from fifth-generation fighters such as the Su-57 and, per Defence Blog, are sized for its internal weapons bay; Rosoboronexport showed them next to a Su-57E at the El Alamein airshow in Egypt in September. Citing Militarnyi, Defence Blog reports that Ukrainian military intelligence examined debris of both: the S-71K ('Kovyor') reportedly carries a 250-kg OFAB-250-270 high-explosive bomb as warhead and lacks terminal guidance, while the S-71M ('Monokhrom') contains an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AI computing module; some reports swap the two designations. Rostec says the smaller Dan-T has been confirmed in hundreds of launches in the 'special military operation' zone and can be fired from aircraft and helicopters, including foreign ones, which points to export ambitions. The Dan-T's origin is disputed: some analysts call it the export name of Kronshtadt's S8000 Banderol small jet-powered cruise missile, Ukrainian experts a strike version of the Dan-M jet target drone. Ukrainian estimates (via The New Voice of Ukraine) put its range at about 500 km with a 135-kg warhead, and say Moscow wants it built entirely from Russian components — after Ukrainian intelligence found a Chinese jet engine, a Japanese battery and mostly US electronics in the Banderol, which has appeared since spring 2025, launched from Su-30/34s, Orion drones and Mi-28 helicopters. Caveat: the core claims come from Rostec via Russian state media and are not independently verified; the technical details are Ukrainian intelligence assessments relayed by media.

Poland switches to sensor-based identification for shooting down aerial objects during Russian strikes on Ukraine, as Sweden returns Gripens to guard the Rzeszów aid hub

2026-09-30

Poland put simplified procedures for detecting and shooting down foreign objects in its airspace into force on 1 October, Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz announced, according to Ukrainska Pravda citing European Pravda and Gazeta Prawna. Deputy Defence Minister Cezary Tomczyk explained on 28 September that the change mainly concerns how Polish pilots on airspace-protection missions during Russian strikes on Ukraine identify targets: the peacetime requirement to identify a target visually before engaging it is replaced by dynamic, sensor-based identification. A pilot or their commander can now decide to shoot down an unidentified object on the basis of radar and other sensor data, or on information supplied by Ukraine. The change follows a series of Russian strikes close to the Polish border. On 17 September a Russian jet-powered strike drone came down about 4 km inside Ukraine near the Dorohusk-Yahodyn border crossing in Volyn Oblast during an attack on western Ukraine; Prime Minister Donald Tusk spoke of 'a very powerful explosion' and said the target was probably a petrol station. Polish fighters were scrambled as the drone rapidly approached Polish airspace, and Poland's Operational Command said it most likely hit its target or was shot down on the Ukrainian side. Tusk called it an exceptional day for security, after similar incidents with Russian drones near Polish territory on 15-16 September. In parallel, Swedish Defence Minister Pål Jonson announced on 30 September that Sweden will rejoin NATO's integrated air and missile defence over Poland from October with JAS 39 Gripen fighters, chiefly to guard the logistics hub around Rzeszów-Jasionka in south-eastern Poland, through which much Western aid for Ukraine passes. Most aircraft will operate from bases in Sweden with only a small forward presence in Poland; the number of jets and the duration were not disclosed. Jonson cited 'repeated violations of NATO airspace' as the reason for high readiness on the eastern flank. Swedish Gripens guarded the same hub before, from May 2025 (six Gripen C and about 120 personnel of the Blekinge Wing), after a first deployment of six Gripens to Malbork in March 2025.

EU sanctioned Russia's Starlink rival Rassvet but not its manufacturer Bureau 1440; Ukraine now urges the US to sanction the companies behind it before it becomes operational

2026-09-30

According to Euromaidan Press, the EU's 21st sanctions package (23 July 2026) targeted Rassvet, Russia's low-Earth-orbit satellite constellation built as a replacement for SpaceX Starlink access after it was cut off, but did not list Bureau 1440, the private company assembling the satellites, or its CEO. Bureau 1440 is already on Ukraine's sanctions list but, per the article, remains outside every Western sanctions regime. The US research group C4ADS called designating the manufacturer the single most visible and effective step available; Viktoriia Vyshnivska of Ukraine's NAKO cautioned against expecting dramatic results, but said sanctions would raise sourcing costs for Western boards and components, many of which already face EU export controls. Ukraine is now also pressing Washington. Denys Sienik, deputy chief of mission at the Ukrainian embassy in Washington, told Reuters (as reported by Ukrainska Pravda on 1 October) that Russia is rapidly expanding Rassvet and that the embassy will give the US government and Congress detailed information on the companies and individuals facilitating the project. "If they launch Rassvet by the end of 2026, they will be able to hit Ukrainian targets real time on our territory," he said, adding that leaving these entities unsanctioned would make the programme possible and would be "a game-changer". According to the report, Alexei Shelobkov, CEO of developer Iks Holding, said in June that operational deployment is planned for 2027; the first batch of 16 satellites was launched in March, with an eventual constellation of 900 planned in the coming years (SpaceX operates more than 10,000 Starlink satellites). The military concern on both sides is the same: Ukrainian technology adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov warns that Rassvet would let Russian forces fly long-range strike drones under real-time satellite control over Ukrainian territory, and Euromaidan Press notes it could let Russian drones operate through Ukrainian jamming. Ukraine itself cannot use Starlink for real-time UAV guidance inside Russia because of geofencing. Ukrainian officials estimate sanctions could slow development by about a year, while Russia reportedly needs five to eight years to complete the constellation. Timelines and threat assessments are claims by Ukrainian officials, Euromaidan Press and the cited experts, not independently verified.

Putin says Ukraine has 'no antidote' to jet-powered Gerans as Russia fields Geran-3/4/5; 1020th AA Regiment downs a Geran-5 at 425 km/h

2026-09-29

Speaking at the Cultural Forum, Vladimir Putin claimed Ukraine was seeking an 'air ceasefire' because it cannot cope with Russia's new jet-powered Geran drones: 'At least for now, they have no way to counter them — no antidote.' Meduza sets out the family behind the claim. The Geran-3 (first jet variant; Meduza dates it to early 2025, TWZ to early 2024) roughly doubled speed to 300–350 km/h with a Chinese Telefly JT80 turbojet, at the cost of range and payload. The delta-wing Geran-4, which TWZ says appeared in May 2026, uses a Telefly TF-TJ2000A with about 2.5 times the JT80's thrust; TWZ gives 450–500 km/h, altitudes up to 8 km and a documented flight of over 700 km (Crimea to Lutsk). The conventionally laid-out Geran-5 uses the same engine, can be launched from aircraft and helicopters as well as from the ground, and costs an estimated $100,000+, more than twice a Geran-2 (Meduza). HUR data cited by Euromaidan Press give the Geran-5 a 90 kg warhead, 450–600 km/h cruise speed, 950 km range and 6 km ceiling. Ukrainian military intelligence estimates Russian output at about 3,000 Geran-4/5 a month. TWZ describes how guidance has evolved. Most drones still fly autonomously to preset coordinates, but some carry GSM modems with SIM cards (Ukrainian, Russian, Belarusian and EU) and Chinese mesh modems (about 300 km range) with tail-mounted relay antennas, so that, per RFE/RL reporting, an operator in Bryansk, Oryol or Crimea can steer the drone and receive video for real-time battle damage assessment; a Geran-5 variant reportedly carries a DSMAC-style terrain-image navigation module (Polkovnyk GSh Telegram channel). Wreckage has included Texas Instruments circuit boards. Tactics include high-altitude flight to stay above cheap interceptors and, per one open-source analyst, a Geran-4 that cuts its engine and glides slowly before diving on its target. In the 28 September night attack, according to the Ukrainian Air Force, 79 of 165 drones launched were jet-powered; 112 were downed or suppressed and hits were recorded at 14 locations. Ukraine is already shooting them down, but at a price. A crew from the 1020th Anti-Aircraft Missile and Artillery Regiment (callsign Yastrub) downed a Geran-5 over Chernihiv Oblast at 425 km/h and above 3 km with a newly delivered interceptor, according to the Serhiy Prytula Foundation, which published the footage; TWZ attributes the 1020th's Geran-5 kill to Wild Hornets' STING S, whose maker claims about a dozen Geran-4s and several Geran-5s in recent days (not independently verified). Zelensky said at the UN General Assembly that four new jet-powered interceptor drones had been successfully tested, and on 26 September that Ukraine intercepts about 55% of jet drones (versus over 90% of older models). Meduza notes that the more than ten electric interceptor models (under $10,000, about four per kill) are nearly powerless against the jets; the alternatives are $70,000–100,000+ missiles or APKWS II rockets (up to $20,000) fired only by F-16s and fewer than 20 Vampire systems. Scaling up faster interceptors could add hundreds of millions or even billions of dollars a year to a budget that, per Zelensky, already has a $27 billion gap in 2026.
