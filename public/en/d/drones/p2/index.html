Drones strike Azot chemical plant in Novomoskovsk, Tula region, a EuroChem producer of nitric acid and ammonium nitrate; fire reported, ammonia unit possibly hit

2026-10-28

Drones attacked the Azot chemical plant in Novomoskovsk, Tula region, on 28 October 2026, and a fire broke out at the facility, according to Militarnyi. The monitoring channel Exilenova+ first reported the attack on Telegram. Based on publicly released footage, Militarnyi assessed that the AM-70 ammonia production unit may have been hit. The article cites no Russian official statement, damage assessment or casualty figures. According to OSINT Varta, Novomoskovsk Azot is one of Russia's largest chemical enterprises and part of the EuroChem Group. It produces ammonia, ammonium nitrate, urea, methanol and nitric acid, products with civilian uses that can also go into explosives and ammunition. Between February 2022 and September 2024 the plant supplied nearly 5,000 tonnes of nitric acid to the Sverdlov Plant in Dzerzhinsk (Nizhny Novgorod region), a producer of the military explosives RDX and HMX. This is not the first attack on the site: on 14 June 2026 drones reportedly destroyed a production workshop and damaged ammonia units, followed by another attack on 26 June. It is a different plant from the Azot facility in Berezniki, Perm Krai, struck on 23 October. The strike fits Ukraine's long-range campaign against Russian chemical plants that feed explosives production.

Ukraine's General Staff says it shot down nearly 60% of Russian jet-powered Geran-4 drones over the past three months

2026-10-27

In an update on its Telegram channel on 27 October 2026, Ukraine's General Staff said the shoot-down rate of Russia's jet-powered Geran drones is steadily rising. Over the past three months Ukrainian air defence has intercepted nearly 60% of the Geran-4 jet drones launched, and in some individual attacks the rate reached 80%. Russia has increasingly been using these faster jet-powered variants alongside the propeller-driven Shahed/Geran-2 type. Their speed makes them harder to counter. The General Staff gave no interception rate for the more advanced Geran-5 jet drone. According to the General Staff, several means are proving effective. Tactical aviation (F-16s, French Mirage fighters and MiG-29s) is used to hunt the jet drones. Short-range air defence is also being strengthened: Gepard and Shilka anti-aircraft guns, the newer Skynex, Skyranger and Terrahawk Paladin systems, interceptor drones, and mobile fire groups drawn from several branches of the Defence Forces. These mobile fire groups carry machine guns and also MANPADS, which the General Staff says troops have used successfully against jet drones. Ukraine is testing cheaper interceptor-drone models, some of which have reportedly already downed dozens of jet-powered Gerans. No models or exact figures were given. The stated aim is to raise interception rates while cutting the cost of defence, because Ukraine is short of guided surface-to-air missiles and wants to keep them for targets that require them. Caveat: all figures come from Ukraine's General Staff, a party to the conflict. They have not been independently verified, and the absolute number of Geran-4 launches behind the percentages was not disclosed.

In March 2026 the Cabinet widened the terms to better protect critical infrastructure. Prime Minister Yuliia Svyrydenko stressed that all such decisions remain under military control. According to Euromaidan Press, the units use interceptor drones and remotely operated heavy machine guns. Crews from six companies are flying combat missions, and the groups had downed more than 60 aerial targets, mostly drones, by 14 October, according to the Defence Ministry. Point defence of individual sites is now partly being handed to the companies that own them, under military command. Little public information exists on how these units operate or are funded, and Nova Post's own claim is unquantified.

Largely intact new Russian small cruise missile, provisionally a Geran-5 derivative ('Geran-5 K5-K'), found in a field in Odesa region

2026-10-27

Photos circulating on 27 October show a Russian small cruise missile that came down in an open field in Ukraine's Odesa region during an attack and stayed relatively intact. Militarnyi says the photos were posted by the local Telegram channel ODESSA INFO LIVE and that the light damage lets Ukrainian specialists study the design in detail. Defence Blog identifies the weapon as the upgraded Geran-5 'K5-K' and says the serial number ANS-3031 is visible on the airframe. Militarnyi headlines it as a missile 'of unknown type'. It is not known whether the missile was shot down, jammed by electronic warfare or failed on its own, and Ukrainian officials have not commented. The photos show a compact cruise missile with a box-shaped fuselage (rectangular cross-section, rounded edges, flat sides), swept wings, a V-shaped tail with offset fins, and a small turbojet whose nozzle sits in the tail and whose air intakes are on the fuselage sides. Militarnyi says the dark composite skin and flat surfaces may be meant to reduce radar cross-section and simplify production. Serhiy 'Flash' Beskrestnov, adviser to Ukraine's president on defence technology, published the first photos of this new missile type on 22 October and called it the Geran-5 K5-K. He named optical navigation and greater range, speed and flight altitude as its distinctive features. Militarnyi points out that its exterior differs significantly from the Geran-5 K5-K variant already known. That variant, used sporadically since late September, has an internal engine, smaller swept or triangular wings and a tail without wingtips. The new airframe also has a diamond-shaped wing and a V-tail. The type designation is therefore still open: either a new K5-K configuration or a separate Geran-5 derivative. According to Militarnyi's Ukrainian military sources and Flash, the new Geran-5 variants use autonomous optical navigation that matches images of the ground to stored data. This lets them correct course without satellite navigation and makes jamming largely ineffective. Developers reportedly aim for about 900 km/h, which would put them largely beyond the reach of interceptor drones. No measured data exist yet. For comparison, Ukrainian intelligence (HUR) gives the baseline Geran-5, first used in January 2026, as about 6.5 m long with a 3.2 m wingspan, up to 850 kg take-off weight, a roughly 90 kg warhead, a Chinese Telefly turbojet, 450–600 km/h cruising speed and a range of about 950–1,000 km. HUR also says around 3,000 Geran-4/5 are produced monthly at Alabuga. These are Ukrainian state and intelligence claims; the identification of the Odesa missile is not independently verified.
