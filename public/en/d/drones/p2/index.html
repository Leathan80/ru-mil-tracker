Ukraine says Russia is developing 800 km/h jet drones, as jet-powered Gerans are already shot down at only about 55 percent versus over 90 percent for propeller Shaheds

2026-10-11

Russia is developing jet-powered attack drones that fly at 800 km/h, and Ukraine is already looking for ways to counter them, Yevhenii Khmara, identified by Euromaidan Press as Ukraine's defence minister, told Glavcom. The ministry has asked manufacturers for proposals and prototypes. Russia's current jet-powered Gerans (among them the Geran-3, one of which was recovered intact in Ukraine in September 2025) already fly several times faster than propeller-driven Shahed types and are described as the main problem for Ukrainian air defence: Ukraine reportedly downs more than 90% of the propeller drones but only about 55% of the jet versions. Khmara calls aviation one of the most effective counters, saying effectiveness is rising thanks to pilot skill and better coordination and that fewer munitions are now spent per aerial target. He describes a layered system ranging from surface-to-air and shoulder-launched missiles to gun turrets, electronic warfare, interceptor-drone crews and short-range air defence, with aircraft such as the Mirage and F-16 at the top. He cited one engagement in which five Bars interceptors destroyed five aerial targets, said heavy-calibre turrets already number in the hundreds, and said longer-range systems are in development. For the next generation, the ministry says it is building up interceptor drones, aviation and munitions, missile and artillery systems, turrets and EW equipment, with reserves stocked in advance. The interception rates and combat results are Ukrainian government figures and have not been independently verified; the article gives no technical details on the Russian 800 km/h design.

Ukraine's drone force claims it has killed or wounded more than 100,000 Russian troops this year, as drones account for an estimated 96% of Russian battlefield losses and Kyiv's FPV orders already exceed all of 2025 by 54%.

2026-10-11

Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces (USF) said on Telegram that its units have killed or wounded more than 100,000 Russian troops since the start of 2026, and more than 150,000 since the force was created on 11 June 2025. The force says it counts only hits that its own pilots confirm on video in the military's Delta system. USF commander Robert "Madyar" Brovdi said that in the first five days of October his pilots killed or wounded 2,699 Russian soldiers, 39.7% more than in the same period of September, and linked the increase to Russia's ongoing assaults on the fortress-belt cities of Donbas. Euromaidan Press adds that drones accounted for about 96% of Russia's battlefield casualties earlier this year, without naming the underlying source in the article. In July, Ukraine's Defence Ministry reported that all drone units together had carried out more than 1 million verified strikes since January, killing or seriously wounding about 193,500 Russian troops, with monthly strikes roughly doubling from about 105,800 in January to more than 200,000 in June. These are Ukrainian claims that have not been independently verified. The figures come alongside a sharp rise in Ukrainian drone procurement. According to the Defence Ministry, the Defence Procurement Agency has contracted about 5.5 times as many drones of all types in recent months as in the same period of 2025, roughly 300% more over the year as a whole, and some $8 billion worth in the first half of 2026 alone, double the year before. The steepest growth is in FPV drones: contracts in 2026 already exceed the total for all of 2025 by 54%, and deliveries have risen about 3.7-fold. Much of the purchasing now runs through the DOT-Chain Defence marketplace, where units select what they need; the ministry says average delivery time for in-stock items has fallen to nine days. Taken together, the reporting indicates that Russian personnel losses at the front are now overwhelmingly inflicted by cheap drones rather than by artillery or direct fire, and that Kyiv is scaling up precisely that capability. Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara frames this as arming the force at every level: deep-strike drones for targets inside Russia, and middle-strike drones to hunt Russian drone crews, logistics and command posts while keeping Ukrainian soldiers further from the fighting. The loss figures and percentage shares come from Ukrainian authorities and a Ukrainian outlet, and cannot be checked against Russian data.

Third Yak-130M prototype flies as Russia turns its jet trainer into a radar-equipped light combat aircraft

2026-10-11

The third prototype of the Yak-130M, an upgraded version of the Yak-130 jet trainer reworked to carry radar and guided weapons, has made its first flight from the Irkutsk Aviation Plant, according to Russia's United Aircraft Corporation (UAC). The aircraft, side number 137, was flown by Yakovlev test pilots Oleg Mutovin and Alexander Guskov; UAC said all systems worked normally but did not give the date of the flight. According to UAC, the third test aircraft should allow trials to finish on schedule and deliveries to begin in 2028; it did not name a first customer or planned numbers. The Yak-130M keeps the airframe and engines of the two-seat subsonic trainer but adds equipment meant to turn it into a light combat aircraft: the BRLS-130R radar (described in Russian sources as an AESA), the KSS-130 communications suite, a SOLT-130K pod combining a camera, thermal imager and laser designator, and the President-S130 self-protection system in a pod. UAC says the jet can use a wider range of guided and unguided weapons by day and night and in all weather. When the first prototype was unveiled in 2025, Rostec pitched the aircraft partly as a drone hunter, able to intercept UAVs and carry air-to-air missiles as well as satellite- and laser-guided bombs. Yakovlev first showed the Yak-130M as a mock-up at Army-2024, framing the upgrade as a way to make the aircraft more competitive for export and announcing three test aircraft. The first prototype made its maiden flight on 25 June 2026 and a second was rolled out in October 2025. The only export customer named so far is Vietnam: in July, the head of the Irkutsk Aviation Plant said during a visit by Vladimir Putin that a contract was being signed for 18 Yak-130Ms. These are manufacturer claims that have not been independently verified.

Why Does russia Need DF-3 Gadyuka Jet-Powered Interceptor Drone With 800 km Range, How Could It Actually Be Used? (Defense Express)

2026-10-11SINGLE SOURCE

The DF-3 Viper (Russian: Gadyuka) is a concept for a jet-powered interceptor drone with a stated range of 800 km and a total mass of 240 kg, presented by the Russian company DOFA-Tek alongside its other jet-powered drones. These include the DF-1 Roscherk interceptor (250 km range, up to 500 km/h, 15 kg total mass including a 1.5 kg warhead), which according to the company has entered factory testing with a next round of tests scheduled for next month, and the DF-5 Iskra strike drone (1,200 km range, up to 850 km/h, 20 kg warhead, 120 kg total mass). On DOFA-Tek's website both the DF-3 and the DF-5 are labelled "technical proposals", i.e. at an early stage of development; the company has not specified the DF-3's payload capacity or type, and the only concept image published so far resembles the Italian Aeritalia G.91 light fighter-bomber. Defense Express argues that an 800 km range makes little sense for a conventional interceptor: more endurance means more fuel, a larger airframe and higher cost, which could make intercepting cheap UAVs economically unviable. The 240 kg mass, double that of the longer-ranged DF-5, points to a large payload, yet a warhead of tens of kilograms would be excessive for a normal interceptor drone. The outlet therefore hypothesises that the DF-3 may be intended as a reusable 'unmanned fighter' carrying smaller interceptor drones, electronic warfare equipment or even machine guns, engaging several targets across a whole region in a single sortie before returning to its launch point, which would explain the range. It stresses that these are unconfirmed hypotheses until more details emerge. Single source: this is a Ukrainian military-affairs outlet (Defense Express) report that has not been independently verified.

Ukraine's SBU Alpha unit releases footage of a drone destroying a Russian Pantsir air-defence system mounted on an elevated 'Flakturm' tower.

2026-10-10

A Ukrainian drone has destroyed a Russian Pantsir air-defence system on top of a so-called Flakturm, one of the purpose-built towers Russia has been erecting to counter Ukrainian drones, according to footage released by the Alpha special operations unit of the Security Service of Ukraine (SBU) and reported by Defence Blog. The SBU did not say where or when the strike took place, which drone was used, which Pantsir variant was hit, or whether the tower itself was also destroyed. The claim has not been independently verified. The towers are nicknamed after the concrete anti-aircraft towers Nazi Germany built in Berlin, Hamburg and Vienna. Russia's use of them became public in September 2023, when state channel Russia-24 showed Pantsirs on metal towers around Moscow. According to Defence Blog, lifting the system several metres gives its radar a clearer view over trees and terrain, so it can spot small, low-flying drones earlier and at longer range. The tradeoff is that a tower cannot move: once its location is known, drone units can plan a precision strike against a target that will still be there when the drone arrives. The Pantsir is a truck-mounted short-range system combining a radar, surface-to-air missiles and twin 30 mm cannons, and is one of Russia's main defences against drones and cruise missiles. SBU head Vasyl Malyuk said in late 2025 that Ukraine had destroyed 48% of Russia's Pantsir systems that year, while Russia builds about 30 per year. Defence Blog places the strike in a series of October attacks on Russian air defences: Ukraine's General Staff reported a hit on a Pantsir-S1 near Kudepsta (Krasnodar region) on 8-9 October, and footage showed FP-2 strike drones destroying a Pantsir-S2 earlier in the month.

Downed Geran-4 drones carry a 32-element, likely Alabuga-made anti-jam GNSS antenna, while Geran-4 and Geran-5 also get camera-based terrain navigation

2026-10-10

A 32-element CRPA (Controlled Radiation Pattern Antenna) has been found in a Geran-4 strike drone shot down over Ukraine, Militarnyi reports, citing an analysis by The Insider of photos and videos of wreckage; the photos, taken in September 2026, are credited to Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov. The unit is effectively two 16-element antennas in one housing: 16 elements work in the GPS L1 band (about 1,575 MHz) and 16 in the L2 band (about 1,227 MHz). A CRPA protects satellite-navigation reception against jamming; the more elements, the more separate jamming sources it can null. Cheaper L1-only versions are the norm on drones and missiles, so a dual-band 32-element design marks a step up in jam resistance. The antenna closely resembles products of the Chinese firm Harxon but shows signs of local manufacture, including a rough metal casing stamped "ALB". Militarnyi assesses it was likely made at the Alabuga plant in Tatarstan, where Geran drones are produced, and that Russia is moving towards at least kit (screwdriver) assembly of such boards there. Harxon was earlier reported as Alabuga's main supplier of multi-element antennas; it was subsequently sanctioned and says it dismissed staff who had contact with Russia. Ukraine had already seized a 32-element CRPA on a Russian weapon system in July. The sources do not describe the antenna fit of the Geran-2 or Geran-3. Separately, Beskrestnov, an adviser to the Ukrainian president on defence technology, wrote on Telegram that Russia is fitting drones with image-based navigation: an onboard camera compares what it sees with stored terrain imagery, which in basic versions can be public satellite or ground imagery such as Google's. Because this does not rely on satellite signals, it is hard to jam. According to him, Russian forces already use it on Molniya, V2U and Lancet drones, and similar systems were recently found on the Geran-4 and Geran-5, which use a flight controller called "Susanin". He expects wider use in 2027, including on medium- and long-range strike drones and cheap cruise missiles, which would push Ukraine towards physical interception rather than jamming. These are claims by a Ukrainian official and have not been independently verified.

On 7-8 October a strike set fire to the site in Sasovo, Ryazan Oblast, which had to be shut down completely; on 8-9 October several modules of the Kaluga data centre in the Grabtsevo industrial park, which Yandex calls its largest, were knocked out; and on the night of 10-11 October the Vladimir site in the Energetik district (expanded in March 2026 with a 40 MW building for 2,800 server racks) was shut down entirely after power disruptions in Yandex Cloud's ru-central1-a zone. Meduza describes the result as a crisis for Russia's internet that will likely take months to repair. Yandex Go, Yandex Eats, Yandex Pay, the Alice assistant, Yandex Music, Telemost and Yandex Cloud went down, along with clients storing data on Yandex infrastructure such as CIAN, Sports.ru, Magnit, Vkusno i Tochka, Russian Railways' websites and MegaFon. According to BBC News Russian, Yandex regularly drilled losing one data centre and shifting load to the others, but two sites being shut down entirely made that much harder; Yandex Cloud went into emergency mode after the third strike, and analyst Eldar Murtazin told Fontanka the company had "run out of spare capacity". Yandex Pay, which replicates transaction data across several data centres simultaneously, had to rebuild its infrastructure to keep that principle; on 13 October fintech head Dmitry Pirogov said transfers and payments had been restored for some users but the service remained unstable. An industry source told Kommersant that replacement equipment can take up to six months to arrive. ComNews CEO Leonid Konik told Vedomosti that a full day of total outage would cost Yandex about 4 billion roubles; actual losses are "much lower", he said, but combined losses across companies run to billions of roubles per day. On 11 October Yandex added drone and missile attacks as force majeure to its user agreement. Russian data-localisation laws (the Yarovaya laws and personal-data law) limit options for moving data abroad; Yandex advised customers to arrange backup capacity with Selectel, K2Cloud or VK Cloud. Three large Yandex sites, a second one in Vladimir Oblast and those in Ivanteyevka and Mytishchi near Moscow, have not been hit. The strikes are already reshaping Russian business behaviour. Deputy Digital Development Minister Yevgeny Filatov urged companies on 6 October to move computing capacity "beyond the Urals", and on 12 October minister Maksut Shadayev said his ministry was working with the Defence Ministry on protection; according to Meduza, VK, MegaFon and Rosatom are buying protective frames, netting, drone detectors and shelters. According to BBC Russian, demand for containerised mobile data centres has risen since late summer; suppliers include Rostelecom's RTK-TsOD and Yadro (X Holding), whose "Borey" container costs 18-25 million roubles without servers, per procurement records. X Holding, which also supplies equipment for Russia's TSPU internet-filtering system, has as deputy CEO Boris Korolev, son of FSB First Deputy Director Sergey Korolev. The same pressure shows in logistics: according to The Moscow Times, citing Polis.online, Rental Pro and Vedomosti, average warehouse insurance rates in Q3 2026 rose 13-20 times year on year to about 2% of property value, and at Renaissance Insurance a Class A warehouse policy rose 37.5-fold between June and September, after Ukrainian strikes on Wildberries warehouses (since mid-July) and Ozon facilities (since late August); Data Insight estimates Wildberries' losses at 147-280 billion roubles and Ozon's at about 100 billion. Meduza notes that Ukraine has moved from marketplace warehouses to data centres and that other companies' data centres could be next, although Kyiv has not described this as a campaign.
