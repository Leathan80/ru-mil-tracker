Ukrainian manufacturer Ukrspetsdronprom unveils the Chomga, a $5,000–7,000 fixed-wing carrier drone for FPV strike drones and interceptors

2026-11-04

Ukrainian drone maker Ukrspetsdronprom has unveiled the RP7 Chomga, a conventional fixed-wing UAV intended as a modular base platform for reconnaissance, strike and special missions, a company representative told Ukrainska Pravda at the Iron Halloween Party 2026 event. The complete system costs between US$5,000 and US$7,000 depending on the communications fit: either the company's own SkyLoki analogue radio link, described as jam-resistant, or a Starlink terminal with an LTE-modem control system. The company says it can be repaired in the field with a cordless drill, a screwdriver and a rivet gun. It needs a prepared strip of up to 50 m, carries up to 15 kg, cruises at 75 km/h (maximum 110 km/h), flies up to 1,500 m and stays aloft for up to 90 minutes. In the carrier configuration the Chomga carries up to three RP7-10 FPV strike drones with 1 kg warheads (or two with larger batteries); the M-Fly variant adds a laser designator with a range of up to 3 km to guide those FPV drones, which have optical seekers for autonomous terminal attack. An air-defence configuration carries three RP7-Zenit interceptor drones (0.4 kg warhead, up to 250 km/h or 320 km/h in boost mode, 20 minutes endurance) linked to the SineLink system. As a bomber it can carry 15 kg out to 60 km and return; as a one-way attack drone with a 15 kg warhead its range is 100–125 km. A lighter Chomga Light carries 5 kg over up to 80 km. The developers rejected using the Chomga as a permanent relay because of its vulnerability at low speed, opting instead for a tethered balloon repeater (RP7-15-RLY SkyLoki Ultra+) with up to 10 hours of endurance. According to the manufacturer, the aircraft is ready for deployment and talks are under way with military units on combat trials; SkyLoki modules were earlier tested against EW together with units of the 10th Army Corps. All specifications are the manufacturer's own and have not been independently verified.

Russia fits drones and missiles with larger multi-element Kometa satellite-navigation antennas to beat Ukrainian jamming; Shaheds with 32-element arrays reported over Kyiv

Russia is equipping its drones and missiles with larger, jam-resistant satellite-navigation antennas, which Ukrainian officials say makes it harder for Ukrainian electronic countermeasures to disrupt their navigation, Defense News reports. The modules in question are the Kometa navigation units made by VNIIR-Progress, which use arrays of small patch antennas. According to Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR), Kometa has been found on Geran drones, glide-bomb kits, Iskander-M and Kinzhal missiles and the new Filin cruise missile. HUR said on 19 October that Russia already uses 16-element arrays at scale and is setting up serial production of 20-element arrays, and that Russian defence firms ordered more than 100,000 Kometa modules this year for cruise and ballistic missiles, strike drones and guided bombs. Jam resistance depends on the number and layout of the elements and on the receiver chip's algorithm for separating satellite signals from interference. Serhii 'Flash' Beskrestnov, an electronic-warfare specialist and adviser to President Zelensky, said in an October Telegram post that Shaheds with 32-element antennas have been found attacking Kyiv, arguing that more elements allow more jamming sources to be suppressed. In his view Russia holds the advantage, because adding elements to an antenna is easier than Ukraine adding electronic-warfare sites; drones that get through leave more targets for costlier interceptors such as interceptor drones, jets and missiles. Ukrainian practitioners describe workarounds. Alkhimik, commander of Ukraine's Night Watch unit, bought the 32-element antenna of a crashed Shahed on the secondary market (as reported by Militarnyi in July) and found it consisted of two 16-element arrays covering different satellite bands; he said two separate jamming systems, one for L1 and one for L2/L5, could defeat it. Anatoliy Khrapchynskyi of Fly Group Ukraine told Reuters in September that spoofing works: networked devices feed drones false coordinates so the autopilot gradually steers them away from cities.
