Night of 22–23 October: nearly half of 140 downed Russian drones were jet-powered Shaheds, Ukraine's Air Force says; Sumy university hit

2026-10-23

Russia launched 176 drones against Ukraine on the night of 22–23 October, according to Ukraine's Air Force as reported by Ukrainska Pravda. As of 08:00, Ukrainian air defence had destroyed or jammed 140 of them, described as Shahed, Gerbera and other types. Notably, the Air Force stated that 68 of the 140 neutralised drones were jet-powered Shaheds – close to half of the total. Hits were recorded at 19 locations and debris from downed drones fell at 13 locations; 10 drones were still in Ukrainian airspace at 08:00 and the attack was ongoing. All figures are claims by the Ukrainian Air Force and have not been independently verified; the report does not give launch areas. One of the main targets was the city of Sumy. Oblast governor Oleh Hryhorov said Russian forces deliberately struck civilian infrastructure in three parts of the city, with damage also in a residential area of the Kovpakivskyi district; he acknowledged that "some of the enemy's drones reached their targets". The city military administration said two petrol-station employees (a 39-year-old woman and a 54-year-old man) suffered minor injuries; fires were extinguished by the State Emergency Service. Around 02:00 a drone strike damaged Sumy State Pedagogical University, according to Suspilne Sumy: four buildings were affected, the main building worst, along with a hall of residence, and about 220 windows were blown out. No students or staff were hurt because staff on site reached shelter in time; in-person classes in university buildings are suspended. Ukrainska Pravda notes that Russian strikes on 20–21 October also damaged university buildings in Kremenchuk, Uman and Kyiv.

Danish firm MyDefence says about 2,000 of its Wingman passive drone detectors are in service with Ukrainian forces

2026-10-22

The Danish company MyDefence has supplied Ukraine's Defence Forces with about 2,000 Wingman drone detectors, Militarnyi reports. "There are currently about 2,000 of these devices in Ukraine," said Ulrich Harde, the company's commercial director, describing Wingman as a combat-proven product. MyDefence also cooperates with Ukraine in other counter-drone areas; no delivery timeline or receiving units were given. Wingman is a compact, wearable infantry detector that works passively, so the operator does not reveal his position through his own emissions. It covers the 2.4, 5.2 and 5.8 GHz bands, or 200 MHz to 6 GHz with the XF broadband antenna, detects both drones and their remote controls, and gives direction-finding to an accuracy of up to 30 degrees. A library of drone signals supports classification and reduces false alarms; the device gives audible and other alarms, is IP67 waterproof and receives regular updates. Two versions exist: the Wingman 103 with an external battery for longer endurance, and the lighter Wingman 105 with a built-in battery. The specifications come from the manufacturer. RF-based detectors of this type cannot detect drones that emit no radio signal, such as fibre-optic-controlled FPVs, which Russia uses widely.

HUR: Russia keeps missile stocks steady at up to 700 ballistic and several thousand cruise missiles, produces 200+ missiles a month, and has ordered over 100,000 Geran-type drones and decoys for 2026

2026-10-22

According to figures Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR) gave the Ukrainian outlet NV, published on 20 October 2026 and reported by Euromaidan Press, Russia holds up to 700 ballistic missiles and several thousand cruise missiles in reserve, roughly the same level HUR reported in July despite the autumn mass strikes. Russia can produce more than 200 missiles a month, 110 of them ballistic. About 115 of the ballistic missiles in stock are Iskander-Ms, produced at up to 65 a month, and a modernised Iskander with a projected range of 550–700 km is in development. Russia also holds up to 400 S-300PM/S-400 air-defence missiles adapted for ground strikes, making about 40 a month. Kinzhal production has reportedly been paused over poor accuracy, with the capacity shifted to conventional ballistic missiles. For cruise missiles, HUR says Russia plans more than 80 Kh-101s a month and is exceeding that, although defects in release from aircraft have sent some batches back to the factories. Russia has over 450 Kalibrs and makes about 29 a month, but because Ukrainian air defence downs them effectively most new ones go to the strategic reserve and the fleets. It also holds more than 600 Oniks anti-ship missiles upgraded for land attack and more than 120 Zircons. Russia's 2026 state order for Geran-type drones and decoys such as the Gerbera exceeds 100,000 units. It holds about 6,200 Gerans in reserve and produces about 10,000 drones a month, mainly at the Alabuga special economic zone in Tatarstan. Since summer production has shifted to turbojet drones, which HUR attributes to Ukraine's mobile fire groups downing slow targets en masse: about 4,000 jet drones a month, mostly Geran-4 and Geran-5 flying at 400–500 km/h, plus about 2,000 cheaper propeller-driven Geran-2s used to wear down air defence and locate its positions. Ukraine still lacks a scalable answer to jet-powered Gerans: Brave1 says seven fast interceptor types have scored kills and over 1,000 are on order, while frontline crews and Sternenko's foundation point to speed and cost gaps

2026-10-21

Ukrainian units on the front line describe jet-powered Gerans as largely beyond the reach of their current interceptor drones. At the command post of the 'Posipaky' battalion of the 39th Anti-Aircraft Missile Regiment on the Zaporizhzhia front, commander 'Ramses' told Ukrainska Pravda that jet drones flying at around 500 km/h are simply left alone, because conventional interceptor drones cannot catch them. Russia now sends them one by one at 20–30 minute intervals rather than in large groups, and most cross the front line; they climb and accelerate over occupied territory and appear on screens only at the last moment at 5,000–8,000 m, above MANPADS reach. Most are Geran-4s, with the cruise-missile-like Geran-5 also in use, and according to Ramses they are operator-controlled via mesh networks in which drones relay the radio signal to each other. He said Russia used about 2,400 jet-powered and 2,400 conventional Gerans in August; the Air Force reports downing only 1,460 jet drones that month. 'Betsyk', commander of an interceptor battalion of the 1030th Anti-Aircraft Missile-Artillery Regiment 'Aquila' (3rd Army Corps), told ArmyInform (via Euromaidan Press) that his crews destroyed several thousand Russian aerial targets during Operation Vivaldi north of Lyman, but only small numbers of jet drones: interceptors need 500–600 km/h, so the regiment relies mainly on self-propelled anti-aircraft guns and SAM/MANPADS systems against them. He expects Russia to adapt once effective interceptors appear, but not to double drone speed. On the countermeasures side, Brave1 head Andrii Hrytseniuk told Ukrainska Pravda that funding for anti-jet-drone solutions began in summer 2025: 22 teams are working on high-speed interceptors (12 with Brave1 funding), with miniature turbojet, propeller-electric and electric ducted-fan designs. Seven interceptor types have been used in combat and scored kills, including an unidentified turbojet interceptor used by the SBU against a Geran-5, and the government has ordered 'well over 1,000', though production is still ramping up; scaling took six months for propeller-Shahed interceptors and 'won't happen overnight' this time. Eleven Ukrainian firms now make small turbojet engines, six in mass production. Brave1 also has ten teams developing cheap anti-aircraft missiles (target price at most $20,000 each), two of which are preparing for operational use; Luch's RK-2PZ, the Shershen system and the Nika missile are among other projects. According to Zelensky, as of early October the Air Force still accounted for half of all jet-drone kills, often with cannon fire at close range because of missile shortages, and the Bars drone for about a third. Euromaidan Press reports on footage released around 17 October by the 1021st Anti-Aircraft Missile-Artillery Brigade showing a ramp-launched, rocket-boosted Bars steered by infrared camera towards a Geran-5 (see e-20261019-07); Zelensky claimed on 4 October a hit rate of about 30% for Bars, the highest among Ukrainian interceptors. Its manufacturer says it builds over 2,000 Bars a month, mostly strike versions, and a Ukrainian-made Bars engine passed final tests in September. Defence Minister Yevhen Khmara says hundreds of domestically made automated machine-gun turrets are in service. Cost is the other constraint. A foundation led by volunteer Serhii Sternenko (which says it has bought 291,502 FPV drones) estimates that a jet-Geran kill costs $25,000–1.2 million with fighter aircraft, about $30,000–4 million with ground-based systems such as IRIS-T, NASAMS, Vampire or Avenger, and from $2,000 with interceptor drones, which are not yet scalable; it claims to have found a cheaper counter-method with Ukrainian manufacturers but gives no details. Ukrainska Pravda puts a conventional Geran at up to $70,000, with the jet version tens of thousands more, against hundreds of thousands for a Stinger and about $500,000 for an AIM-9X. Interviewees stress that no single system suffices and that only a mix of interceptor drones, turrets, guns, aircraft and missiles can work. The kill counts, hit rates and order figures are Ukrainian claims and have not been independently verified.
