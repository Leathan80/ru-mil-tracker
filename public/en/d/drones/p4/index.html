The Insider investigation: China's Shenzhen Xingkai Technology supplies mesh modems for Russian Geran and Gerbera drones

2026-10-15

Militarnyi, citing an investigation by The Insider, reports that the Chinese firm Shenzhen Xingkai Technology supplies mesh modems used in Russian Geran and Gerbera long-range drones. According to the journalists, the equipment reached Russia via the companies Atlant-Engineering and LT Global Forwarding and was declared at customs as antennas and remote-control devices. The Insider estimates the company holds about half of the market for high-performance mesh modems used in these drones. Investigators examined a modem recovered from a modified Gerbera and then approached the manufacturer posing as suppliers to the Alabuga drone plant. A company representative identified the device as the XK F-359, sent documentation, photos and warehouse video, and quoted ¥19.5 million (about $2.87 million) for 500 units, continuing talks despite the stated military end use and proposing payment through DBS Bank, Ping An Bank and China Merchants Bank. The modem reportedly provides line-of-sight links of 100–300 km, letting drones relay commands to each other so operators can retask them in flight deep inside Ukraine and receive onboard video. It uses AMD programmable FPGA chips that the firm says it obtains through intermediaries; AMD says it does not supply the company directly and has no partnership with it. The Insider also found modems from a second Chinese maker, Xi'an Sihou Network Technology, in downed Russian drones. Militarnyi recalls that Shenzhen Xingkai was earlier listed among 11 unsanctioned Chinese suppliers of components for Molniya drones, and that in January 2026 Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR) identified the company's XK-F358 radio modems in Russia's new Geran-5 jet drones. The investigation's findings have not been independently confirmed.

Poland's operational commander says the air force is on nightly maximum readiness against Russian drones and has switched from visual to positive identification to engage them faster.

2026-10-14

Russian drone incursions are forcing the Polish Air Force to operate at the highest readiness every night, putting heavy strain on personnel and equipment, Maj. Gen. Ireneusz Nowak, operational commander of the Polish Armed Forces and responsible for Polish airspace, said at the Warsaw Security Forum, as reported by Breaking Defense. He said 80 percent of his attention goes to air defence, while Poland's integrated air defence modernisation is only about 30 percent complete: new systems are being procured, but the ground and air components are still three to four years away. To bridge the gap, the air force changed its rules of engagement on 1 October: instead of Visual Identification (VID), in which a pilot must confirm a target's type, markings or nationality by sight before engaging, it now applies Positive Identification (PI), allowing engagement based on radar data, other sensors and intelligence, including information passed on by Ukraine. "So now we are much more flexible, much more responsive," Nowak said. He acknowledged that using fighter jets against cheap drones is a poor cost ratio, but said there is currently no alternative, and called for "massive, responsive" counter-UAV weapon systems. Norway's military representative to NATO, Lt. Gen. Rolf Folland, said the air superiority model of recent decades is under pressure because munitions cannot be expended "in one blast" but must be used again and again. Near-term options are delayed. Integration of APKWS laser-guided rockets on Polish F-16s is held up because Poland could not obtain the certified fuzes used by the US Air Force due to business restrictions and is waiting for a new fuze type. Nowak called the F/A-50GF, which the defence minister announced has entered combat duty, a very promising drone hunter against the current Russian threat, but said weapon integration means it will only be fully operational in about two years - "the time is too long," he said.

Since the night of 13–14 October, the military "Starshield" service that SpaceX provides to Ukrainian forces has stopped working on fast-moving objects without prior warning, according to Ukrainian presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov. Starshield is SpaceX's military business unit, with enhanced encryption and dedicated terminals running over the Starlink constellation. A source in Ukraine's Defence Ministry told Reuters that receivers now work in motion again, but only up to 100 mph (160 km/h). Russian jet drones and missiles fly at roughly twice the cap speed, according to The Telegraph (via Euromaidan Press). On 16 October President Zelensky said the restrictions had disrupted several operations, including interceptions of jet Gerans. He added that Ukraine does not use Starlink for strikes inside Russia, and that the Defence Ministry had received signals from the White House that capability would be restored, although speeds had not yet returned to previous levels. Who ordered the cap is disputed. That pressure included a tough meeting with Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff in Miami on 9 October and a US diesel deal with Russia. For Russian forces, the cap temporarily eases pressure on front-line positions and rear logistics hit by Starlink-guided strike drones, and it weakens Ukraine's cheapest counter to jet Gerans. Rostec claims its 30 mm ZAK-30 Tsitadel anti-drone gun with programmable shrapnel rounds outperforms Western rivals such as Skynex and Phalanx.

2026-10-14

Russian state defence conglomerate Rostec is marketing its new 30 mm ZAK-30 "Tsitadel" (Citadel) anti-drone gun as a direct competitor to Western systems such as Germany's Skynex and the US Phalanx and its land-based C-RAM variant, Defence Blog reports. In a statement this week Rostec claimed that combat use has shown advantages in automation, cost per shot and effectiveness. According to Rostec, Skynex's 35 mm AHEAD rounds produce a powerful fragment cloud but make the system heavier and more expensive, while Phalanx/C-RAM fire 20 mm Vulcan Gatling guns at very high rates without programmable rounds and therefore use more ammunition per target. These are manufacturer claims and have not been independently verified. Tsitadel fires 30 mm shrapnel shells with a remotely set fuze; the fire-control computer calculates the optimal burst point on the drone's flight path so the shell detonates just ahead of the target, which Rostec says reduces the number of rounds per kill. Targets are detected and tracked by radar and an electro-optical sensor working in visible light and infrared, with a highly automated detect-to-engage sequence.
