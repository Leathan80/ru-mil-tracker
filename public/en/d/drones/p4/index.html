Cost of insuring Russian data centres against drone attacks jumps up to 100-fold after Ukrainian strikes on Yandex and Sberbank

2026-11-13

The cost of insuring data centres in Russia has risen sharply since Ukraine began striking them with drones, Meduza reports, citing the Russian business daily Kommersant. 2026-11-13SINGLE SOURCE

The Kazan-based paper Business Online, in its weekly review of the war, quotes an article from the September 2026 issue of the Russian General Staff journal Voyennaya Mysl (Military Thought) by V. Kavtarov and S. Sakhanov. The authors argue that linear formations have become extremely vulnerable now that enemy drones dominate the battlefield, and propose a 'qualitatively new' defensive method they call the 'dispersed echeloned bastion': abandoning a continuous front line in favour of a network of autonomous combat modules ('bastions') spread across the front and in depth. Each bastion is built around mobile groups equipped with uncrewed ground vehicles, FPV interceptor drones and EW assets, tied together by a single situational-awareness picture from Zala and Orlan reconnaissance drones. An attacker who wedges into such a defence does not break through it, the authors write, but comes under crossfire from prepared, concealed and frequently changing module positions. A follow-up column by Business Online military commentator Nikita Yurchenko (15 November) quotes more of the same Voyennaya Mysl article (titled "On the direction of development of the employment of the Unmanned Systems Forces of the Russian Armed Forces", by Colonel V.V. Kavtarov and Major S.A. Sakhnov). The authors describe an "integrated blockade": heterogeneous drone systems in one control loop, divided into "blocking sectors" — FPV drones and Lancets on free hunt in the tactical zone, Gerans suppressing air defence and logistics nodes, uncrewed boats sealing the coast — isolating an area to a depth of up to several hundred kilometres without direct contact; they cite the FPV blockade of the Yunakivka–Sudzha road in Kursk oblast as proof. For the bastion defence they claim 2–3 times fewer losses to drone strikes, 60–80% higher survivability against FPV hits than a classic trench system, bastions spaced 500–2,000 m apart and ground robots changing position every 15–30 minutes, and they set out a five-phase drone-led attack (reconnaissance and relay placement, robotic engineer reconnaissance and breaching, strikes on enemy drone control points, air and EW cover by interceptor drones, then infantry clearance with UGVs under smoke and drone resupply/evacuation). The column also repeats deputy defence minister Valery Solodchuk on the ~30 km "kill zone" supplied only by drones and robots, and quotes historian Ilya Moshchansky that the Arena-M active protection system was tried on tanks around August but overwhelmed by 40–50 drones per tank against 12 interceptor elements. In the same review, citing the Telegram channel Voyennaya Khronika and the US think tank FPRI, the paper says Russia's drone units are moving from specialised, highly autonomous formations towards established structures integrated into the combined-arms armies, with unmanned systems brigades and more regiment- and battalion-level drone units. About 600 specialists of the Rubicon centre, together with its former commander Colonel Sergei Budnikov, are said to have moved to the 50th Unmanned Systems Brigade 'Varyag', which operates along the Bryansk, Kursk and Belgorod border; Rubicon itself is described as working in the occupied Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson and Zaporizhzhia regions. Czechia discusses up to 16 L-39NG Skyfox jets for Ukraine as low-cost hunters of Russian drones

2026-11-12

Czechia could supply Ukraine with up to 16 Aero L-39NG Skyfox light jets, The War Zone reports. Four were previously agreed for transfer and twelve more are under discussion. Czech Deputy Prime Minister and Industry and Trade Minister Karel Havlíček raised the expanded deal on a visit to Kyiv on 11 November, where he met Ukrainian Prime Minister Serhiy Koretskyi. He called the talks encouraging, but no agreement on the additional aircraft has been finalised. Ukraine had earlier sought Czech L-159 ALCA light combat jets; President Petr Pavel supported transferring up to four, but Prime Minister Andrej Babiš's government refused, saying the Czech military needed them. The intended role builds on Aero Vodochody's 'Drone Hunter' concept: an electro-optical targeting system plus launchers for 70 mm laser-guided rockets, developed with Thales Belgium, with gun pods as an option. This configuration is not yet in operational service. The Skyfox is a modernised L-39 with a US Williams FJ44-4M turbofan, a glass cockpit and five hardpoints. TWZ gives a top speed of about 560 mph (about 900 km/h) and a ceiling of 38,000 ft. Aero Vodochody puts operating costs at about $1,700–2,300 per flight hour, against about $22,000 for an F-16-class fighter. A 70 mm guided rocket of the APKWS II type costs roughly $20,000–25,000, against about $1 million for an AIM-120 and about $500,000 for an AIM-9X. The jet is meant to take pressure off Ukraine's F-16s, MiG-29s, Su-27s and Mirages, more than half of whose F-16s were reportedly stuck in maintenance amid shortages of AIM-9 and AIM-120 missiles. It is aimed especially at Russia's jet-powered Geran-3/4/5 drones, with the Geran-4 reportedly reaching about 280–310 mph and 26,000 ft. Justin Bronk of RUSI called the L-39NG very cheap and simple to operate compared with frontline fighters, with speed and endurance suited to intercepting jet drones. His main caveat is the lack of an onboard radar, which Ukraine's ground radar network can partly offset. Many Ukrainian pilots and technicians already know the L-39. Aero Vodochody builds about 12 Skyfox a year (capacity 18), and its schedule is reportedly booked through Q2 2027, which could delay deliveries. Russian outlet Topwar reported the same Czech claims; its readers questioned the radar-less concept and the delivery backlog.

War on the Rocks analysis by ex-PM Honcharuk: Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces compete in a 'Time Domain' of adaptation speed, a model Russia has copied

2026-11-10

In an opinion-driven analysis for War on the Rocks, Oleksiy Honcharuk (Ukraine's prime minister in 2019-2020, former adviser to the first commander of the Unmanned Systems Forces, chair of the Aerorozvidka supervisory board and, since 2025, chairman of the defence-tech firm UFORCE) argues that the decisive dimension of the war is how fast each side adapts. He calls this the 'Time Domain': a contest over the interval between a new capability reaching the front and the enemy's working counter, which can now shrink to weeks. Ukraine created the Unmanned Systems Forces against opposition from career generals; he states that with about 2.5% of personnel the branch accounted for roughly a third of Russian losses in the past year. On Russia, Honcharuk credits it with being first to field jam-resistant fibre-optic drones at scale, around Kursk, through Rubicon, a defence-ministry centre that links developers, staff officers and operators in one loop; that lead lasted months, until Ukraine had more than 20 certified fibre-optic models from 11 producers by April 2025. He writes that Putin ordered a dedicated branch in December 2024 and that Russia's Unmanned Systems Forces were operational by late 2025, which he reads as Moscow copying a form that works. He presents Ukraine's 412th Brigade 'Nemesis' and Lasar's Group as 'Combat Hubs' that fuse combat, analysis, R&D and production on daily, six-weekly and six-monthly cycles; the Nemesis figures (over 520 targets destroyed for 107 airframes lost in seven months) come from the project's own records, not public data. He urges Western militaries to track adaptation time in planning, buy adaptation cycles rather than platforms, train against unpredictable opposition and create a small dedicated unmanned branch, and suggests a visible adaptation capacity could deter, while warning that a rapidly closing window can also provoke an aggressor. This is an argument piece by an author with a stake in Ukrainian and British-Ukrainian defence technology, not a neutral assessment; his loss figures and claims about Russia's force structure are not independently sourced in the article.
