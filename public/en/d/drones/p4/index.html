Putin says Ukraine has 'no antidote' to jet-powered Gerans as Russia fields Geran-3/4/5; 1020th AA Regiment downs a Geran-5 at 425 km/h

2026-09-29

Speaking at the Cultural Forum, Vladimir Putin claimed Ukraine was seeking an 'air ceasefire' because it cannot cope with Russia's new jet-powered Geran drones: 'At least for now, they have no way to counter them — no antidote.' Meduza sets out the family behind the claim. The Geran-3 (first jet variant; Meduza dates it to early 2025, TWZ to early 2024) roughly doubled speed to 300–350 km/h with a Chinese Telefly JT80 turbojet, at the cost of range and payload. The delta-wing Geran-4, which TWZ says appeared in May 2026, uses a Telefly TF-TJ2000A with about 2.5 times the JT80's thrust; TWZ gives 450–500 km/h, altitudes up to 8 km and a documented flight of over 700 km (Crimea to Lutsk). The conventionally laid-out Geran-5 uses the same engine, can be launched from aircraft and helicopters as well as from the ground, and costs an estimated $100,000+, more than twice a Geran-2 (Meduza). HUR data cited by Euromaidan Press give the Geran-5 a 90 kg warhead, 450–600 km/h cruise speed, 950 km range and 6 km ceiling. Ukrainian military intelligence estimates Russian output at about 3,000 Geran-4/5 a month. TWZ describes how guidance has evolved. Most drones still fly autonomously to preset coordinates, but some carry GSM modems with SIM cards (Ukrainian, Russian, Belarusian and EU) and Chinese mesh modems (about 300 km range) with tail-mounted relay antennas, so that, per RFE/RL reporting, an operator in Bryansk, Oryol or Crimea can steer the drone and receive video for real-time battle damage assessment; a Geran-5 variant reportedly carries a DSMAC-style terrain-image navigation module (Polkovnyk GSh Telegram channel). Wreckage has included Texas Instruments circuit boards. Tactics include high-altitude flight to stay above cheap interceptors and, per one open-source analyst, a Geran-4 that cuts its engine and glides slowly before diving on its target. In the 28 September night attack, according to the Ukrainian Air Force, 79 of 165 drones launched were jet-powered; 112 were downed or suppressed and hits were recorded at 14 locations. Ukraine is already shooting them down, but at a price. A crew from the 1020th Anti-Aircraft Missile and Artillery Regiment (callsign Yastrub) downed a Geran-5 over Chernihiv Oblast at 425 km/h and above 3 km with a newly delivered interceptor, according to the Serhiy Prytula Foundation, which published the footage; TWZ attributes the 1020th's Geran-5 kill to Wild Hornets' STING S, whose maker claims about a dozen Geran-4s and several Geran-5s in recent days (not independently verified). Zelensky said at the UN General Assembly that four new jet-powered interceptor drones had been successfully tested, and on 26 September that Ukraine intercepts about 55% of jet drones (versus over 90% of older models). Meduza notes that the more than ten electric interceptor models (under $10,000, about four per kill) are nearly powerless against the jets; the alternatives are $70,000–100,000+ missiles or APKWS II rockets (up to $20,000) fired only by F-16s and fewer than 20 Vampire systems. Scaling up faster interceptors could add hundreds of millions or even billions of dollars a year to a budget that, per Zelensky, already has a $27 billion gap in 2026.

russia Equips New Geran-5 Drones With Kh-101-Style Navigation: Why Darkness Could Be Problem

2026-09-28SINGLE SOURCE

According to Defense Express, Russia has upgraded its Geran-5 jet-powered drones with a new guidance module called Susanin, a Digital Scene Matching Area Correlation (DSMAC) system that navigates by comparing preloaded reference satellite imagery with images from an onboard camera. This lets the drone keep navigating where satellite navigation (GNSS) is jammed or unavailable, making it more resistant to electronic warfare. Defense Express stresses that the technology is not new: comparable systems have flown on the Russian Kh-101 cruise missile and the American Tomahawk for years. It also notes that Ukraine uses similar scene-matching on its own deep-strike drones. The outlet points to a key limitation: accuracy depends on lighting, season and even the time of day, since the sun angle changes shadow patterns. At night the system performs considerably worse, particularly without extra illumination or specialised image processing. This creates a tension, the outlet argues, because Russia mostly launches Kh-101 missiles at night, when Ukrainian mobile fire groups find targets harder to detect. A night-launched Geran-5 relying on Susanin could thus lose navigation accuracy, although this is Defense Express's assessment and not an observed result. Caveat: this is single-source reporting from Defense Express (Ukrainian defence outlet); the claims have not been independently verified.

Sources: Defense Express

The channel also says the K5 is faster than the standard Geran-5 and may therefore be misidentified in flight as other jet-powered strike weapons such as the Dan-T. Militarnyi stresses that Ukrainian sources have not officially confirmed either the K5 designation or the presence of a DSMAC-class system. Russian sources partly corroborate the claim. He did not say which Geran modification carries it or describe it as DSMAC. Rossiyskaya Gazeta (27 September), citing military Telegram channels, reports that Susanin compares terrain imagery with a map loaded before flight, similar to the "Otblesk-U" optical correction option of the Kh-101 strategic cruise missile. The Russian channel Voyenny Osvedomitel said the jet Geran is "increasingly becoming a full-fledged cruise missile". For comparison, Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR) gives the baseline Geran-5 as 6.5 m long, with a 3.2 m wingspan, a maximum take-off weight of up to 850 kg, a warhead of about 90 kg, a cruising speed of 450–600 km/h, a range of about 950 km and a ceiling of 6 km. HUR's teardown of a baseline Geran-5 found a Kometa-M12 jam-resistant satellite navigation receiver, an inertial navigation system, a tracker with cellular modems, a mesh modem and a Chinese TELEFLY TF-TJ2000A turbojet. The public HUR component list includes no separate DSMAC system. Russia had already moved the turbojet from an external nacelle into the fuselage to cut drag, which may add speed and range. If confirmed, terrain-image navigation would give the drone a way to find its route that does not depend on GNSS or radio links. GNSS jamming and spoofing, which the baseline already partly counters with the Kometa-M12 receiver, would then do less against the final route correction.
