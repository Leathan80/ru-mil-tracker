Russian drones crash and explode in Moldova and Romania through 1 October, including a Geran-4 jet drone blast beside a highway in Orhei district and a strike on a telecom mast; ISW links incursions, Polish Starlink sabotage and Berlin airport halt to one pattern

2026-09-24

During a Russian attack on Ukraine overnight into 24 September 2026 (Ukraine's Air Force reported 282 drones plus ballistic and other missiles), drones strayed into Moldova and Romania. Moldova's Defence Ministry reported four drone incursions and partially closed its northern airspace. Near Cernița (Florești district) a drone hit a tree, fell into a field beside a farm and exploded without casualties; police preliminarily identified it as a Gerbera, a low-cost Russian drone widely used against Ukraine, and the first found in Moldova. A second explosion near Vadul Rașcov is under investigation. President Maia Sandu counts 39 Russian drone incursions in 2026, against 17 in all of 2025. In Romania, radar tracked a target entering northern airspace; two F-16s were launched and residents received emergency phone alerts. The drone stayed about four minutes before crashing near Solca, without casualties or damage; Romanian officials did not confirm its origin, while ISW-based reporting describes it as a camera-equipped Gerbera, one of four over Bukovyna (Ukraine says it shot down three). Poland's Operational Command scrambled fighters twice and activated ground-based air defence and radar. The incidents continued. On 26 September an unidentified drone crossed Moldovan airspace between 11:16 and 11:18, from the direction of Stepanivka (Odesa Oblast) past Novokotovsk in breakaway Transnistria. That evening, after residents alerted the authorities, Moldova's Border Police found debris of an unidentified drone outside Volintiri (Ștefan Vodă district, near the Ukrainian border), cordoned off the area and contacted the Ukrainian authorities; type and origin had not been officially identified by 27 September. Romania reported an unidentified object in its airspace overnight into 26 September, and allied fighters were scrambled. On the night of 28-29 September Romania again scrambled jets for an aerial asset near its airspace, though no violation was officially reported. On 29 September Romania's Defence Ministry said debris of an object, presumably a drone, had fallen on farmland on Great Brăila Island in Brăila County, reported by a local resident during the night. He said a fire broke out after the crash but went out by itself, with no casualties or damage. Interior Ministry units and the Romanian Intelligence Service (SRI) cordoned off the site and the pieces will be sent for expert examination; type and origin were not stated (Ukrainska Pravda via European Pravda). Police had already found drone fragments near Chișinău on 18 September. On 30 September, during another Russian mass attack on Ukraine, Kyiv Independent reported a drone crashing in Moldova (only the headline was available to us). According to Euromaidan Press, a Russian drone exploded near Hîrbovăț against a civilian mobile operator's mast, leaving blast marks over about 12 metres of the structure; service stayed up. (Euromaidan Press placed the village in Transnistria; Ukrainska Pravda, citing Moldova's General Police Inspectorate, puts it in Anenii Noi district and says the drone exploded about 40 m above the ground after striking the mast.) A second, jet-powered drone was detected at 10:26 heading toward Soroca in the north, and flights over northern and central Moldova were suspended. Parliament Speaker Igor Grosu said Russia would answer for its war crimes. That evening a third incident followed: according to Moldova's Defence Ministry and General Police Inspectorate (via Newsmaker, European Pravda and Ukrainska Pravda), a drone entered Moldovan airspace at around 20:45 and exploded after colliding with trees in a tree line right next to a highway near Brănești in Orhei district, in the centre of the country. Shortly before midnight police said it had features typical of a jet-powered Geran. On 1 October they reported a main crater of about 50 cm across and 30 cm deep, damaged trees and traces of shockwave and heat within up to 30 m, two smaller craters (about 50 and 30 cm) on the roadside some 15 m away, and fragments scattered over roughly 120 m, including rear wing sections marked 'Geran-4', electronic modules and propulsion parts, among them a heat-damaged jet engine. No casualties were reported. ISW links the 24 September crashes to two incidents on 23 September. The first was a fire that destroyed the power system and backup generator of a Starlink ground station in Wola Krobowska, Poland. Deputy PM Krzysztof Gawkowski called it sabotage aimed at Ukraine's military internet access, though no perpetrator has been officially named. The second was a 45-minute halt at Berlin-Brandenburg airport after an unconfirmed drone sighting. ISW assesses that intentional and accidental Russian drone incursions are likely part of a Kremlin effort to normalise airspace violations by making them more frequent. It also says growing Western intelligence warnings suggest Moscow is diversifying its means of attack, citing Lithuanian reports of possible container-launched Gerberas in the Mediterranean. Euromaidan Press adds a political angle: on 29 September 46 opposition lawmakers filed a motion to suspend pro-EU President Maia Sandu (67 of 101 votes needed, then a referendum within 30 days). Ukrainian analyst Oleksandr Kovalenko argues the timing is deliberate, a pro-Russian destabilisation campaign; this is his assessment, not an established finding.

Danish intelligence (DDIS) sees 'low but growing risk' of a limited Russian military attack on a NATO border state in the coming months, alongside escalating hybrid warfare

2026-09-24

Denmark's Defence Intelligence Service (DDIS/FE) published an unclassified update, 'Assessment of the Threat from Russia', on 24 September 2026, supplementing its February 2025 assessment. It states that Russia will further escalate its hybrid war against NATO and the West in the coming months, citing intensified sabotage, destructive cyberattacks and drone attacks on European targets — including a foiled drone plot against Leipzig/Halle Airport that German authorities say Russia planned over several months — and Russian drones and in some cases missiles hitting NATO countries bordering Ukraine. It names the 14 September incident in which a Russian frigate off Gedser fired flares at a Danish military helicopter as the most serious recent example of aggressive Russian conduct towards NATO units in the Baltic; Denmark's security service PET has separately warned of sabotage planning against Danish defence companies. DDIS assesses a 'low but growing risk' that Russia will carry out a limited military attack on one or more NATO countries bordering Russia in the coming months — even while the Ukraine war continues and without having built the capability for a regional war — as a potential 'desperate move' to divert European resources from Ukraine and divide NATO. Possible forms it names: isolated long-range strikes on infrastructure supporting Ukraine (with Moscow possibly claiming the weapons strayed because Ukraine jammed them), a false-flag operation using Ukrainian-made drones, or a limited, possibly unmarked troop contingent — the latter requiring a months-long, hard-to-hide build-up. An outright invasion is judged 'highly unlikely' but 'cannot be ruled out' and would need at least six months of preparation. The February 2025 timelines remain valid according to DDIS: about six months for Russia to be able to fight a local war against a neighbour, about two years to pose a credible regional threat to the Baltic Sea NATO states, and about five years to be ready for a large-scale war in Europe — assuming the Ukraine war ends or freezes, Russian forces grow faster than the West's and Moscow judges the US would not back European allies. DDIS says Russia sees a 'window of opportunity' before European military capability grows towards 2030, and that its behaviour hinges on whether it believes it can stay below the threshold for a collective NATO response — a threshold Russia itself is likely unsure about.

Russia adapts to Ukraine's middle-strike drone campaign with mobile fire groups, Rubicon interceptors and Starlink jammers

2026-09-23

According to a Kyiv Independent field report from the southern front, Russian forces have adapted to Ukraine's expanded "middle strike" campaign. That campaign uses drones against targets from roughly 35 km (the end of FPV range) out to about 300 km behind the line. Within weeks of the campaign being scaled up in the south, Russia changed logistics routes and fielded new countermeasures. Columns carrying high-value cargo are now often escorted by mobile fire groups, some riding on the vehicles and some posted at intervals along key routes such as the E58 highway to Crimea. Russia's elite Rubicon drone unit has also published dozens of videos of its interceptor teams downing Starlink-equipped Hornet drones and other middle-strike drones. The commander of Ukraine's 422nd Unmanned Systems Regiment, Mykola "Tyson" Kolesnyk, said these measures now bring down "a certain percentage" of Ukrainian drones but have not critically changed his unit's work. He also said that moving Rubicon's interceptor teams to the rear to guard the highways has let more Ukrainian fixed-wing drones cross the front uncontested. The report also describes Russian efforts to take away Ukraine's Starlink advantage. Starlink was cut off for Russian forces in early February 2026 after mandatory terminal registration through Ukraine's Diia app was introduced. Since then, Russian fixed-wing drones, from the tactical Molniya to the long-range Geran class, have increasingly been fitted with decentralized mesh radio modems to keep their links and video feeds under Ukrainian EW. Russia has announced its own satellite internet system, Rassvet, but its rollout has suffered setbacks, including a satellite that reportedly fell from the sky and others that did not reach their intended orbits. Russia is also testing several Starlink jammers. The most prominent is the Peresvet-M, which according to Ukrainian presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskretnov floods the terminal's uplink channel. Beskretnov said it can deny Starlink over about 20 km² and has been deployed to protect key targets in Crimea. Kolesnyk said his regiment, working with the SBU's Alpha centre, destroyed a first Russian Starlink jammer on 14 June 2026 and has since helped destroy at least five such systems. He described them as bulky and vulnerable because of their large power needs. About two weeks before the interview, according to Kolesnyk, Russia began running the jammers from the electricity grid instead of generators. He said the unit felt their effect only once and that it is still unclear whether Russia will scale the jammers up or whether they are mainly an information operation. The regiment says it struck a substation in occupied Zaporizhzhia Oblast that powered radars, EW equipment and Starlink jammers, and that Russian air defence downed two of the four Zozulia drones sent. All these claims come from a Ukrainian unit that is a party to the conflict and have not been independently confirmed.
