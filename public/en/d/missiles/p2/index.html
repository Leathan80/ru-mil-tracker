Russia's mass missile and drone strike of 2-3 November hits Ukrainian power plants and hospitals in Dnipro and Kharkiv; 38 killed nationwide in a day

2026-11-03

On the night of 2-3 November Russia launched one of its largest combined strikes of the war on Ukraine's energy system. According to Ukraine's Air Force, it used Iskander-M/KN-23 ballistic missiles and Zircon missiles fired from Rostov, Voronezh and Kursk oblasts, 59 Kh-101/Kh-59/Kh-69/Iskander-K cruise missiles launched from Vologda, Orenburg and Oryol oblasts, and 289 Shahed (120 of them jet-powered), Gerbera and Parodiya decoy drones launched from Oryol, Kursk, Millerovo, Primorsko-Akhtarsk, occupied Donetsk and Hvardiiske and Chauda in Crimea. The Air Force initially gave no number of ballistic missiles; President Zelensky later said 17 were fired. Air defence reported downing or jamming 3 ballistic missiles, 49 cruise missiles (three more still being verified) and 257 drones, with hits at 23 locations and debris falling at 14 more. The main target areas were Kyiv, Kharkiv, Vinnytsia and Dnipropetrovsk oblasts. Zelensky said energy and generation facilities and substations were hit in nine oblasts, and Ukrenergo called it one of the most massive attacks on the energy sector, forcing emergency power cuts in Kyiv and several regions. Civilian medical sites were hit as well. In Dnipro, a morning missile strike killed two people and injured 20, according to Governor Oleksandr Hanzha. It damaged a medical centre and one of the city's hospitals, and Mayor Borys Filatov said a hospice was 'destroyed', with about 50 terminally ill patients moved elsewhere and 20 residential buildings damaged. In Kharkiv, ballistic missiles and guided bombs hit a hospital at around 03:00. Meduza, citing Mayor Ihor Terekhov and Governor Oleh Syniehubov, reported that the passageway between hospital buildings in the Nemyshlianskyi district was struck, 62 people were injured and 226 patients were being evacuated. The regional prosecutor later counted 73 victims, including people with acute stress reactions. The Kyiv Independent put the toll from Russian attacks across Ukraine over the day at at least 38 dead; its headline figure for the injured was later raised from 158 to 167. Most of the deaths came from a separate glide-bomb attack on Komyshuvakha in Zaporizhzhia Oblast on 2 November, which killed at least 31 people with 48 still missing, according to acting governor Mykhailo Semikin. Zelensky said the follow-up strike on a shelter used a FAB-3000 bomb. All casualty and interception figures come from Ukrainian officials and have not been independently confirmed.

Sternenko: small cruise missiles such as Banderol will be a bigger problem for Ukraine than jet-powered Shaheds

2026-11-03

Serhii Sternenko, a volunteer, founder of the drone-supplying Sternenko Community fund and former adviser to Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov, told Ukrainska Pravda in an interview (3 November 2026) that Ukraine faces larger threats than jet-powered Shaheds. He says Russia is scaling up cheap small cruise missiles that can be used in mass. Dozens a month are already being launched, mainly the Banderol, from other drones or helicopters; he also names the Filin and the Dan-T. He puts their speed at 600-700 km/h and expects numbers to grow substantially in the first half of 2027, at which point they will be a harder problem than jet Shaheds. He names ballistic missiles as a second problem, one that also affects European partners, while noting he is no expert on them. These are his own estimates and have not been independently confirmed. Speed drives his reasoning on interceptors. He describes the Geran-3 as the slowest and within reach of ordinary interceptors, the Geran-4 as flying at about 400 km/h or more, and the Geran-5 as closer to a missile and the hardest to intercept. Electric-motor interceptors have had the most success so far, but he rates them least promising: he estimates they can reach 550-600 km/h, and only for a few minutes. Turbojet interceptors have more potential but cost more and are harder to build because engines are a bottleneck. A recent government decision is meant to simplify production, which he says will take time to show results. He expects roughly serial production of a counter to jet Shaheds by the end of 2026. The Bars jet-powered drone-missile, adapted as an interceptor, is already in small-batch use. In the longer term he favours a multi-purpose jet-powered platform the size of a Geran or Gerbera that carries machine guns, interceptors or small surface-to-air missiles. On 1 October he had already written on X that the Banderol and Dan-T are significantly more dangerous than Shaheds and that their use will reach the thousands. At that point he called interceptors against jet Shaheds a temporary fix. He argued for short- and medium-range surface-to-air missiles, partly from partners and partly home-built, for large numbers of seeker heads and rocket motors, and for cheap jet trainers as interceptors. He also says Russia is building mesh relay networks that in some cases reach 200 km or more. Sternenko supplies drones to the armed forces through his fund, so he is not a neutral observer.

Russia fits drones and missiles with larger multi-element Kometa satellite-navigation antennas to beat Ukrainian jamming; Shaheds with 32-element arrays reported over Kyiv

2026-11-02

Russia is equipping its drones and missiles with larger, jam-resistant satellite-navigation antennas, which Ukrainian officials say makes it harder for Ukrainian electronic countermeasures to disrupt their navigation, Defense News reports. The modules in question are the Kometa navigation units made by VNIIR-Progress, which use arrays of small patch antennas. According to Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR), Kometa has been found on Geran drones, glide-bomb kits, Iskander-M and Kinzhal missiles and the new Filin cruise missile. HUR said on 19 October that Russia already uses 16-element arrays at scale and is setting up serial production of 20-element arrays, and that Russian defence firms ordered more than 100,000 Kometa modules this year for cruise and ballistic missiles, strike drones and guided bombs. Jam resistance depends on the number and layout of the elements and on the receiver chip's algorithm for separating satellite signals from interference. Serhii 'Flash' Beskrestnov, an electronic-warfare specialist and adviser to President Zelensky, said in an October Telegram post that Shaheds with 32-element antennas have been found attacking Kyiv, arguing that more elements allow more jamming sources to be suppressed. In his view Russia holds the advantage, because adding elements to an antenna is easier than Ukraine adding electronic-warfare sites; drones that get through leave more targets for costlier interceptors such as interceptor drones, jets and missiles. Ukrainian practitioners describe workarounds. Alkhimik, commander of Ukraine's Night Watch unit, bought the 32-element antenna of a crashed Shahed on the secondary market (as reported by Militarnyi in July) and found it consisted of two 16-element arrays covering different satellite bands; he said two separate jamming systems, one for L1 and one for L2/L5, could defeat it. Anatoliy Khrapchynskyi of Fly Group Ukraine told Reuters in September that spoofing works: networked devices feed drones false coordinates so the autopilot gradually steers them away from cities. The figures come from HUR and Ukrainian specialists and have not been independently verified.

Sources: Defense News

First photo of Russia's new low-cost Filin cruise missile shows ground launcher and solid-fuel booster; HUR cites 1,200 km claimed range and 50/100 kg warheads

2026-11-02

A photo of Russia's new Filin cruise missile on a ground launcher, with a solid-fuel booster strapped under its fuselage, has surfaced for the first time, Defence Blog reports, citing the Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi. The booster accelerates the missile off the launcher and then drops away, after which the Filin's small turbojet takes over for the cruise phase. The photo does not show where or when it was taken, or what vehicle or rail carries the launcher. According to Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR), which published a 3D model and parts breakdown on its War&Sanctions portal in October, Russia began using the Filin against Ukraine in late summer 2026. It is built by NPO Molniya, part of the state-owned Tactical Missiles Corporation. The missile is small: a carbon-fibre and fibreglass composite fuselage 3.5 m long, a 3 m wingspan and some 3D-printed parts. Russian claims give a speed of up to 800 km/h, a range of up to 1,200 km and flight altitudes from just above the ground to 6 km; HUR says versions exist with 50 kg and 100 kg warheads, a fraction of the payload of larger missiles such as the Kh-101. These performance figures come from Russian sources and are unconfirmed. HUR says the engine is a Russian-made R500 from a company called Reynolds, also found in the air-launched S-71K Kover and S-71M Monochrome cruise missiles, that the onboard electronics come from PLAZ (involved in the Harpiya, a modified Geran-2), and that the missile contains US- and Chinese-made electronic components. With a jet engine, a ground launcher and a small warhead, the Filin sits between the slower propeller-driven Geran drones and Russia's far more expensive full-size cruise missiles. Defense Express has suggested the Filin may be the 800 km/h jet-powered drone that Ukrainian sources reported earlier this year. Defence Blog places it among several new low-cost Russian strike weapons, alongside the air-launched S8000 Banderol, jet-powered Geran versions and a small, previously unseen cruise missile that came down almost intact in Odesa region on 27 October.
