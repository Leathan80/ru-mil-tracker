First photo of Russia's new low-cost Filin cruise missile shows ground launcher and solid-fuel booster; HUR cites 1,200 km claimed range and 50/100 kg warheads

2026-11-02

A photo of Russia's new Filin cruise missile on a ground launcher, with a solid-fuel booster strapped under its fuselage, has surfaced for the first time, Defence Blog reports, citing the Ukrainian outlet Militarnyi. The booster accelerates the missile off the launcher and then drops away, after which the Filin's small turbojet takes over for the cruise phase. The photo does not show where or when it was taken, or what vehicle or rail carries the launcher. According to Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR), which published a 3D model and parts breakdown on its War&Sanctions portal in October, Russia began using the Filin against Ukraine in late summer 2026. It is built by NPO Molniya, part of the state-owned Tactical Missiles Corporation. The missile is small: a carbon-fibre and fibreglass composite fuselage 3.5 m long, a 3 m wingspan and some 3D-printed parts. Russian claims give a speed of up to 800 km/h, a range of up to 1,200 km and flight altitudes from just above the ground to 6 km; HUR says versions exist with 50 kg and 100 kg warheads, a fraction of the payload of larger missiles such as the Kh-101. These performance figures come from Russian sources and are unconfirmed. HUR says the engine is a Russian-made R500 from a company called Reynolds, also found in the air-launched S-71K Kover and S-71M Monochrome cruise missiles, that the onboard electronics come from PLAZ (involved in the Harpiya, a modified Geran-2), and that the missile contains US- and Chinese-made electronic components. With a jet engine, a ground launcher and a small warhead, the Filin sits between the slower propeller-driven Geran drones and Russia's far more expensive full-size cruise missiles. Defense Express has suggested the Filin may be the 800 km/h jet-powered drone that Ukrainian sources reported earlier this year. Defence Blog places it among several new low-cost Russian strike weapons, alongside the air-launched S8000 Banderol, jet-powered Geran versions and a small, previously unseen cruise missile that came down almost intact in Odesa region on 27 October.

Sources: Defence Blog

Russia launches 433 drones overnight and 326 more by day on 31 October-1 November, mostly propeller-driven Shaheds, plus six S8000 Banderol cruise missiles; hits at 16 locations

2026-11-01

On the night of 31 October-1 November (from 18:00 on 31 October) Russian forces attacked Ukraine with 433 Shahed-type strike drones, Gerbera drones and Parodiya-type decoy drones, according to Ukraine's Air Force as reported by Ukrainska Pravda. Only 64 of them were jet-powered. The drones were launched from the areas of Oryol, Kursk, Shatalovo, Bryansk and Millerovo in Russia and from occupied Donetsk. By 07:30 aviation, surface-to-air missile units, electronic warfare assets, unmanned-systems units and mobile fire groups had shot down or jammed 371 drones, 47 of them jet-powered, with more than 40 still in Ukrainian airspace. Hits were recorded at 16 locations and falling debris from downed drones at 14 more. Ukrainska Pravda also notes that a Russian airstrike destroyed the executive committee building of Kramatorsk City Council late on 31 October. The attack continued in daylight. Between 06:30 and 19:00 on 1 November, the Air Force said, Russia launched 326 attack drones, 55 of them jet-powered, plus Gerberas and other drone types, as well as six S8000 Banderol/Dan-T small cruise missiles, which can be launched from aircraft and from drones. Air defence shot down or jammed 274 drones, 46 of them jet-powered, and all six Banderol/Dan-Ts; more than 20 attack drones were still airborne in the evening. No figure for hit locations was given for the daytime wave. Across roughly 25 hours this amounts to some 760 drones, a marked increase on the 134 by night and 168 by day reported for 30-31 October. The composition also shifted: jet-powered Shaheds made up about 15% of the night wave and 17% of the day wave, against more than half of the night wave a day earlier. By the Air Force's count, 47 of 64 jet drones were stopped overnight against 324 of the other 369 drones, and 46 of 55 jet drones by day; these are preliminary figures with drones still in the air. All attack and interception figures come from the Ukrainian Air Force, a party to the war, and have not been independently verified.

Russia has lost about 13 military aircraft since late September, Defence Blog tallies: seven Su-34s destroyed on the ground by Ukrainian strikes, a Tu-95MS and two Su-30SMs in crashes, and a Su-57 burned out at Akhtubinsk

2026-10-31

Defence Blog puts Russia's military aviation losses at about a dozen aircraft in roughly five weeks. It bases the count on the Ukrainian open-source project Oko Gora, which counted 11 Russian fighter aircraft and one helicopter lost in October, plus two Su-34s partially damaged. Adding the Tu-95MS that crashed on 29 September brings the total to about 13 airframes. The listed losses are: on 4 October, SBU drones hit a Su-34 and a Su-35 at Khanskaya air base in Adygea, about 500 km from Ukrainian-held territory, along with an ammunition depot; on the night of 14-15 October, Ukrainian Navy Neptune missiles struck Borisoglebsk air base (Voronezh Oblast), where satellite imagery showed four Su-34s destroyed and two badly damaged; and on 29 October another Neptune strike destroyed two Su-34s at Marinovka air base (Volgograd Oblast). Russia has confirmed only the two training-flight crashes and has not acknowledged losses from Ukrainian strikes. The crashes and other losses: on 29 September a Tu-95MS crashed on take-off near Ukrainka air base in Amur Oblast. Defence Blog says six of the seven crew were killed, although earlier reporting gave a higher death toll among strategic aviation personnel (see the earlier entry). It was the first Tu-95MS lost in a crash since 2015, and Russia no longer builds the type, which launches Kh-101 cruise missiles at Ukraine. On 21 October a Su-30SM crashed on a training flight in Kaliningrad Oblast, with both crew ejecting; Russian and Ukrainian outlets reported a second Su-30SM lost the same day. The Russian Knights aerobatic team later announced the deaths of two pilots, Col. Oleg Yerofeyev and Lt. Col. Vladimir Kochetov, without giving a cause; Ukrainian analysts link them to the second crash, which Russia has not confirmed. On 29 October a Su-57 caught fire during an engine start at the Akhtubinsk flight-test centre (Astrakhan Oblast) and burned out, according to the Ukrainian channel Absolutely Reliable Source, which published satellite imagery of a burn scar. If confirmed it would be the fourth Su-57 lost or damaged this year. On 30 October Ukraine's 3rd Assault Brigade said it destroyed a landed Mi-8 in Luhansk Oblast with two Hornet drones. The Su-34, Russia's main glide-bomb carrier, bore the brunt: seven destroyed in October, or nine counting the two damaged at Borisoglebsk. Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR) said on 22 October that Russia had about 140 Su-34s of all versions in service and that industry delivered 22 new ones between October 2025 and October 2026, per Ukrinform; October's losses thus equal about a third of a year's output. Oryx had visually confirmed 205 Russian aircraft destroyed or damaged since February 2022, with the Su-34 the most-lost type at 48. The individual incidents are covered in earlier entries; the overall tally relies largely on Ukrainian sources and satellite imagery, and several losses are not confirmed by Russia.

2026-10-30SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

Rybar says new modifications of the Geran one-way attack drone reached Russian drone units in October 2026. According to the channel, they carry munitions designed to cut through metal surfaces and cables, which made them look like the answer to Ukraine's cable-stayed bridges over the Dnipro. In practice, Rybar writes, the results have been limited. Over the past month the new drones struck Kyiv's cable-stayed Southern and Northern bridges many times. FAB glide bombs and cruise missiles aimed at crossings in Zaporizhzhia and Dnipropetrovsk had a similarly limited effect, it says. The channel puts this down less to weak munitions than to how robust such structures are. In its view, bringing down girder bridges, and even more so cable-stayed ones, requires systematic strikes on structural weak points to unbalance how the load is distributed. Rybar concludes that disrupting Ukraine's transport system does not require destroying every crossing, because even local damage restricts traffic and forces repairs. It argues that the priority is to keep up the tempo of strikes and steadily weaken the bridges so they become harder to repair. The existence and effect of the cutting-munition Geran variants are not independently confirmed. Dutch firm Destinus says it has built 1,750 Ruta B1 cruise missiles and now makes nearly 300 a month, more than Europe's annual output before 2022, while small jet engines remain the bottleneck for Ukraine's strike programmes

2026-10-30

Destinus, a Netherlands-headquartered defence company, said on 29 October that it has completed 1,750 Ruta B1 cruise missiles and reached a monthly output of nearly 300, which it told Reuters exceeds Europe's entire annual cruise-missile production before Russia's full-scale invasion, Euromaidan Press reports. The Ruta B1 (Block 1) has a range of about 300 km and runs on Destinus' own T150 turbojet; in June 2026 the company announced its 1,000th T150. Block 2, now in production, carries a 250 kg payload more than 700 km and is stored, transported and launched from the same container; Block 3 is planned with a 2,000 km range, with flight tests scheduled for 2027. Destinus is also working with Rheinmetall on cruise missiles and on rockets for multiple-launch systems, including a planned European equivalent of the GMLRS fired by HIMARS. The article stresses that engines, not airframes, are the constraint on Ukraine's deep-strike programmes. Reuters reported in April 2026 that a looming shortage of mini jet engines could hold back Ukraine's jet-powered strike drones; Czech maker ZofiTech was then producing about 200 engines a month, nearly all for Ukraine, against expected demand of thousands. Destinus says it expects to meet Europe's demand, including Ukraine's, for 1.5-kilonewton-class cruise-missile engines from next year, and CEO Mikhail Kokorich said it already builds such turbojets in series for itself and other manufacturers, but the company has not disclosed its engine output. Zelensky acknowledged the Ruta's combat use in October 2025, and in June 2026 then-Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov said the Netherlands was ready to finance about 700 Rutas for Ukraine. Kokorich argued that Europe's challenge is shifting from production to integration: sufficient deterrence would need 'tens of thousands of missiles', plus new units, logistics, storage, maintenance and targeting infrastructure. He said Destinus uses no Chinese components, which makes production slower and costlier, and predicted that cheaper missiles with ranges under 1,000 km will make up most future demand. The production and engine figures are company claims and have not been independently verified.
