Ukrainian expert: Russia's best missile defences ring Moscow, leaving other strategic sites exposed to Ukraine's emerging ballistic missiles

2026-09-23

Vadym Kushnikov, a military analyst with the Ukrainian defence portal Militarnyi, argued on Ukrainian Radio (reported 23 September 2026 and picked up by Ukrinform, UNIAN and Euromaidan Press) that Russia cannot protect all of its strategic sites against Ukrainian ballistic missiles. In his assessment, Russia does have systems capable of intercepting ballistic missiles — S-300, S-400 and the newer S-500 "Prometey" — but it has concentrated the most capable ones around the capital. He said the most modern S-500 complexes, with 77N6-N and 77N6-N1 interceptors derived from the Soviet-era A-135 Moscow missile-defence system, are deployed in Moscow Oblast. Elsewhere, Kushnikov said, Russia suffers a shortage of air-defence complexes, including S-300 and S-400, relative to the size of its territory — a gap he said is visible in satellite imagery, although exact inventories are not public. According to UNIAN's account, he also pointed to Russia having spent down its interceptor stocks, and concluded that even a strong air defence cannot cover every strategic object across such a vast country, leaving regions outside Moscow more exposed to massed and combined Ukrainian strikes. This matters as Ukraine's deep-strike arsenal shifts from foreign-supplied to domestic weapons: alongside the Neptune and Flamingo cruise missiles, Ukraine now fields the Sapsan, an Iskander-class ballistic missile with a range of about 500 km that President Zelenskyy confirmed in combat use in late 2025. A domestic ballistic missile would allow deep strikes into Russia without Western approval. Kushnikov was frank about the constraints on the Ukrainian side: production sites, many of them in eastern Ukraine, are direct targets of Russian strikes, and key components — notably solid-fuel rocket motors and guidance for a warhead travelling at over five times the speed of sound on a ballistic trajectory — are hard to manufacture; scaling up production is a separate challenge. He gave no concrete timeline for when Ukraine's ballistic missiles will be available in quantity. This is an expert opinion from a Ukrainian analyst, not an official assessment, and the claims about Russian deployments and shortages have not been independently verified.

Russia has adapted a decades-old aircraft-launched target drone into a mass-producible cruise missile called the Dan-T. According to RBC-Ukraine (cited by Euromaidan Press), the Dan-T began life as the Dan-M, a jet-powered target drone built by Russia's Sokol design bureau in the early 1990s to mimic US Tomahawk cruise missiles for air-defense training. To convert it into a weapon, Russia reportedly strips the recovery parachute and fits a warhead; the missile flies autonomously via satellite and inertial navigation over hundreds of kilometers. The striking figure in the piece is that Russia reportedly aims to build 14,000 Dan-T missiles next year, with roughly 2,000 units expected by the end of the current year. This 14,000 figure is a stated production target/plan, not a confirmed existing stockpile — the article does not attribute it to a named official or analyst, only to RBC-Ukraine's own reporting, so it should be treated as an estimate pending independent confirmation. For comparison, the article also notes Russia is separately producing the S8000 Banderol — another cheap cruise missile — at roughly 80 units per month, and suggests the Dan-T could partly replace it as a cheaper alternative. The article's core argument is that the danger from this weapon lies not in technological sophistication but in cost and volume: because the airframe design is over 30 years old and cheap to mass-produce, Russia can launch missiles "by the thousand," forcing Ukraine to expend scarce and expensive air-defense interceptors on comparatively low-cost targets — a saturation/cost-asymmetry tactic similar to that used with Shahed/Geran-series drones. Despite the "experts say" framing in the headline, no individual expert is quoted by name in the body of the article.

For Ukraine's air-defense posture, this matters because it would extend the operational life of Soviet-legacy S-300 batteries by feeding them Western-made replacement rounds. The sale underscores Washington's continued reliance on Ukraine-funded, rather than US-gifted, arms transfers and on the reconstruction fund as a financing vehicle, as Ukraine braces for another winter of Russian strikes on its power grid and air defence network.

For comparison, the baseline 9M723-1 reaches about 390 km and the stretched variant (9M723-2 / "Iskander-1000") up to roughly 550 km; the new system would reach up to 800 km. In role it would sit in the same class as quasi-ballistic short-range systems such as North Korea's KN-23, but with greater reach; it is a separate weapon from the intermediate-range Oreshnik. From Russian or Belarusian launch areas, 800 km would bring western Ukraine into range — Lviv, Volyn and the western border corridors that carry most incoming Western military aid — on a depressed, hard-to-intercept flight profile that stretches Ukrainian air defenses. Skibitskyi said Russian forces "may try to hit targets in western Ukraine during those tests," folding weapons trials into the wider campaign objective of intensifying missile and drone strikes to push Ukraine into a social and economic crisis. This is explicitly a Ukrainian-intelligence assessment: there is no independent confirmation, no imagery or production data, and the cited range and timeline figures are HUR estimates.

Zelensky and Norway's PM advance the ten-nation European FREYJA anti-ballistic programme

2026-09-08

FREYJA is a multinational European ballistic-missile-defense program, launched in Paris in July 2026 by a ten-nation coalition: Ukraine together with Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden and the United Kingdom. It aims to build an affordable, integrated European shield against ballistic and other air threats, reducing reliance on non-European systems. Ukraine's Fire Point builds the interceptor (the FP-7.x) and acts as system integrator, while European companies supply radars, sensors, seekers and fire control. On 11 September, EU Defence Commissioner Andrius Kubilius confirmed the European Commission is considering funding for FREYJA, possibly through the €90 billion Ukraine support loan or the €1.5 billion EDIP instrument — a response to Europe's interceptor shortage as US stockpiles and production capacity come under strain. Estimated cost per intercept is around $700,000, versus roughly $3.8 million for a Patriot missile. Specific national industry partners are now named: alongside Fire Point (Ukraine), these include Kongsberg (Norway), Weibel (Switzerland — notably not one of the original ten coalition countries), Saab (Sweden), Diehl Defence and Hensoldt (Germany), Thales and Safran (France), Sener (Spain) and Leonardo (Italy). First results are expected in 2027. Fire Point CEO Iryna Terekh gave an update at Poland's MSPO defense exhibition: the cost target for the FP-7X interceptor is $1 million per unit, with a "zero test" planned by the end of 2026 and a working prototype expected in the first half of 2027. Norway and Ukraine co-lead the steering group. In August, Fire Point already secured more than twelve agreements with European defense contractors for radar, guidance systems and other components; Terekh said she seeks "the best players in the market" who work at "Ukrainian speed," avoiding vendor lock-in or supplier-specific "kill switches."

A full-scale mock-up was first shown at the Arms and Security 2021 exhibition, and President Zelensky appeared alongside what was believed to be a Koral missile in an April 2026 video. According to the developer, Koral is a medium-range interceptor intended to engage aircraft and ballistic targets as part of a layered air-defense system, with a secondary ability to strike ground or surface targets that have a distinct radar signature. Euromaidan Press and Defence Blog report that the intended target set includes systems such as the Iskander and Kinzhal ballistic/aeroballistic missiles and Grad and Smerch multiple-launch rockets. If matured and produced at scale, an indigenous interceptor with a claimed anti-ballistic capability would reduce Ukraine's dependence on scarce Western systems such as the Patriot. Ukraine's Defence Intelligence discloses 155 Ruselectronics enterprises supplying components for Russian weapons

2026-09-08

Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR) has published data on 155 enterprises that are part of, or managed by, the Ruselectronics (Roselektronika) holding, a subsidiary of Russia's state corporation Rostec. The information was added to the "aggressor's military-industrial complex" section of HUR's War&Sanctions portal. The holding groups plants and research institutes specialising in electronic components, communications equipment, automated control systems, robotics, microwave electronics and telecommunications technology. According to HUR, these firms supply components for Russian ballistic and cruise missiles, UAVs, electronic warfare systems, military equipment, and radar and communications systems used across tactical, frontline and strategic aviation. HUR highlighted specific production roles: JSC NII Girikond makes precision potentiometers for Zircon hypersonic missiles; JSC NITSEVT supplies flight-data processing units for Iskander ballistic missiles; and JSC NPP Integral-V produces pulse and heating transformers for the 9M723 (Iskander) missile. The agency stressed that almost half of the holding's listed enterprises are not under sanctions by any coalition partner, and that even where sanctions exist (as against Girikond and NITSEVT) they have had little effect on overall output — which HUR frames not as proof that sanctions fail but as evidence that monitoring and enforcement mechanisms must be improved. The disclosure continues HUR's running effort, via the War&Sanctions portal, to map Russia's defence-industrial base and the foreign and domestic supply chains feeding it — earlier tranches covered foreign components found in Russian systems, the Pantsir-S1 supplier network, Su-35/Su-30 production, and named Russian scientists in military research. HUR calls for tighter supply-chain control to prevent sanctions circumvention, positioning the database as a targeting aid for Western sanctions authorities and OSINT researchers.
