Iran's Ballistic Missiles That Could Reach Ukraine — and What It Would Take for NATO's Aegis Ashore to Stop Them

2026-07-27SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express published two analytical pieces (27-28 July 2026) assessing what a direct Iranian ballistic-missile strike on Ukraine would look like, against the backdrop of Iranian threats (see the tracker's separate verified-stream entry on the NYT report about a possible Iranian strike on a Ukrainian port). Iran's arsenal reportedly includes the Ghadr (~2,000 km range), Emad (~1,800 km) and Sejjil (~2,000 km), and the Khorramshahr — the longest-ranged system at a reported ~4,000 km, enough to reach Ukraine from anywhere in Iran. A Khorramshahr variant is said to carry a submunition warhead dispersing up to 20 submunitions over an ~8 km-radius area. Defense Express states Iran's medium-range missile stockpile has been "significantly depleted" by exchanges with Israel and strikes on Iranian launchers. On interception, Defense Express argues countering such missiles generally requires dedicated systems like THAAD or Arrow-2/Arrow-3. The second piece widens to NATO's own shield: the two European Aegis Ashore sites — Deveselu (Romania) and Redzikowo (Poland) — were built starting in 2009 specifically to counter the Iranian threat. Were NATO to use these sites against an Iranian missile, Defense Express says it would be the first-ever combat use of Europe's ground-based missile shield. Both pieces come from a single Ukrainian outlet, Defense Express, and are explicitly analytical/technical assessments, not confirmed operational reporting; the scenario of a direct Iranian strike on Ukrainian territory remains speculative/hypothetical, based on Tehran's rhetoric rather than a reported attack. Missile-range, warhead, and interceptor figures are Defense Express's own technical estimates and are not independently verified.

Turkey Reportedly Eyes Egypt as S-400 Buyer to Resolve F-35 Impasse; Cairo Already Fields Russian S-300VM

2026-07-27SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express (author Ivan Kyrychevskiy) reports that Turkey is still seeking a buyer for its Russian S-400 air defense systems, acquired under a 2017 contract with Moscow and the direct cause of Turkey's exclusion from the US F-35 program. Egypt has reportedly emerged as a probable buyer, with talks said to have taken place when Egypt's defense minister visited Ankara. Turkish officials reportedly frame the deal not as a straightforward arms sale but as part of establishing broader military-technical cooperation with Cairo — viewing the transfer as a way to remove the obstacle blocking Turkey's return to the F-35 program. Egypt has operated the Russian S-300VM (Antey-2500) system for roughly a decade, only publicly unveiling it for the first time in early July 2026. Cairo claims the system is capable of intercepting ballistic missiles at ranges up to 40 kilometers, adding to its existing Russian-made inventory that includes Tor-M1 and Buk-M2 air defense systems. An eventual S-400 acquisition would further deepen Egypt's layered Russian-origin air defense posture. The article also flags an unresolved practical question: how Egypt would handle maintenance of any acquired S-400s and what role Russia would play — a point relevant to the wider dynamics of Russian arms-export leverage and technical dependency among third countries in the Middle East. This report rests on a single Ukrainian source (Defense Express) and has not been independently verified; the status of negotiations and any eventual deal remain unconfirmed.

The piece references an Iranian missile that evaded interceptors and struck King Faisal Air Base in Jordan, and an earlier Defense Express analysis on the contested effectiveness of Patriot air defense in the Gulf region. Lockheed Martin Unveils New Low-Cost ACE Missile for Patriot, Designed for Joint Production With Europe

2026-07-23SINGLE SOURCE

According to Ukrainian defense outlet Defense Express, Lockheed Martin unveiled the PAC-3 Adapted Capability Effector (ACE) at the Farnborough air show on 20 July 2026 — a new, lower-cost interceptor missile for the Patriot air-defense system. The article states the ACE reuses the same PAC-3 guidance system and fire-control software as the existing PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement (MSE) but at reportedly less than half the per-unit cost. Per the report, ACE is intended to match MSE's effectiveness against airbreathing threats, cruise missiles, and close/short-range ballistic missiles, though it reportedly lacks MSE's extended medium/longer-range ballistic-missile intercept capability. The article's central angle is the joint-production-with-Europe plan: Lockheed Martin reportedly wants to engage European industrial partners to accelerate development and reach mass production faster, aiming for a "shared, interoperable interceptor that strengthens transatlantic defense posture." Initial production could reportedly begin within about 36 months of the announcement, putting first deliveries around mid-to-late 2029. The article ties this to Lockheed's earlier European Patriot-related partnerships, including an MBDA-built factory in Germany producing older PAC-2 GEM-T rounds and a memorandum with Germany's Diehl Defence on the PAC-3 MSE supply chain. SINGLE SOURCE — this item is drawn from one Ukrainian outlet (Defense Express) and has not been independently corroborated by a second outlet or, insofar as the article itself does not directly cite one, by an official Lockheed Martin statement.

Why Does Russia Need More Than 40 MiG-31 Kinzhal Carriers if It Has Only About 100 Missiles?

2026-07-21SINGLE SOURCE

According to an analysis by Defense Express (Ivan Kyrychevskiy, July 20, 2026), Russia now operates more than 40 MiG-31K/I aircraft converted to carry the Kh-47M2 Kinzhal hypersonic missile, while the stockpile of that weapon stands at only around 100 units, according to Ukraine's Defense Intelligence directorate (HUR). Production runs at roughly five missiles per month, with a target of 60 for all of 2026, and the weapon has reportedly been used only eight times so far this year. HUR says Russia has deliberately slowed both production and operational use of the Kinzhal because of its high cost and relatively limited combat effectiveness. The number of Kinzhal-capable MiG-31s grew from 24 at the end of 2023 (per The Military Balance) to 40+ now, an increase of at least 16 airframes over two and a half years. Since Russia no longer manufactures new MiG-31 interceptors, these additional carriers almost certainly come from long-term storage stock, estimated at 130-150 airframes as of 2018. The newest variant, the MiG-31I, adds automatic navigation to the missile launch point as its main improvement over the earlier MiG-31K. The author himself calls the logic behind this fleet expansion 'questionable': with 40+ carriers against roughly 100 missiles in stock plus an expected production of about 30 more this year, Russia effectively has only about three missiles available per aircraft. The article exposes this mismatch but does not offer a firm explanation for the scale-up itself — it frames it mainly as an open question about Russian weapons logistics. Note: this is a single-source analysis from Defense Express, a Ukrainian military-analysis outlet, and its figures and conclusions have not yet been independently corroborated by a second source.

How Aster Missile Licensing Could Pave the Way for Ukraine to Get Comet Air-to-Air Missile with 200–300 km Range

2026-07-20SINGLE SOURCE

In mid-July 2026, France pledged to license Ukraine for domestic production of the Aster missile (used in the SAMP/T air-defense system), alongside SCALP cruise missiles and AASM Hammer precision-guided bombs. President Macron announced this at a press conference in Paris on the sidelines of a "Coalition of the Willing" meeting, as part of a broader package that also includes the delivery of 16 Rafale fighter jets. Ukrainian defense outlet Defense Express argues in an analysis piece that this Aster license could open the door to an entirely different weapon system: the "Comet," a new long-range air-to-air missile reportedly being developed by MBDA based on Aster technology and components, with a claimed range of 200-300 km, exceeding that of the Meteor missile. Comet is expected to be ready around 2030 and is specifically intended for the Rafale F5 variant. Since Aster is already set to be produced under license in Ukraine, Defense Express reasons that Ukraine's defense industry could plausibly join Comet's development or production. SINGLE SOURCE — this is a single-source analysis/speculation from Defense Express, not a confirmed program. There is no official confirmation — from France, MBDA, or Ukraine's Ministry of Defense — of any actual deal, licensing agreement, or development track involving Ukraine in the Comet missile. The cited specifications for Comet (200-300 km range, ~2030 readiness) are also unverified.
