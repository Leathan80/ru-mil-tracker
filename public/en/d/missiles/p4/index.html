Ukraine's hidden RAM-2X strike drone: maker claims over 350 Russian systems destroyed

2026-09-17

Ukraine Fields Italian-Swiss Skyguard/Aspide Air Defense System, Built Around a Cold War Sparrow-Derived Missile

2026-09-16

Ukraine's 38th Anti-Aircraft Missile Regiment has released the first combat footage of a Skyguard/Aspide air-defense system in operational use, showing crews loading missiles and the system standing watch. The Skyguard/Aspide combines a Swiss-made fire-control unit — originally from Oerlikon Contraves, now part of Rheinmetall Air Defence — with Italian-built Aspide missiles. The system can engage targets at ranges of roughly 12 to 25 km and altitudes from 15 meters up to 6,000 meters, and is designed against aircraft, helicopters, cruise missiles and drones. The Aspide missile itself traces its lineage to the American AIM-7 Sparrow, a radar-guided weapon whose development the US Navy began in the late 1940s for Cold War-era bomber interception; Aspide is essentially an Italian evolution of that mid-20th-century design, adapted for today's threats. Ukraine received its Skyguard/Aspide systems as Western aid: Spain began deliveries in November 2022, after training 19 Ukrainian personnel a month earlier at its Zaragoza base, and Italy pledged further units in February 2023. Italian-supplied variants reportedly differ from the Spanish ones, carrying target-illumination radar and TV-tracking equipment mounted directly on the launcher. The deployment comes as Ukraine faces a widening shortage of higher-end interceptors: Patriot missile deliveries are reported to have fallen from 675 units in 2023 to a projected 264 in 2026. Short-range systems such as Skyguard/Aspide are being used against drones and cruise missiles, letting Ukraine conserve its scarce Patriot interceptors for ballistic-missile threats — a trade-off made more pressing as Ukraine enters its fifth consecutive winter of Russian strikes on energy and other infrastructure.

How Ukraine's Koral Surface-to-Air Missile, Now 50% Heavier, Could Intercept Ballistic Missiles

2026-09-10SINGLE SOURCE

On 10 September 2026, Ukrainian trade outlet Defense Express published an analytical piece on the Koral surface-to-air missile, which had already been publicly displayed a few days earlier (8–9 September) at the MSPO 2026 defense exhibition in Kielce — a reveal recorded separately elsewhere in this tracker (verified stream, based on Militarnyi, Euromaidan Press and Defence Blog reporting). Where that earlier coverage focused on the unveiling itself and the already-known specifications (450 kg, 36 kg warhead, 4.8 m length, 230/300 mm diameter), Defense Express puts these figures in context by comparing them to the missile's original 2021 configuration (300 kg, 4.33 m, 230/260 mm diameter) — hence the "50% heavier" framing. The outlet then examines the technical basis for the claim that Koral can intercept ballistic targets, concluding that the missile lacks a ring of gas-dynamic lateral-thruster controls — a feature typically associated with "hit-to-kill" interceptors capable of destroying ballistic targets through direct kinetic impact. Instead, Koral relies on inertial navigation with radio correction plus an active radar homing seeker in the terminal phase, combined with a proximity fuze and fragmentation effect. On that basis, Defense Express places Koral closer to the US PAC-2 GEM-T than to the hit-to-kill PAC-3 family, naming the PAC-3 ACE as the closest comparison point. The article does not quote any named officials or independent experts; the analysis rests solely on publicly displayed specifications. This development has been reported only by Ukrainian trade outlet Defense Express and has not been independently confirmed.

Ukraine unveils indigenous Shershen SAM with R-120K missile and Ai-Petri anti-glide-bomb EW system at MSPO 2026

2026-09-09

At the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Kielce, Poland, Ukrainian company Radionix presented its modular "Shershen" (Hornet) air-defence system, built around the Perun command-and-control vehicle, the Athena surveillance/tracking radar (detection range roughly 85 km) and four Borei launchers per battery. A more detailed report from The War Zone (TWZ, 10 September 2026) confirms and sharpens the picture: the unit on display was the second operational prototype and is described as "close to its production configuration." The design's core feature is a deliberately open architecture — the manufacturer states the system is "deliberately open... to use different missiles from different manufacturers" — letting the launcher fire both Soviet air-to-air missiles (R-27/AA-10, R-73/AA-11, R-60) and Western types (AIM-9 Sidewinder, AIM-132 ASRAAM), alongside the new, Ukrainian-developed R-120K. Unlike Shershen, Ai-Petri is not an exhibition concept: development began in 2014, tactical versions have been fielded since 2024, and the manufacturer claims around 400 systems delivered. As with Shershen, its claimed effectiveness against glide bombs remains independently unverified.

Rostec says the weapon draws on design work in both cruise missiles and unmanned aviation, has completed all development stages, and has been tested on a range and in combat in Ukraine, claiming "hundreds" of launches against real targets — a developer claim with no independent verification. Russian coverage frames the item as sitting "between a missile and a drone." Russian sources (Rostec via RIA Novosti and TASS) give a launch weight of up to 270 kg and a range of up to 450 km, with unofficial estimates reaching 500 km. A compact turbojet provides a subsonic cruise, a ceiling of around 9 km and a speed of roughly 750 km/h. Guidance is an autopilot with combined satellite and inertial navigation; in its current form the weapon can only strike targets at known coordinates, functioning as a small cruise missile rather than a true loiter-and-search weapon. Analysts estimate a warhead near 135 kg, which the Topwar author considers implausibly high and which Rostec has not confirmed. The stated rationale is cost and mass: a cheap, simple small missile to saturate enemy air defences and force the expenditure of expensive SAMs — the same niche as Banderol. Ukrainian intelligence-based estimates cited in Russian coverage suggest Russia could build up to ~10,000 such missiles in 2027. Ukraine develops RK-19 missile to sustain Soviet-era Osa-AKM (SA-8) air defence

2026-09-09

Ukraine has developed the RK-19, a new surface-to-air missile intended to replace the dwindling stock of original 9M33-series missiles for the Soviet-era Osa-AKM system (NATO designation SA-8 Gecko). Per the sources, the missile was designed by the Luch Design Bureau, part of the state conglomerate Ukroboronprom. It was first referenced in an April 2026 video address by President Zelensky and shown publicly at the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Poland. Production volumes and pricing have not been disclosed, indicating the weapon is in development or early production rather than fielded at scale. The stated specifications — all Ukrainian-side/developer claims, not independently verified — are: a range of up to 10 km, a maximum engagement altitude of up to 5 km, and a top speed of 600 m/s, versus roughly 500 m/s for the standard 9M33M3. Guidance remains radio-command with a proximity fuze, identical in principle to the original Soviet design, allowing the Osa vehicle's existing radar and fire-control suite to be retained unchanged. The warhead weighs 14.3 kg (high-explosive fragmentation); the transport-launch container with missile weighs 163 kg (against about 157 kg for the standard round), with dimensions of 3,255 x 320 x 303 mm. In engagement terms the RK-19 is essentially equivalent to the original, its main gain being higher speed — an incremental upgrade, not a redesign. The wider point is sustaining legacy Soviet short-range air defence. Ukrainian Osa systems performed well in 2022 and part of 2023, but it is the missiles, not the launchers, that are running out. A domestically built interceptor lets those dozens of existing vehicles keep engaging precisely the targets now saturating the airspace: jet-powered attack drones and cruise missiles, including — per the sources — Russia's Banderol. For a daily tracker this is an indicator both of Ukrainian weapons self-sufficiency and of the strain on air-defence munition stocks; its operational value depends entirely on whether the claimed performance and production capacity are borne out in practice.

SINGLE SOURCE — Russian sources (a regional governor and state media RT), relayed and critically framed by Defence Blog. This report could not be independently confirmed. According to Kursk Region Governor Aleksandr Khinshtein and Russian state media (RT), a mobile air-defense team from the volunteer BARS-Kursk brigade shot down a Ukrainian Flamingo (FP-5) cruise missile with gunfire overnight on Saturday-Sunday, August 22-23, 2026. After an air-raid alert, the team reportedly spotted a 'huge' missile flying at roughly 500 km/h at an altitude below 50 meters, hit it with small-arms fire, causing it to lose altitude and crash into a wooded area. Russia says mission data recovered from the wreckage showed the missile — with a stated range of 3,000 km and a warhead equivalent to about one ton of TNT — was aimed at a 'strategic facility' in Tula Region. Technically the claim is striking: the Flamingo is a large ground-launched cruise missile developed by Ukraine's Fire Point, weighing about 6,000 kg at launch with a roughly 1,150 kg warhead, and is normally countered with air-defense systems or fighter aircraft rather than small arms. Observers have noted that the released thermal-camera footage is 'pretty ambiguous,' that the reported speed is below the Flamingo's stated cruise speed, and that no independent party has confirmed that gunfire — rather than, say, a surface-to-air system — actually brought the missile down. Ukraine has not confirmed the incident. The claim fits a broader Russian narrative of successful Flamingo intercepts. Ukraine has used the missile against targets deep inside Russia since its unveiling in August 2025, including earlier hits on the VNIIR-Progress plant (June 2026) and the Avitek plant (July 2026). Open-source trackers such as AMK Mapping claim Russia has intercepted 10 of 11 Flamingos launched since July 1 — but neither Kyiv nor Moscow has released official figures, so this broader interception claim is likewise unverifiable independently.

US to develop new RIM-7 Sea Sparrow modification used in Ukrainian-American FrankenSAM

2026-08-22SINGLE SOURCE

SINGLE SOURCE — Ukrainian publication (Defense Express), not independently corroborated. The U.S. Navy is pursuing a next-generation variant of the Evolved Sea Sparrow Missile (ESSM/RIM-162), which itself evolved from the RIM-7 Sea Sparrow — the missile Ukraine fires from its FrankenSAM air defense system. According to Defense Express, the Navy issued a "Sources Sought" notice to gauge industry interest in developing and producing a new "Guided Missile Assembly," with a proposal deadline of October 19, 2026. The effort would be releasable to all navies in the NATO SeaSparrow consortium (the U.S., Australia, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Germany, Greece, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain and Turkey). The stated requirements call for a missile able to engage targets across a range of speeds and altitudes — from sea-skimming threats to high-diving aircraft — and to counter highly maneuverable, high-G targets in contested RF, electro-optical and infrared environments, while discriminating between closely-spaced targets and resisting countermeasures. This points to a significant guidance and target-discrimination upgrade. Physically, the new variant is expected to retain the current 10-inch (25.4 cm) diameter to remain compatible with Mk41 vertical launch cells (four missiles per cell), broadly matching today's ESSM (RIM-162): roughly 280 kg, 3.66 m long, a 39 kg warhead, range over 50 km and speeds above Mach 4. The push appears driven by the need to replace aging missile stocks and improve guidance against modern threats. For Ukraine, the FrankenSAM link is notable: since 2024 Ukraine has fired RIM-7 Sea Sparrow missiles from converted Soviet-era Buk-M1 launchers, pairing Western missiles with Soviet air-defense platforms to offset a shortage of original Buk munitions. It remains unclear, however, whether or when a future ESSM successor would become relevant to Ukraine's actual inventory: the current announcement is only an exploratory market-research notice with a proposal deadline in October 2026, missile development and production programs typically take years, and there is no indication this specific effort will alter Ukraine's existing FrankenSAM systems in the near term. The operational impact on Ukrainian air defense therefore remains speculative for now.

Germany's Cobra 600 Jet Drone Completes First Flight, Armed With IRIS-T Missile, Creators Hope It Can Shoot Down russian Su-34 KAB Carriers

2026-08-22SINGLE SOURCE

SINGLE SOURCE — Ukrainian publication (Defense Express), not independently corroborated. German defense contractor Diehl Defence, working with startup POLARIS Raumflugzeuge, has developed the Cobra 600, a reusable jet-powered interceptor drone with a takeoff mass of 600 kilograms. The first test flight took place in the second half of August 2026 over the Baltic Sea, covering roughly 20 kilometers; the flight carried no payload or missile simulator. Speed, range, and cost figures have not yet been disclosed by the manufacturer. The drone is designed to carry and launch the IRIS-T air-to-air missile, functioning as a kind of 'missile taxi' meant to extend the reach of ground-based air defense systems. Developers hope that its small size, low observability, and sufficient flight range will allow it to intercept Russian Su-34 fighter-bombers carrying KAB glide bombs, one of the principal threats to Ukrainian troops and cities. Defense Express itself stresses that this capability remains purely hypothetical for now: the initial flight involved neither a missile nor a payload simulator, and further tests with IRIS-T simulators and eventually the live missile are planned for coming months. Operational readiness and actual effectiveness against Su-34s are therefore entirely unproven at this stage.

Why Did russian Ka-52 Helicopter Fail to Shoot Down Ukrainian FP-1 UAV with Missile, What Alternatives do russians Have

2026-08-21SINGLE SOURCE

SINGLE SOURCE — Ukrainian publication (Defense Express), not independently corroborated. Video footage published on 21 August 2026 shows a Russian Ka-52 attack helicopter crew attempting to shoot down a Ukrainian long-range strike drone, the FP-1, with an air-to-air missile. The missile flew past the target and missed; the crew then switched to cannon fire in an attempt to destroy the drone. Defense Express identifies the missile used, based on the caption shown in the video, as an Igla-V (or possibly Igla-S), but notes that the footage alone does not allow reliable confirmation of which missile type was actually fired. According to Defense Express, the technical reason for the miss lies in the infrared seeker of the Igla-family missiles, which can struggle to lock onto targets emitting little heat. The FP-1, a slow-flying, long-range drone with a small thermal signature, falls into that category. The outlet frames this as a broader weakness of infrared-guided air-to-air missiles against this type of strike drone. As an alternative, Defense Express points to the Vikhr anti-tank guided missile in an air-to-air modification with a non-contact fuse and semi-active laser guidance, where the helicopter crew guides the missile to the target itself — potentially increasing the chance of hitting targets like the FP-1. Cannon fire remains a fallback option but requires more time and effort. The publication suggests Russia may default to Igla systems due to existing stockpiles, while the Vikhr modification requires additional production. Independent confirmation of the incident, the missile identification, and the stated limitations is lacking.

Sources: Defense Express

The claim (10 MiG-29 aircraft in one day, 24 aircraft in one week) rests solely on Russia's own Ministry of Defense journal and has not been independently verified by Ukrainian or third-party sources; secondary reporting does not specify the exact VM issue number/date or the precise date in 2023. Notable mainly as a rare official acknowledgment that Ukraine has since recognized and countered the tactic.
