Ukraine claims deepest-ever drone strike, hitting gas-condensate plant at New Urengoy beyond the Urals

2026-09-09

On 9 September 2026 Ukraine's General Staff claimed a drone strike on the Novy Urengoy Gas Condensate Stabilization Plant in the Yamalo-Nenets autonomous district, east of the Urals. Sources put the distance from Ukrainian-controlled territory at roughly 2,000 km in a straight line, with estimates ranging from about 1,700 to 3,200 km. The War Zone and Ukrainian sources call it the deepest Ukrainian drone strike of the war, surpassing the July 2026 hit on the Omsk refinery. The plant has an annual capacity of about 19.5 million tons and produces stabilized gas condensate, petroleum fractions and diesel; Kyiv framed it as a facility supplying the Russian armed forces. Regional governor Dmitry Artyukhov confirmed a fire caused by falling drone debris, reported no casualties, and said damage was being assessed; Russian officials disputed Ukrainian responsibility and characterized the attack as an attempt to sow panic. The drone type is unconfirmed: TWZ points to the Fire Point FP-1 (claimed range over 3,000 km, 60 kg payload) based on hints from the manufacturer's founder, but no launch site or route was disclosed and there is no independent confirmation of damage or attribution. Airspace restrictions and temporary airport closures were imposed. Rybar — a Russian milblogger channel writing in a personal, editorial voice (not a Defence Ministry outlet) — argues that the idea of a "safe deep rear" is dissolving further and that the main effect so far is informational: panic and heavy Ukrainian media amplification. It also stresses the cumulative cost of repeated "Kover" airspace-closure plans across dozens of localities between Ukraine and Novy Urengoy, which halt air traffic. Drones flying some 3,000 km unimpeded over Russian territory, Rybar says, cannot be countered with "fire extinguishers" alone, and it calls for systematic threat and route analysis plus layered interception. The strike fits a broader 2026 pattern of lengthening Ukrainian attacks on Russian energy infrastructure. This is a single strike and the actual damage remains unverified.

Russian marketplace sellers shift to own warehouses after drone strikes as trade ministry downplays damage

2026-09-09

Sellers on the Russian online marketplaces Wildberries and Ozon have shifted heavily toward storing and shipping goods from their own warehouses (the so-called FBS model) rather than relying on the platforms' own fulfillment centers. At Wildberries, the share of sellers using FBS rose to 97.4% by the end of August 2026 — nearly doubling — while at Ozon it grew by 7.8 percentage points to 73.1%. Wildberries itself acknowledges 'growing seller interest' in FBS, but says sellers are mainly trying to combine both models, since the platform's own warehouses still offer faster delivery for goods with steady demand. The shift follows a sustained Ukrainian drone campaign against Russian e-commerce logistics infrastructure that has run since mid-2026, first targeting major Wildberries facilities and later Ozon warehouses as well. By early August, cumulative losses to Wildberries and affected sellers were estimated at 'hundreds of billions of rubles,' and in late August the Russian government approved support measures, including tax-payment deferrals for both sellers and the platforms. Russia's Ministry of Industry and Trade, meanwhile, is downplaying the need for full reconstruction. Deputy Minister Roman Chekushov said not all damaged logistics facilities need to be rebuilt, and that by the end of 2026 the ministry will have clarity on how logistics will be restructured. This points to a deliberate restructuring of the sector rather than straightforward reconstruction — and to a degree of official minimization of the strikes' cumulative toll. Note that this framing comes from a Russian government official; the seller-behavior figures were independently reported by Meduza.

Ukraine's KhKBM says it built ~600 BTR-4s under fire and unveils the self-funded Pegas IFV at MSPO 2026

2026-09-09

At the MSPO defence exhibition in Kielce, Poland, the Kharkiv Morozov Machine Building Design Bureau (KhKBM/KMDB) said it has built roughly 600 BTR-4 8x8 armoured personnel carriers since the start of 2022, having relocated production out of Kharkiv beyond Russian strike range and worked through repeated attacks on Ukraine's defence industry. Company representatives put annual capacity at about 200 vehicles and said the BTR-4E is roughly 80% Ukrainian-made by component count. The 600 figure is a company statement and is not independently confirmed. The resilience story has a flip side: KhKBM also said the state is not currently ordering BTR-4Es. The last existing state order was completed in mid-2026, and on-hand component stocks are enough for only another 100-200 hulls. The plant has kept producing under fire, but faces an order drought that threatens continuity. KhKBM separately unveiled the Pegas, a new tracked IFV of about 35 tonnes that it has been developing on its own initiative since 2024 using company funds, with no state financing. Intended to replace Soviet BMP-1/BMP-2 vehicles, it is designed for STANAG 4569 Level 4 all-round protection and Level 6 across the frontal arc, with planned armament via the Topaz module (30 mm cannon, coaxial 7.62 mm, two ATGMs, 30 mm automatic grenade launcher) plus planned anti-UAV electronic warfare. A mock-up was displayed at MSPO and a first prototype is reported to be about half assembled. All specifications are design intent; nothing is combat-proven.

Ukrainian intelligence: Russia to test new ballistic missile with ~800 km range, possibly by striking western Ukraine

2026-09-09

Ukraine's military intelligence directorate (HUR) says Russia is preparing final tests of a new ballistic missile with a range of up to roughly 800 kilometers before the end of 2026. The statement was made by Major General Vadym Skibitskyi, deputy head of the HUR, at a session of the Ukraine-NATO Interparliamentary Council on 8 September 2026. The HUR attributes development to the Moscow Institute of Thermal Technology (MITT), the design bureau behind the Yars, Topol-M, Bulava, Iskander-M and Oreshnik systems. There is no Russian acknowledgement and no designation or type name for the new missile has been disclosed. Technically, the HUR frames the weapon as a range-extension step above the Iskander family. For comparison, the baseline 9M723-1 reaches about 390 km and the stretched variant (9M723-2 / "Iskander-1000") up to roughly 550 km; the new system would reach up to 800 km. In role it would sit in the same class as quasi-ballistic short-range systems such as North Korea's KN-23, but with greater reach; it is a separate weapon from the intermediate-range Oreshnik. From Russian or Belarusian launch areas, 800 km would bring western Ukraine into range — Lviv, Volyn and the western border corridors that carry most incoming Western military aid — on a depressed, hard-to-intercept flight profile that stretches Ukrainian air defenses. Skibitskyi said Russian forces "may try to hit targets in western Ukraine during those tests," folding weapons trials into the wider campaign objective of intensifying missile and drone strikes to push Ukraine into a social and economic crisis. He urged partner states to tighten sanctions on the foreign components used in Russia's long-range weapons. This is explicitly a Ukrainian-intelligence assessment: there is no independent confirmation, no imagery or production data, and the cited range and timeline figures are HUR estimates.

HUR: Russia failed to substitute Western chips in the Kinzhal missile and reverted to US-made processors

2026-09-08

According to Vladyslav Vlasiuk, the Ukrainian president's commissioner for sanctions policy, Russia's effort to substitute Western microelectronics in the Kh-47M2 Kinzhal missile with domestic chips has failed. In 2023 the Russian defence industry attempted a mass replacement of American Texas Instruments processors with locally produced equivalents, but a Zarya-61 (Zorya-61) onboard-computer block recovered from a Kinzhal shot down in May 2026 was again found to contain American Texas Instruments processors. Vlasiuk said Russian analogues "did not produce the necessary results," and that Moscow now has to combine its own component base with foreign parts sourced through various markets and supply chains. The finding fits a wider pattern of legacy and shadow-supply-chain sourcing: examined Iskander debris contained an AMD microchip made in 1996 and a Texas Instruments processor marked 1986, while other Ukrainian teardowns cited alongside this report found 96 foreign components in a Geran-4 drone downed on 12 August 2026 (seven of them manufactured in 2026) and 147 foreign components from the US, Switzerland, Germany, China and Japan in an Iskander-M examined in July. Ukrainian Defence Intelligence has separately mapped 155 firms under the Roselektronika holding, with sanctions imposed on roughly half. Caveats: the claim is essentially single-source, resting on Vlasiuk's public statement (carried via Interfax-Ukraine and multiple outlets on 8 September 2026). No independent verification, imagery of the boards, or technical explanation of why the domestic processors underperformed has been released. The report also does not quantify current Kinzhal production rates, and some outlets date the relevant board's assembly to 2025 with components from 2021-2024, so the exact provenance of the recovered electronics remains partly uncertain.

Ukraine's Defence Intelligence discloses 155 Ruselectronics enterprises supplying components for Russian weapons

2026-09-08

Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR) has published data on 155 enterprises that are part of, or managed by, the Ruselectronics (Roselektronika) holding, a subsidiary of Russia's state corporation Rostec. The information was added to the "aggressor's military-industrial complex" section of HUR's War&Sanctions portal. The holding groups plants and research institutes specialising in electronic components, communications equipment, automated control systems, robotics, microwave electronics and telecommunications technology. According to HUR, these firms supply components for Russian ballistic and cruise missiles, UAVs, electronic warfare systems, military equipment, and radar and communications systems used across tactical, frontline and strategic aviation. HUR highlighted specific production roles: JSC NII Girikond makes precision potentiometers for Zircon hypersonic missiles; JSC NITSEVT supplies flight-data processing units for Iskander ballistic missiles; and JSC NPP Integral-V produces pulse and heating transformers for the 9M723 (Iskander) missile. The agency stressed that almost half of the holding's listed enterprises are not under sanctions by any coalition partner, and that even where sanctions exist (as against Girikond and NITSEVT) they have had little effect on overall output — which HUR frames not as proof that sanctions fail but as evidence that monitoring and enforcement mechanisms must be improved. The disclosure continues HUR's running effort, via the War&Sanctions portal, to map Russia's defence-industrial base and the foreign and domestic supply chains feeding it — earlier tranches covered foreign components found in Russian systems, the Pantsir-S1 supplier network, Su-35/Su-30 production, and named Russian scientists in military research. HUR calls for tighter supply-chain control to prevent sanctions circumvention, positioning the database as a targeting aid for Western sanctions authorities and OSINT researchers.

Western partners converge on Patriot for Ukraine ahead of winter: UK £100m package, EU €3.2bn, German PAC-2 drawdown

2026-09-08

Around the 8 September 2026 Ramstein-format meeting (Ukraine Defense Contact Group), Western partners announced a coordinated boost to Ukrainian air defense, explicitly timed for the winter. The United Kingdom committed a roughly £100 million (over €100 million) 'winter' air-defense package including Patriot interceptor missiles, large-caliber artillery shells and drones. London also confirmed a full-year pledge of more than 120,000 drones (25,000 already delivered, more before winter) across interceptor, reconnaissance and strike types, and is coordinating long-range artillery ammunition deliveries with Germany, Denmark, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway and Sweden. On financing, the European Commission approved Ukraine's application for approximately €3.2 billion for Patriot missiles and critical components under the Ukraine Support Loan framework. This required waiving the rule that such funds may only buy weapons from EU manufacturers, since Patriot is a US-made system. The money is meant to replenish stockpiles ahead of the heating season, with emphasis on intercepting the ballistic missiles Russia fires at cities and the power grid. Germany pledged PAC-2 missiles from its own Bundeswehr stockpile for immediate use, with Defense Minister Pistorius telling Ramstein: 'if in doubt, decide in favor of Ukraine.' The defining feature of the cluster is the gap between orders and deliveries. Ukraine is contracting for roughly 1,000 Patriot missiles, paid for with partner support and EU credit funds, but Ukrainian defence official Yevhen Khmara says most will arrive only 'in the coming years.' Kyiv therefore formally asked partners to transfer missiles from their existing reserves now, with later compensation out of contracted production. The stakes tie directly to Russia's expected winter campaign of mass drone and missile strikes on Ukraine's energy grid: new production contracts only provide coverage years out, so protection this winter depends on drawdowns from existing Western inventories.

Romania says it disrupted a Russia-run sabotage-reconnaissance operation scouting NATO bases and An-124 airlifters

2026-09-08

Romania's domestic intelligence service (SRI) announced on 8 September 2026 that it had disrupted a Russia-coordinated sabotage operation on Romanian territory. A Russian citizen — identified by Romanian media as a man from Novosibirsk — had been under surveillance since February 2026, roughly seven months, as part of a wider investigation into sabotage attempts in the country. According to the SRI, an intermediary instructed him to take photos and videos of targets of strategic military interest. The targets named include NATO-used military bases, communications nodes, defense institutions and critical infrastructure. Notably, the man was specifically tasked against Ukraine's Antonov An-124 "Ruslan" heavy cargo aircraft — the strategic airlift that moves weapons and equipment into Ukraine — giving the case a direct connection to the supply chain sustaining Kyiv. The SRI assessed that the tradecraft (reconnaissance and imagery by a recruited operative, run through a cut-out intermediary) matched sabotage networks attributed to Russian handlers elsewhere in Europe, and pointed to an incident at Leipzig airport where an explosives-rigged drone was found near an An-124. The case fits a sustained run of arson, drone incursions and covert surveillance across NATO's eastern flank. Details of formal charges or expulsion had not been released at publication; the man was detained in Romania. The information comes from the SRI and Romanian and Ukrainian media.

Ukraine says a domestic engine for the 'Bars' jet strike drone passed final tests; production moving to Europe, but turbojet supply still caps output

2026-09-07

A Ukrainian developer has detailed upgraded specifications for the "Bars" modular jet-powered strike drone (effectively a drone-missile for deep strikes into Russia). In its longest-range configuration it is now quoted at a range of up to 1,000 km, a warhead of up to 60 kg and a speed of more than 700 km/h, against a previous 700 km and 22.5 kg. Speaking to RBC-Ukraine, the manufacturer said the airframe has gone through more than 40 engineering revisions since development began in 2023 and is offered in several mission configurations (deep-strike, mid-strike, front-strike). It is launched with a solid-fuel booster; in the terminal phase the operator can choose between manual control and optical / machine-vision guidance. All performance figures come from the manufacturer and have not been independently verified. Production is being partly relocated to Europe. This builds on an implementation agreement Ukraine and Germany signed on 8 July 2026 on the margins of the NATO summit in Ankara, under the "Build with Ukraine" initiative: Germany finances the first phase of production and every unit built under it goes to Ukraine's Defence Forces. A co-production facility in Europe is reported to have begun operating in early September 2026, with the first batch expected around 10 October 2026; the site and industrial partner were not named. The key new development is the powerplant. The turbojet is described as the only major component still imported — previously the developer said engines were "sourced from all over the world" — and a Ukrainian-designed compact turbojet has now passed its final tests, though no manufacturer, thrust or performance data has been released. The company puts current localisation at more than 74% Ukrainian-made components and expects the domestic engine to raise that figure substantially. According to Defense Express — a single-source Ukrainian outlet whose specific claims warrant caution — the main constraint on scaling up Bars output is not assembly capacity but turbojet-engine lead times: Ukrainian jet-drone and cruise-missile makers depend on a small number of European suppliers whose combined annual output runs only to a few hundred units.

Defense Express: how fast a cheap interceptor missile against jet drones can reach production — the Frankenburg case

2026-09-06SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express uses the Estonian defense startup Frankenburg Technologies (founded 2024, backed by roughly €40 million after a €30 million Series A in February 2026) as a case study in how quickly a cheap interceptor missile against jet-powered attack drones can go from idea to serial production. The product is the Mark I: a compact solid-fuel missile weighing under 2 kg, 65 cm long and 60 mm in diameter, with an imaging seeker, an engagement range of about 2 km and an altitude capability of roughly 1.5 km. It reaches high subsonic speed and is meant to engage targets flying up to 600 km/h — the speed band of jet-powered Geran-4/Geran-5 and Shahed variants. Per Defense Express, unit cost is "significantly cheaper than a Shahed-type attack drone," and the missile can be fired from aircraft, drones and ground-based launchers. The timeline argument is the heart of the piece. A first €1.5 million investment came in October 2024; February 2025 brought an R&D contract from Latvia's Ministry of Defence and the start of test launches; by summer 2025 the launcher, a reliable missile and a working warhead were complete. In December 2025 the system achieved its first successful interception of a NATO Class III fixed-wing drone, roughly thirteen months after active development began. The first production line opened in Riga in June 2026. Planned volumes: 1,500 missiles in 2026, rising to 100 per day by end-2026, with additional plants envisioned in Estonia, the UK and Poland and an ultimate target of one million units per year. The employment logic links Ukraine to Baltic counter-UAS demand: Russia is said by Defense Express to be building around 3,000 jet-powered Geran drones per month, forcing a shift to mass, low-cost interception rather than spending expensive air-defense missiles on expendable drones. This summary rests on a single source, Defense Express, a Ukrainian outlet; its performance, cost and production claims are not independently verified and the timeline is largely drawn from the manufacturer itself.

Sources: Defense Express

«Дороже самого удара» — «Герани» против украинской экспортной логистики (Rybar: Costlier than the strike itself — Geran drones vs. Ukrainian export logistics)

2026-08-25SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

This is a post from the Russian pro-government Telegram channel Rybar (a milblogger), not an official Russian source. It represents a personal/editorial voice; milbloggers can contradict each other and the Russian Ministry of Defence, and the claims below have not been independently verified. Rybar claims that following an overnight Russian drone strike (Geran-type), the Vinogradovka–Vulcănești border crossing between Ukraine and Moldova has been closed, after earlier strikes on the crossings at Reni and Tabaky. According to the channel, the damage from these repeated, individually limited strikes accumulates into a larger economic problem: carriers are forced to reroute to neighboring crossings, causing longer queues and added strain on infrastructure, while tariffs increasingly bake in extra costs for fuel, downtime, insurance and the risk of sudden rerouting. This is said to be especially painful for Ukrainian agricultural exports, where low margins and delivery deadlines leave little room to pass costs on to buyers, resulting either in bigger discounts on Ukrainian goods or importers switching to more predictable suppliers. At the same time, imports (fuel, components, military-related goods) are said to become more expensive, so Kyiv loses both export revenue and tax income while import costs rise. Rybar concludes that a drone does not need to permanently destroy a crossing to have an effect — alarms and the need to halt traffic are enough to undermine carriers' confidence in a route. The channel argues such strikes cannot fully stop Ukrainian exports (and that this is not even the goal), but they make every exported ton slower, more expensive and less profitable; combined with strikes on ports, railways, and fuel/energy infrastructure, the effect is said to accumulate, with alternative routes able to offset only part of the lost sea trade. For European sponsors, Rybar claims, this means a steadily rising cost of support for diminishing economic return, with Ukraine allegedly turning into a chronically loss-making project that needs money just to keep functioning.

Ukrainian Forces Have Carried Out Over 100,000 Ground Drone Missions So Far in 2026

2026-08-24SINGLE SOURCE

According to Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, Ukrainian ground robotic systems have carried out more than 100,000 logistics and evacuation missions since the start of 2026 — the article explicitly names only these two mission types, not assault or mine-clearing tasks. The figures come from Delta, Ukraine's digital battlefield-management system, which combines drone, satellite and sensor data. The ministry is quoted: "Each of these missions represents a potential life saved among Ukrainian service members." The monthly breakdown shows steady, near-uninterrupted growth: January 7,511; February 7,960; March 9,072; April 11,028; May 14,059; June 16,664; July 19,969 (the peak month); and 15,729 already in the first 20 days of August. Since the start of 2026, over 22,000 ground robotic systems have been contracted — almost twice as many as during the whole of the previous year — and service members have ordered systems worth nearly UAH 1.9 billion (about US$42.5 million) through the Brave1 Market marketplace under the e-Points programme (points earned for confirmed strikes on Russian personnel and equipment). As background, the article notes that Ukrainian company Tencore and Finnish company Insta are establishing a joint venture to manufacture the TerMIT 2.0 ground robotic system for Ukraine's defence forces. Caveat: these are self-reported figures from Ukraine's own Ministry of Defence via a single Ukrainian outlet (Ukrainska Pravda), without independent verification — useful as an indicator of the institutionalization and scaling-up of Ukrainian ground robotics, not as an externally verified statistic.

Sanctions Push Russia's Aviation Industry Into Crisis, Forcing Rostec Restructuring

2026-07-28SINGLE SOURCE

Ukrainian defense-news outlet Defense Express reports, citing Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service (SZRU), that Western sanctions have pushed Russia's aviation industry into a structural crisis. Since 2022, sanctions have cut Russia off from Western engines, avionics, precision components and maintenance support, driving what the report describes as growing technological isolation of the sector. In response, state conglomerate Rostec is consolidating several aviation businesses under a single holding company, to be managed by Ilyushin Finance Co.: passenger carrier Red Wings, cargo airline Sky Gates, medical aviation assets, and leasing firm Aviacapital-Service. Officially, the merger is framed as centralizing management and establishing a unified maintenance system. According to the SZRU, however, the real purpose is to obscure the deteriorating financial condition of the individual companies by merging their financial reporting into one holding. Concrete symptoms of the crisis cited include: at Red Wings, only one of three Boeing 777 aircraft remains in scheduled passenger service; Sky Gates is struggling with shortages of engines and spare parts and extended maintenance cycles due to an aging fleet; and the domestic Tu-214 airliner program reportedly meets only around 10% of its planned production output. Military programs such as the Tu-160 and Tu-22M3 are also linked to this broader production strain. CAVEAT — this is a single-source report: the information comes exclusively from the Ukrainian outlet Defense Express, which in turn relies entirely on a statement by Ukraine's foreign intelligence service SZRU. The article does not name specific sanctions packages, dates, or verified financial figures, and none of the claims have been independently confirmed by Russian, Western, or other sources.
