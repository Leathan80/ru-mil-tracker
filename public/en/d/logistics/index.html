Ukraine strikes Russia's largest oil refinery at Omsk for the second time, using upgraded FP-1 drones flying over 2,500 km

2026-10-08

Ukraine's General Staff confirmed that Ukrainian forces struck the Omsk oil refinery in south-western Siberia on the afternoon of 8 October. The plant, owned by Gazprom (Gazprom Neft), is Russia's largest refinery, with an annual crude processing capacity of more than 21 million tonnes and, according to the General Staff, a refining depth of about 99%. It produces fuels, lubricants and petrochemical products and, Kyiv says, supplies the Russian military. Omsk lies roughly 2,500 km from Ukrainian-controlled territory. The General Staff presented the strike as self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter, aimed at reducing Russia's military-economic potential. Ukrainian drone maker Fire Point said the attack was carried out with upgraded versions of its FP-1 long-range attack drones, which flew more than 2,500 km to the target; it reported that "a hit on the target was recorded, followed by a fire". The independent Russian outlet Meduza, citing Astra, reported a column of black smoke over the plant, and Ukrainian monitoring channels shared local videos of explosions and fire around the refinery. Omsk Oblast governor Vitaly Khotsenko confirmed a drone attack on the region, saying one drone "reached the northern industrial hub" and the others were shot down, but he did not name the refinery or give damage details. The extent of the damage has not been independently established. This was the second Ukrainian strike on the Omsk refinery. On 6 July 2026 an attack hit the ELOU-AVT-11 primary distillation unit (design capacity 8.4 million tonnes per year), according to the General Staff; sources told Reuters that a crude distillation unit accounting for 38% of the plant's capacity was damaged, and the refinery temporarily halted operations.

«Безпілотна естафета» на «нуль»: як НРК, «Вампір» і малі дрони доставляють вантажі піхоті (ArmyInform: An 'unmanned relay' to the zero line: how UGVs, the 'Vampire' and small drones deliver supplies to infantry)

2026-10-08SINGLE SOURCE

ArmyInform, based on a photo report by Ukraine's 93rd Separate Mechanised Brigade 'Kholodnyi Yar', describes how food and water reach front-line infantry in an 'unmanned relay'. From early morning twilight, fighters of the Corvus company of the brigade's rifle battalion load supplies onto a ground robotic system (UGV), which drives them to the operators of the heavy 'Vampire' hexacopter. On the run described, the UGV was escorted because Russian FPV drones were swarming over the road. The Vampire operators split the cargo into packages and fly them towards the infantry, but not all the way to the positions; at an intermediate point the loads are divided again and small drones cover the last few hundred metres in several short flights, sometimes repeatedly each day. According to the article, the large Vampire is more visible and flying it straight to the positions would let the enemy trace the route. When Russian drones appear, UGVs are hidden in bushes or behind burnt-out vehicles, and soldiers take cover and try to shoot down FPV drones where possible. The article gives no UGV model, payload weights or exact location (its tags mention Druzhkivka). Single source: this is a report from ArmyInform, the news agency of the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, that has not been independently verified.

Russia's top tank maker takes on T-90 repairs as new builds strain

2026-10-08

According to Defence Blog, Uralvagonzavod (UVZ) in Nizhny Tagil, Russia's main tank manufacturer and the only plant building new T-90M tanks, is now carrying out major (capital) repairs of T-90Ms badly damaged in Ukraine. Photos circulating on social media this week show a train with heavily damaged T-90Ms on rail cars heading to the plant. Analysts note that such repairs were traditionally handled by the military's armored repair plants (BTRZ) and now compete with the factory's production of new tanks. Armored vehicle analyst Andrei Tarasenko attributes this to the breakdown of the BTRZ repair network, adding that a repair under factory conditions costs about as much as a new tank. Oryx lead analyst Jakub Janovsky warned that some organizations may count factory repairs and upgrades as "new production", blurring estimates of Russia's actual output. He added that recoverability depends on where a tank is hit: vehicles disabled in the contested gray zone are usually destroyed before they can be recovered, while those damaged further back can be retrieved with far less effort. Analysts link the shift to pressure on the tank industry from Western sanctions and labor shortages. Some T-90Ms leaving UVZ reportedly use refurbished hulls from older T-90As rather than newly built ones; in 2024 the company itself publicized restoring a T-90M in four weeks after it had been hit by 26 drones. UVZ still delivers vehicles: this week it reported another batch (number not given) of T-90Ms and upgraded T-72B3Ms with extra anti-drone protection, and some T-90Ms now carry the Arena-M active protection system, which Russian forces demonstrated against drones during Tsentr-2026. The interpretation of the photos rests on analysts' assessments and has not been independently confirmed.

Latvian court sentences woman to six years in prison over €43 transfer that investigators say was meant for drone-jamming equipment for Russian troops

2026-10-07

Riga City Court has sentenced Polina Kamleva, a resident of Rēzekne, Latvia, to six years in prison for financing an armed conflict, Meduza reports (citing the Latvian outlet Delfi). Prosecutors had asked for eight years; the offence carries up to ten years under Latvia's criminal code. Kamleva is described as a pro-Russian activist and a member of the Latvian Russian Union party. The ruling was reported on 7 October 2026. According to the report, the money went to the Russian side. Investigators say Kamleva transferred €43 (157 Belarusian rubles) intended for Russian forces, specifically towards a mobile electronic warfare system, i.e. a drone-jamming device, for Russian military use. She asked an acquaintance in Belarus to make the transfer because, as she put it, transfers from Latvian banks to Russian banks were impossible. The request for funds came from an acquaintance in Russia linked to the Kaskad military-patriotic center, and the two exchanged WhatsApp messages in December 2024 in which she agreed to help. Latvia's State Security Service detained her in December 2025. Kamleva denied criminal intent: she argued the messages did not clearly establish that the money was meant for Russian forces and cited unemployment and psychological distress at the time. The case illustrates how small-scale crowdfunding for Russian EW and counter-drone equipment is being prosecuted in the Baltics, including routing payments via Belarus to bypass banking restrictions. Single detail to note: the claims about the purpose of the transfer are those of the investigators and prosecution as relayed by Meduza and Delfi.

2026-10-07

Ukraine's 413th Separate Raid Regiment of the Unmanned Systems Forces says that, together with other units of the Defence Forces, it struck the Snizhne Machine-Building Plant in occupied Donetsk Oblast in September 2026; the regiment posted a strike video on Telegram on 6 October, Euromaidan Press reports. Ukraine's General Staff had reported the strike on 11 September, saying the extent of the damage was still being clarified. According to the regiment, the plant produces engine components for attack aircraft and combat helicopters; the General Staff says it specialises in gas-turbine engine parts such as compressor and turbine discs and blades, and never built complete engines. No independent confirmation of the damage is given. The plant was a division of the Zaporizhzhia engine maker Motor Sich and came under Russian control when Snizhne was occupied in 2014. In September 2014 it was still machining blanks from Motor Sich into turbine blades; a worker said it was one of only two plants in the former Soviet Union making such blades for helicopters. Motor Sich said in December 2017 that it had lost control of the plant, while former honorary president Viacheslav Bohuslaiev told RFE/RL in January 2018 that the company still owned it and kept "full control". In February 2023 Ukraine's Prosecutor General's Office served Bohuslaiev a notice of suspicion of aiding a terrorist organisation, alleging that Motor Sich shipped blanks to the plant and received finished parts via foreign-registered companies; he denies the charges and has been in pretrial detention since October 2022. Snizhne has been hit before: in May the Unmanned Systems Forces and the SBU struck a Russian drone-pilot training centre there, and a strike on energy infrastructure in September cut power across the city. Euromaidan Press frames the strike as part of Ukraine's long-range campaign against suppliers that keep Russian attack jets and helicopters flying. The strike claim comes from Ukrainian military sources and has not been independently verified.

The design assumes attack: facilities are dispersed, separated by earthworks, and munitions and fuel are kept under concrete and earth so that a strike's blast stays contained and cannot trigger a chain reaction. Defense Express summarises a General Staff of the Armed Forces of Ukraine overview of strikes carried out on 21–27 September 2026. The article does not say which units or weapons carried out the strikes, and gives no damage assessment beyond the General Staff's list. The SBU says the depots supply fuel to the Russian army and describes the strikes as a response to Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities. The two-versus-four difference is not resolved: Euromaidan Press describes what it could confirm from local reports and satellite data, while the SBU claim covers more sites. Russia imposes a moratorium on inspections of commercial sites damaged by drone attacks until 1 October 2027

2026-09-28

According to Meduza, the Russian government has imposed a moratorium until 1 October 2027 on inspections of commercial sites damaged by drone attacks. It covers sites damaged since 1 January 2026, which are exempt from scheduled inspections and certain unscheduled regulatory checks. Deputy Prime Minister Dmitry Grigorenko said the aim is to ease the administrative burden so that companies can spend their resources on repairs. Meduza notes that Ukraine has attacked Wildberries warehouses in several Russian regions since midsummer, and that Ozon warehouses have also been hit since August, causing billions of rubles in damage. Russian oil refineries are also regularly targeted by drones. Caveat: this rests on Meduza's report of statements by a Russian official; the damage figures were not independently verified.
