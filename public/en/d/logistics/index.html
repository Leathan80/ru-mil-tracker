Kommersant: Ukrainian drone and naval-drone attacks have all but halted Russia's Black Sea and Azov ports, costing budgets and businesses over 200bn rubles (~$2.4bn) a month

2026-10-21

Ukrainska Pravda, citing the Russian business daily Kommersant, reports that operations at Russia's Black Sea and Sea of Azov ports have virtually stopped because of Ukrainian aerial drone and uncrewed surface vessel (USV) attacks. The ports concerned are the southern ones, including Novorossiysk, Taman and Tuapse, not the Baltic ports. Industry players estimate that state budgets and businesses are losing more than 200 billion rubles (about $2.43bn) a month. Kommersant's sources include OTEKO, which operates terminals at Taman. The article does not explain how the monthly loss was calculated; it is an industry estimate, not an official figure. According to Kommersant, cargo handling at southern terminals fell by 95–100% in September and passenger traffic by 85%. Cargo is being rerouted to ports in north-western Russia or overland. Agricultural cargo through Krasnodar Krai ports fell to 3.8 million tonnes in July–August, from 7.9 million tonnes in the same period of 2025. The largest southern ports have begun cutting staff, putting employees on unpaid leave or shorter working weeks. Krasnodar Krai governor Veniamin Kondratyev has asked Deputy Prime Minister Dmitry Patrushev for state subsidies for the ports, modelled on support for the region's closed airports. The options include wage support, maintenance costs for equipment, silos and berths, utility bills, and partial reimbursement of loan interest. On 6 October Patrushev instructed ministries to consider the proposals. The Association of Commercial Sea Ports has separately asked Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak for tax and lease deferrals, a wage subsidy of one month's minimum wage per employee, and better terms for war-risk insurance. The southern ports employ about 20,000–25,000 people, and experts put such a wage subsidy at 550–670 million rubles a month. The report fits earlier claims of Ukrainian drone forces that the Sea of Azov is 'at a standstill' (e-20260923-07) and earlier strikes on Novorossiysk (e-20261010-s01). This time the economic impact is acknowledged in Russian media.

CSIS/Bush Institute report: about 12,000 North Korean workers assemble Shahed/Geran drones at Russia's Alabuga zone, with Pyongyang taking up to 90% of their pay

2026-10-21

Militarnyi, citing a joint report by the Washington-based Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) and the George W. Bush Institute ('Human Rights Abuses in Drone Production at Yelabuga', published 14 October 2026), reports that North Korea has sent about 12,000 workers to Russia to assemble Shahed-type strike drones (Geran-2) in the Alabuga Special Economic Zone in Tatarstan. According to the report, they earn about $2.50 per hour, and the North Korean state takes 70–90% of their wages. The researchers point to systematic withholding of wages from North Korean workers and describe the arrangement as forced labour organised by Pyongyang. The report identifies three labour pools at Alabuga: Russian youth, foreign recruits from other countries, and North Koreans. Alabuga College recruits young Russians under the label of 'work placements'. Shifts reportedly last up to 12 hours, pay depends on strict production quotas, and workers are threatened with fines if they tell their families what the plant produces. The researchers suspect coercive labour practices across all three groups. Satellite imagery from September shows new production halls and warehouses under construction at the zone, which Militarnyi links to the recruitment of foreign labour. The 12,000 figure matches the number Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR) gave in November 2025, when it said Moscow planned to bring in about 12,000 North Koreans for Alabuga via the North Korean firm Jihyang Technology Trade Company. Reporting on the CSIS paper indicates that the headcount rests largely on Ukrainian intelligence and has not been independently confirmed. Bloomberg reported in September a higher figure of about 25,000 North Koreans at Russian drone plants, many entering on student visas; the two numbers may count different things. The tracker has not previously covered North Korean labour at Alabuga, but has covered the site itself: plans to expand Alabuga-Volokno's carbon-fibre output that could raise Geran production by up to 78% (e-20260920-05), and Alabuga-built engines and GNSS antennas found in Geran-4 drones (e-20261001-02, e-20261012-04). Workers from North Korea would ease a labour constraint on that expansion.

Starshield speed cap pushes Wild Hornets to rethink air-launched Sting interceptors and look at Eutelsat; Georgian Legion reports wider Starlink dropouts

2026-10-20

Wild Hornets, the Ukrainian maker of the Sting interceptor drones used against Russian Shaheds, is reconsidering how far its future projects should rely on SpaceX's Starlink/Starshield after the speed cap imposed on Ukrainian terminals in mid-October (see e-20261016-08). Spokesman Mike Orliuk told The War Zone on 20 October that current Sting operations are unaffected: Sting uses its own communications system for the basic link to the interceptor, and the Starlink terminal only connects the command post to a ground station, comparable to a cell tower, which does not move. The problem is a planned project to launch interceptors from airborne platforms, in which drones would carry a Starlink terminal on board and must fly fast, which the cap makes impossible. No decision has been taken; the company will test alternatives it declined to name, has shown interest in Europe's Eutelsat constellation, and Orliuk warned against depending on a single solution. He said other firms far more dependent on Starlink have raised concerns with him. Wild Hornets claims Sting has destroyed over 15,000 Shaheds and Gerberas in a year, including about 1,600 Shaheds in September; these are company claims. The cap's effects go beyond high-speed systems, according to Mamuka Mamulashvili, commander of the Georgian Legion fighting in the Kramatorsk area. He told TWZ that besides the limit of about 160 km/h, Starlink connectivity repeatedly cut in and out, disrupting drone control across several systems. Alternatives such as mobile internet, fibre optics, radio and OneWeb cannot fully replace Starlink in Ukraine at present. Ukrainian officials say equipment still stops working at about 130–161 km/h in parts of the front. Zelensky said on 16 October that some interceptors chasing jet-powered Gerans, which fly faster than 400 km/h, use Starlink and that some could not be intercepted while the link was slowed. TWZ names relay drones daisy-chained over long distances and balloons relaying Starlink to drones below as possible partial mitigations, but stresses that nothing currently matches Starlink's beyond-line-of-sight capability. Who ordered the cap remains disputed. Reuters, citing US and Western officials, reported on 15 October that the order came from inside the White House. A US official told TWZ the president never made such a request. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth said the Pentagon gave no order and that Ukraine had violated Starlink's terms of service. Secretary of State Marco Rubio denied that aid is being deliberately withheld and called Starlink 'a private company', and Elon Musk said speed and usage limits are decided by the US military and ultimately by President Trump. SpaceX did not respond to TWZ. For a Russian analysis of the restriction compared with autonomous loitering munitions, see the single-source entry e-20261021-s05.

The milblogger channel Rybar calls Ukraine's railways the sector Russia is destroying most methodically, beyond data centres, warehouses and ports. According to the post, most strikes hit Kirovohrad, Odesa and Dnipropetrovsk oblasts: port infrastructure in Odesa, and metallurgy-linked facilities in Dnipropetrovsk and Kirovohrad, where plants were halted both by direct hits and by strikes on the traction rolling stock needed to ship their output. The post offers a useful Russian-side picture of target selection (stock, then repair capacity and power supply), but the figures are Rybar's own open-source counts. Ukrainian brigade claims first frontline evacuation of a wounded soldier by heavy hexacopter drone

2026-10-19

The unmanned ground systems company of Ukraine's 92nd Separate Assault Brigade says it has flown a wounded soldier off the front line with a heavy hexacopter drone, calling it the world's first aerial evacuation of a casualty from the battlefield. The casualty, an infantryman of the 30th Separate Mechanised Brigade, was wounded while holding his position. According to the brigade, intense Russian drone activity and mined terrain made evacuation by the unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) that Ukrainian units now routinely use impossible, leaving no safe ground route. The soldier was flown to a safe location, is receiving treatment and rehabilitation, and his life is no longer in danger. The date and location were not disclosed. The aircraft was a heavy hexacopter from Ukrainian manufacturer Gurzuf Defence, a type normally used as a bomber drone, which the company provided free of charge. Brigade specialists worked with the manufacturer to modify it so it could carry a person while staying stable, without overturning or excessive swaying, and tested it at Gurzuf Defence sites before planning the mission with neighbouring units. A UAV operator from the 30th Brigade flew the drone; the 14th Mechanised Brigade and the 10th Army Corps also took part. The brigade did not disclose the drone's specifications, the distance flown or how the casualty was secured, and blurred the drone in its video. The War Zone writes that the soldier hung in a harness beneath the drone and had to be able to strap himself in, and that reports suggest the type was an Audag-6, reportedly rated for about 200 pounds (~90 kg) and distances of up to 15 miles (~24 km). TWZ adds that Ukraine began front-line testing of StarGate, a purpose-built medevac hexacopter from SkyFall with an enclosed stretcher and a payload of about 330 pounds (~150 kg), only days earlier. The backdrop is the drone-saturated "kill zone" along the front: helicopters cannot operate near the line, medics on foot face extreme risk, and Ukraine relies mainly on UGVs, which according to Euromaidan Press carried out some 112,000 logistics and evacuation missions from January to August 2026 but struggle with mines, mud and craters. TWZ points to the limits of the improvised approach: no room for a medic, a casualty who must be able to strap himself in, and vulnerability in contested airspace. The "world's first" claim and the details come from the Ukrainian brigade itself and have not been independently verified; TWZ notes the footage could also show a staged demonstration.

Three months of Ukrainian drone strikes on Wildberries and Ozon warehouses: targets, rationale and economic fallout, including a shift of major sellers to Ozon

2026-10-19

Meduza reviews the campaign of Ukrainian long-range drone strikes on warehouses of Russia's two largest online marketplaces. It began on 18 July 2026 with attacks on Wildberries sites in Elektrostal (Moscow region) and Kotovsk (Tambov region); Russia's Emergency Situations Ministry reported one dead in Elektrostal and seven in Kotovsk. Koledino (Moscow region) was hit on 20 July, followed over the summer by Shushary (St Petersburg), Volgograd, Aleksin (Tula region) and Kursk (15–16 August). Ozon was first hit at Chapayevsk (Samara region) on 21–22 August, followed by Adygeysk, Nevinnomyssk, Makhachkala and Krasnodar; at least eight Ozon facilities have been struck. Consultancy IBC Global counted 49 attacks on 36 warehouse sites between 18 July and 24 September. The stated rationale is the platforms' role in military supply. President Zelensky has said Wildberries sells military gear and parts for combat equipment, and Ukraine says the marketplaces are used to supply sanctioned components for drone and navigation equipment production. A Meduza analysis of 1,008 fundraising posts for Russian troops found 1,882 items ordered via Wildberries, worth about 18.6 million rubles. They included body armour, night-vision devices, radios, Starlink equipment and drone components. Wildberries founder Tatyana Kim called the attacks 'terrorist'. Meduza's findings show the marketplaces acting as a channel for crowdfunded supplies to Russian frontline units, alongside formal MoD logistics. The effects are mainly economic and social. Workers describe a chaotic evacuation at Elektrostal (some 7,000 on the night shift), downtime and pressure to move to contracts without downtime pay. Kazakhstan's e-commerce association put sellers' damages at over 335 million rubles by late August. Sellers filed 599 lawsuits against Wildberries and 236 against Ozon between mid-July and mid-September. Ozon's insurer paid 8.2 billion rubles to about 90,000 sellers by 18 September, and Wildberries announced reimbursements of up to 500,000 rubles per seller. The Russian Finance Ministry offered affected businesses a one-year tax deferral, and delivery volumes at pickup points have fallen sharply. The strikes are also shifting the market. According to MPStats data cited by Kommersant (via Meduza, 21 October), Ozon's share of major sellers' sales across the two platforms rose to 60% by the end of summer, from 42.46% in January, when Wildberries led with 57.54%. Experts attribute this to Wildberries' logistics being hit harder than Ozon's. Wildberries disputes the figures and says its major sellers' sales remain stable; Ozon did not comment. Neither source quantifies any effect on deliveries to the armed forces.
