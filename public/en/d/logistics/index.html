«Крылатая ракета «Филин». Недорогое средство большой дальности» (Topwar: 'The Filin cruise missile: an inexpensive long-range weapon')

2026-10-26SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar author Kirill Ryabov discusses the 'Filin' (Eagle-Owl), a new long-range ground-launched cruise missile. He relies almost entirely on material that Ukraine's military intelligence (HUR) published on its War&Sanctions portal on 22 October: debris photos, a 3D model, a component list and estimated specifications. HUR says Russia has used the missile since late summer 2026 and names Moscow-based NPO Molniya as the developer. HUR also says it found 35 US-made and 22 Chinese-made components, that the inertial navigation unit is Chinese, and that some onboard equipment is made by OOO PLAZ, with similar units used on the 'Garpiya' drone. The article stresses that Russian official bodies have not commented, and that launch numbers, serial production and combat results are unknown. The specifications cited: composite airframe of carbon and glass fibre with some 3D-printed parts, a high-mounted swept wing, a dorsal air intake and a three-surface tail. Length up to 3.5 m, wingspan up to 3 m, launch mass up to 210 kg, and a 50 or 100 kg high-explosive fragmentation, penetrating or thermobaric warhead. It is powered by an R500 small turbojet from the Russian company Reynolds. Speed is up to 800 km/h, altitude from low level to 6 km, and range 600-1,200 km; the article notes it is unclear which range goes with which warhead. Guidance uses a K-101 flight controller, a K-103-01 inertial unit and a 16-channel anti-jam 'Kometa' satellite navigation module, so the missile is meant for fixed targets at known coordinates. The author says nothing in the component list confirms terminal scene-matching guidance, which expert Yuri Knutov had suggested. The missile is ground-launched with a solid-fuel booster. The article's main argument is unification: according to HUR, the R500 engine has also been found on the air-launched S-71K 'Kovyor' and S-71M 'Monokhrom' missiles. The author places Filin alongside 'Banderol' and 'Dan-T' as compact jet strike weapons. Shared engines and electronics could cut production costs, and ground launch saves aircraft resources, but he says the data are too thin to measure the cost benefit. This is a single source: Topwar (Voennoye Obozreniye), a Russian pro-government military news site, not an official MoD outlet; it largely relays Ukrainian intelligence (HUR) claims, and the claims have not been independently verified.

Russia's campaign against Kyiv's 'power ring': the capital's own CHP generation is out and strikes on 330/750 kV substations raise the prospect of 8-20 hour daily blackouts this winter

2026-10-24

In an analysis for Ukrainska Pravda (24 October 2026, translated from Ekonomichna Pravda), energy journalist Mykola Topalov describes how Russia is systematically attacking Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast with two aims: to destroy the city's combined heat and power (CHP) plants and to break the so-called 'power ring'. That ring is the system of high-voltage lines and 330 kV substations linking the capital to the national grid from several directions, with the 750 kV Kyivska substation as a key node through which power from the Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear plants reaches the city. Kyiv normally uses 1-1.2 GW (12-15% of national demand), rising to 1.8-2 GW in winter. Before the large-scale winter attacks of 2025, local generation from CHP-5, CHP-6 and Darnytsia CHP (CHP-4) supplied up to about 1.3 GW, according to an energy-company source. Those plants can no longer generate electricity, yet all three were hit again on the night of 13-14 October. The article explains the tactical logic. Russia does not need to destroy the whole ring: damaging a few critical nodes that carry a large share of the flow is enough. Power may then exist in the system but cannot be delivered to Kyiv in sufficient volume, forcing dispatchers into emergency or scheduled cuts. The ring's key components have been under constant attack for more than a month. Repairs are slowed by repeat strikes, long air-raid alerts, risks to repair crews, and government plans to cut the share of energy workers exempt from mobilisation. Replacing a large transformer takes much longer if no spare is held in reserve. The article does not give numbers of missiles or drones per strike. It stresses that protection of these sites against ballistic missiles depends mainly on air defence, that the engineering protection built at most substations cannot guarantee equipment survives a direct hit, and that Patriot interceptors are in short supply. The article sets out three winter scenarios. If repairs keep pace and new strikes cause no critical losses, outages would be about 8-10 hours a day. With continued attacks and lingering damage during moderate cold, they would be 12-16 hours. The worst case, regular large-scale strikes on key substations during severe frost, would mean up to 20 hours a day. Energy-sector sources nevertheless do not expect Russia to 'plunge Kyiv into darkness' entirely. Critical infrastructure (water pumps, heating, transport, hospitals, communications) needs about 200 MW. According to Ekonomichna Pravda's sources, 150 MW of distributed generation is already running, rising to 180-185 MW by the end of 2026, and private plants could add another 55-60 MW before winter, about half of which are ready. The scenarios are the outlet's assessment based on unnamed energy-sector sources.

Sources: Ukrainska Pravda (EN)

Ukraine carries out first known aerial casualty evacuation by heavy-lift drone: Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 extracts wounded soldier after 491 days in the grey zone near Kupiansk

2026-10-23

On 4 October 2026 Ukrainian forces evacuated a wounded infantryman from the grey zone on the Kupiansk front by heavy drone. Ukrainska Pravda and Reuters (republished by Meduza) both call it the first known operation of its kind. The soldier is Kostiantyn "Magura" Vlasenko of the 2nd Mechanised Battalion, 30th Separate Mechanised Brigade. He had held a position on the left bank of the Oskil River since 2 June 2025. On 14 February 2026 a Russian drone-dropped munition wounded him in both legs. He lost one foot and amputated the other himself. For months he could not be reached: the area was mined, Russian infantry had infiltrated around his position, and Russian FPV and reconnaissance drones attacked anything that moved. A ground robot sent to fetch him was destroyed on the way (Reuters says two), and infantry groups also failed. Drones resupplied him every four to five days. The aircraft was the Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 from Gurzuf Defence, a modular heavy-lift logistics drone with a cargo winch. Ukrainska Pravda gives its payload as up to 130 kg. Reuters reports roughly 90 kg over up to 25 km, so the sources disagree. The drone was adapted over about six months with help from the 92nd Separate Assault Brigade's unmanned ground systems company. The day before the flight it delivered a mountaineering harness (a Petzl Pitagor evacuation triangle) and a phone with instruction videos, so the soldier could clip himself to the winch cable. Reuters puts the cable height at about 70 m. The drone flew at first light without fog, at a time when Russian drone activity had been assessed as lowest. It used Starlink for control, and Ukrainska Pravda says no jamming was detected. Several measures protected it: Vampire heavy bomber drones flew decoy sorties in neighbouring sectors to draw Russian anti-aircraft FPV operators (one Vampire was shot down), an Autel quadcopter watched overhead, a Lucky Strike interceptor drone stood ready to ram Russian UAVs, and 10th Army Corps artillery fired on identified Russian drone-launch points. Units from the 14th and 43rd Mechanised Brigades also took part, with EW support from the 3rd Army Corps. The 16 km route needed a battery swap at a pre-positioned ground team. The soldier was then handed to a Peacemaker unmanned ground vehicle, which drove him another 8 km to a vehicle and a stabilisation point. Reuters gives different figures: a 15 km distance and a 15-minute flight. Ukrainska Pravda says the drone was airborne for under 1.5 hours in total. Vlasenko is now in rehabilitation in Kharkiv. The operation matters for tactics because ground casualty evacuation in the drone-saturated 'kill zone' has become nearly impossible, and the 'golden hour' is now rarely achievable. The developer itself warns against treating this as a new routine. Gurzuf co-founder Pavlo Feldblium calls aerial evacuation a last resort that needs unique, multi-unit planning (about 15 personnel, two months of planning, seven days of pre-flight preparation). Without decoys, escorts and counter-battery fire, he says, the slow drone with its underslung load would have been shot down by the first Russian interceptor. Gurzuf will nevertheless fit its mass-produced Ayudag-6 drones with evacuation mounting kits and is developing variants with higher payloads. The details come from Ukrainian military participants and the developer, and from Vlasenko's own account. They have not been independently verified.

Sources: Ukrainska Pravda (EN) · Meduza (EN)

Three months of Ukrainian drone strikes on Wildberries and Ozon warehouses: targets, rationale and economic fallout, including a shift of major sellers to Ozon

2026-10-19

Meduza reviews the campaign of Ukrainian long-range drone strikes on warehouses of Russia's two largest online marketplaces. It began on 18 July 2026 with attacks on Wildberries sites in Elektrostal (Moscow region) and Kotovsk (Tambov region); Russia's Emergency Situations Ministry reported one dead in Elektrostal and seven in Kotovsk. Koledino (Moscow region) was hit on 20 July, followed over the summer by Shushary (St Petersburg), Volgograd, Aleksin (Tula region) and Kursk (15–16 August). Ozon was first hit at Chapayevsk (Samara region) on 21–22 August, followed by Adygeysk, Nevinnomyssk, Makhachkala and Krasnodar; at least eight Ozon facilities have been struck. Consultancy IBC Global counted 49 attacks on 36 warehouse sites between 18 July and 24 September. The stated rationale is the platforms' role in military supply. President Zelensky has said Wildberries sells military gear and parts for combat equipment, and Ukraine says the marketplaces are used to supply sanctioned components for drone and navigation equipment production. A Meduza analysis of 1,008 fundraising posts for Russian troops found 1,882 items ordered via Wildberries, worth about 18.6 million rubles. They included body armour, night-vision devices, radios, Starlink equipment and drone components. Wildberries founder Tatyana Kim called the attacks 'terrorist'. Meduza's findings show the marketplaces acting as a channel for crowdfunded supplies to Russian frontline units, alongside formal MoD logistics. The effects are mainly economic and social. Workers describe a chaotic evacuation at Elektrostal (some 7,000 on the night shift), downtime and pressure to move to contracts without downtime pay. Kazakhstan's e-commerce association put sellers' damages at over 335 million rubles by late August. Sellers filed 599 lawsuits against Wildberries and 236 against Ozon between mid-July and mid-September. Ozon's insurer paid 8.2 billion rubles to about 90,000 sellers by 18 September, and Wildberries announced reimbursements of up to 500,000 rubles per seller. The Russian Finance Ministry offered affected businesses a one-year tax deferral, and delivery volumes at pickup points have fallen sharply. The strikes are also shifting the market. According to MPStats data cited by Kommersant (via Meduza, 21 October), Ozon's share of major sellers' sales across the two platforms rose to 60% by the end of summer, from 42.46% in January, when Wildberries led with 57.54%. Experts attribute this to Wildberries' logistics being hit harder than Ozon's. Wildberries disputes the figures and says its major sellers' sales remain stable; Ozon did not comment. Neither source quantifies any effect on deliveries to the armed forces.
