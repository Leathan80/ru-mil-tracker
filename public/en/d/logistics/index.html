2026-11-13SINGLE SOURCE

The Kazan-based paper Business Online, in its weekly review of the war, quotes an article from the September 2026 issue of the Russian General Staff journal Voyennaya Mysl (Military Thought) by V. Kavtarov and S. Sakhanov. The authors argue that linear formations have become extremely vulnerable now that enemy drones dominate the battlefield, and propose a 'qualitatively new' defensive method they call the 'dispersed echeloned bastion': abandoning a continuous front line in favour of a network of autonomous combat modules ('bastions') spread across the front and in depth. Each bastion is built around mobile groups equipped with uncrewed ground vehicles, FPV interceptor drones and EW assets, tied together by a single situational-awareness picture from Zala and Orlan reconnaissance drones. An attacker who wedges into such a defence does not break through it, the authors write, but comes under crossfire from prepared, concealed and frequently changing module positions. The paper frames this against the current front: deputy defence minister Colonel General Valery Solodchuk has said the 'kill zone' has grown to about 30 km, within which supplies are delivered only by drones and robots and rations have been made lighter with dehydrators and vacuum packers. Business Online concludes that rapid advances inside that 30 km belt are impossible under current conditions and expects Russian forces to shift to new ways of sustaining the front-line zone this winter. In the same review, citing the Telegram channel Voyennaya Khronika and the US think tank FPRI, the paper says Russia's drone units are moving from specialised, highly autonomous formations towards established structures integrated into the combined-arms armies, with unmanned systems brigades and more regiment- and battalion-level drone units. «New Tu-22M3s, Tu-160s Are Not Really Being Built, Tu-214 Deliveries Average Just One Year: So What Is Kazan Aircraft Plant Doing?» (Defense Express) — Ukrainian analysis says Kazan Aircraft Plant has delivered one Tu-22M3M, up to five Tu-160M2s and three Tu-214s since 2022, far below Russian targets

2026-11-09SINGLE SOURCE

Defense Express (author Ivan Kyrychevskiy, a serviceman of the 413th Raid Regiment of Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces) examines the output of the Kazan Aircraft Plant (KAPO), the Russian plant known for the Tu-22M3 and Tu-160 strategic bombers and the Tu-214, including military variants. Citing Russian specialist sources, it says KAPO can in theory build up to three Tu-214s a year with a production cycle of at least 14 months, but has delivered only three Tu-214s to customers since 2022. One of them had stood unfinished and rusting behind the factory fence since the late 2000s. The often-quoted 'one aircraft a year' is, according to the outlet, partly a statistical artefact: the same Tu-214 was counted as manufactured in 2025 and as delivered in 2026. Two more airframes are at various stages of completion. Plans from 2023 called for 70 Tu-214s by 2030; actual output is about ten times lower, yet the targets have not been substantially revised. Russia now plans four Tu-214 deliveries in 2026 (including aircraft for security agencies), eight in 2027 and twelve in 2028, while acknowledging that even at maximum capacity KAPO cannot deliver more than four a year. On strategic aviation, the plant has delivered only one upgraded Tu-22M3M to the Russian MoD since the full-scale invasion began (reportedly fitted with an in-flight refuelling probe the standard Tu-22M3 lacks), and reportedly up to five Tu-160M2s assembled from airframe sections stockpiled in the Soviet era. Defense Express concludes that genuinely new bomber production is virtually nonexistent, despite KAPO management's claims of a 'large-scale modernisation' of the plant. It attributes the gap less to theft than to a lack of organisational capability, and recalls that in January 2026 Tupolev's mounting problems led only to a change of management. Single source: an analysis by the Ukrainian defence outlet Defense Express, partly based on unnamed Russian specialist sources; the delivery figures are not independently confirmed.

Former Russia-to-Europe gas pipelines have become an underground front: both armies use them to infiltrate behind enemy lines and bypass the drone kill zone

2026-11-02

Meduza summarises October reports by The Atlantic (Simon Shuster) and The New York Times (Maria Varenikova) on fighting inside disused gas pipelines beneath the front line. Both armies move through the steel pipes, which once carried Siberian gas to Ukraine and the rest of Europe, to cross a drone-dominated kill zone tens of kilometres wide and to surface behind enemy lines. Ihor Obolensky, commander of the 2nd Khartiia Corps of Ukraine's National Guard, says Russian commanders adopted the tactic first and Ukrainian assault troops are now catching up. Maps obtained from the state energy company showed at least 57 steel pipes under the combat zone, most 1-2 metres in diameter; Kyiv ended gas transit for good only in early 2025. The reference case is Russia's 'Operation Pipe' in Kursk Oblast: in early March 2025, between 100 and several hundred soldiers crawled several kilometres through a section of the 1,420 mm Urengoy-Pomary-Uzhhorod trunk line and emerged behind Ukrainian lines, helping Russia retake Sudzha, although Meduza notes Kremlin propaganda exaggerated its effect. Obolensky says the surprise attacks mostly came in bad weather, when drones struggled to spot soldiers surfacing, and that Russian troops kept appearing several kilometres behind Ukrainian positions. Similar tactics followed inside Ukraine, including at Pokrovsk. At Kupiansk in 2025, Russian forces crossed under the Oskil River from Lyman Pershyi to Radkivka through the local 1,020 mm Shebelynka-Ostrogozhsk pipeline and then used it to supply their forward troops; the entrance at a compressor station near Radkivka is now held by Ukraine. Khartiia soldiers reportedly spend weeks underground, moving on modified coal-mining carts powered by electric scooter motors; the NYT describes an underground logistics hub hundreds of metres long linked to the pipe network. Fighting in the pipes involves high-calibre rifles and grenades, ricochets, blast overpressure, gas masks and oxygen tanks, and sandbag barriers that must be breached under fire. Obolensky says Khartiia regained about 14 km of pipeline in north-eastern Ukraine in October in an operation code-named Veles, which he calls the first successful counterattack under such conditions, but that most of the network remains under Russian control. He warns that control of the Donbas pipeline network could give Russia a decisive advantage in an expected winter offensive. These are Ukrainian commander accounts relayed by Western media and have not been independently verified.
