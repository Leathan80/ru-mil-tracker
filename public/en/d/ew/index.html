«На наших глазах рождается новый класс кораблей?» (Topwar: 'Is a new class of warship being born before our eyes?') — Topwar columnist argues the Russian Navy needs dedicated counter-drone ships after losing the corvette Merkury to sea drones

2026-11-26SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar columnist Roman Skomorokhov takes the loss of the Project 20380 corvette Merkury in Benghazi as his starting point. He lists six Russian vessels lost to sea drones, among them the missile boat Ivanovets, the landing ship Tsezar Kunikov, the patrol ship Sergey Kotov and Merkury, and notes that five of the six were practically unarmed against such targets, that the AK-630 gun is obsolete and that Merkury's air defence was not designed for small surface targets. In his view destroyers, frigates and corvettes are general-purpose designs poorly suited to escorting merchant ships against drones, and Russia has very few of them anyway. He calls for a specialised 'counter-drone defence' ship class, by analogy with air defence and anti-submarine warfare, based on the Project 22800 Karakurt hull: 800–900 tonnes, a crew of about 30–35, more than 30 knots maximum (against the Magura's 22 knots) and 2,000–2,500 nautical miles of range. Armament would be Pantsir-M and/or Kashtan-M, Kord machine guns, Orlan-10 reconnaissance drones and large numbers of interceptor drones, plus EW that jams satellite navigation and communications (Volna-Kupol-Garant, Repellent, Tirada) and naval versions of Pole-21 and Tobol. He doubts the leadership will see the need, accusing the Russian military of fighting 'by century-old canons', and expects sea-drone attacks to continue whatever agreements are signed. The piece is an opinion and design proposal, not an announced programme. Fibre-optic Molniya drones now seen on several front sectors, flying up to 30 km into Ukraine's rear beyond the reach of EW, says Ukrainian adviser Beskrestnov

2026-11-19

Ukrainska Pravda reported on 19 November 2026 that Ukrainian forces had recorded fibre-optic Molniya drones on several sectors of the front over the previous few days. Molniya is a Russian fixed-wing drone used for strike and reconnaissance. The source is Serhii 'Flash' Beskrestnov, a radio technology specialist and adviser to Ukraine's defence minister. He puts the drones' flight altitude at about 50 m and their reach at up to 30 km into the Ukrainian rear. He also published a video of one of these drones being intercepted and thanked the crews who detect and shoot them down. The drone itself is not new. Beskrestnov says the first Molniya drones fitted with fibre optics were seen about a year ago but were not widely used at the time. In July he reported that Russia was testing a fibre-optic Molniya with a range of up to 50 km and planned mass production. The new element is that the drones now appear across several front sectors rather than in isolated cases. That fits the earlier production plans, but the scale has not been independently confirmed. The article does not name the sectors or give numbers. For Ukrainian electronic warfare the implications are direct. A fibre-optic link emits no radio signal, so RF detection does not work and jamming cannot cut the drone off. At around 50 m altitude the drone is also hard to pick up on radar. The video link gives high-quality images, so the drone can carry out reconnaissance on the way to its target. Defence therefore relies on visual and acoustic detection and on physical interception. Until now, fibre-optic control was known mainly from short-range FPV drones. On a fixed-wing platform with a reach of tens of kilometres, the same EW-proof approach extends to logistics routes deeper behind the front. All figures are claims by Beskrestnov and have not been independently verified.

GPS jamming on 9 November disrupted about 140 flights over the North Sea, the Netherlands and eastern England; a Russian warship escorting a sanctioned cargo convoy is the prime suspect

2026-11-15

On the evening of 9 November an estimated 140 aircraft lost accurate GPS positioning over the southern North Sea, the Netherlands, eastern England and north-west Germany, according to an analysis of ADS-B data by the aviation-data group Hexbird reported by The i Paper, ADS Advance and Militarnyi. The interference lasted about 19 minutes (17:08–17:27 GMT) and hit up to 92 aircraft at once, some 39 of them in or near UK-controlled airspace; affected flights included a Ryanair Edinburgh–Rome, an easyJet Manchester–Hurghada and a British Airways Heathrow–Stockholm service. Position-accuracy indicators dropped within six seconds of each other on aircraft spread over roughly 510 km, mainly at cruising altitude, a pattern Hexbird judges consistent with jamming (not spoofing) by a single transmitter near the surface. The Dutch infrastructure ministry told NOS that at least seven aircraft had problems and all landed safely; air navigation provider LVNL said seven such flights were guided into Schiphol. There was no operational impact, and the Dutch Digital Infrastructure Inspectorate (RDI) is investigating. Hexbird's model placed the likely source north of Texel, with low confidence. At that moment the sanctioned Russian cargo ships Lady R, Lady D and Maia-1 were sailing south towards the English Channel under Russian naval escort, shadowed by French and Dutch naval vessels; Lady R and Maia-1 were previously suspected of carrying North Korean weapons for Russia, and Lady D was linked to military cargo loaded at Baltiysk in October. Former naval intelligence officer James Droxford told The i Paper the escorting warship was 'almost certainly' the origin and suggested the jamming was meant to deter Western surveillance of the convoy; Ramsey Faragher of the Royal Institute of Navigation said the size of the affected area pointed to military-grade equipment. Earlier in November Militarnyi reported the Baltic Fleet corvette Stoykiy (Project 20380) escorting Russian cargo ships through the Danish straits, but which ship escorted the convoy on 9 November is not confirmed. Attribution remains open: Hexbird says its analysis 'neither identifies the convoy as the source nor rules it out', the convoy's position did not coincide with its most likely source area, and UK authorities have not attributed the incident. If Russian, it would be the westernmost known Russian GNSS jamming affecting civil aviation, extending a pattern so far concentrated on the Baltic and NATO's eastern flank.

Ukraine's Pluton detection system upgraded to detect the mesh-network modems of Russian Geran, Gerbera and Molniya drones

2026-11-10

The Ukrainian company INFOZAHYST told Militarnyi that its Pluton drone-detection ESM system has been upgraded to detect the mesh modems used in Russian Geran-, Gerbera- and Molniya-type drones. Monitoring covers the main known frequencies of 1.5 GHz and 3.4 GHz, and the developer says the upgrade allows timely detection of threats at distances of up to 70 km. A new software version also allows remote monitoring and partial control of any number of Pluton systems over a dedicated VPN. Mesh modems are one of the most notable recent upgrades to Russian drones: each drone acts as both receiver and relay, which extends control range, lets operators correct a drone's flight path almost until impact, and makes jamming much harder because the network reroutes when a node drops out. Euromaidan Press notes that a jam-resistant link still emits and can therefore be detected. It also recalls earlier reporting that the mesh modem found in downed Russian drones is an openly sold Chinese part (Frontelligence Insight, August 2026), and that Ukraine's HUR in December 2025 described a Geran-2 variant relaying video and commands via a Chinese Xingkay Tech XK-F358 modem. Pluton scans 500 to 6,000 MHz (extendable to 24 GHz according to Militarnyi), already detects FPV, Supercam, Zala, Lancet and Orlan drones, estimates distance and time of arrival at a protected site, and can work with the Plastun-RP-3000M direction finder. Digital FPV video detection and control of counter-UAS assets are planned for the next update. All performance figures are developer claims; it is not stated whether the 70 km range applies specifically to mesh modems, and there is no independent test data or information on deployment numbers.

Ukrainian expert says Iskander-M seekers let Russia hit Kyiv's Dnipro bridges despite GNSS jamming

2026-11-09

Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov, a Ukrainian radio-technology expert and presidential adviser, wrote on Telegram on 9 November that Russian 9M723 Iskander-M ballistic missiles keep hitting bridges very accurately even though satellite navigation is suppressed across the whole of Ukraine during air-raid alerts. Under such jamming, he argued, an Iskander would struggle to achieve metre-level accuracy, and its inertial system alone cannot deliver that precision. According to Beskrestnov, the missiles therefore carry optical homing heads or more expensive radar-guided ones: as the warhead descends, a radar scans the terrain for a few seconds and a correction is fed into the missile's guidance system. Beskrestnov pointed to War&Sanctions, the database of Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR/DIU), which lists both seekers for the 9M723 Iskander-M: the 9E436 optical correlation homing head, made by Russia's TsNIIAG with the NII Televideniya institute involved, and the 9B918 active radar homing head, made by NPP Radar MMS. Euromaidan Press notes that the database confirms these components exist but does not say how they perform under jamming. This is the technical explanation of one expert; there is no official Ukrainian or Russian confirmation of which seeker was used in the bridge strikes.
