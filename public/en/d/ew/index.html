Russia is fitting Geran drones with a US-made magnetometer compass that holds course under GPS jamming, but Western electronics remain at their core, Ukrainian officials say

2026-10-28

Russia has begun installing magnetometers in the tails or tail fins of its Geran attack drones, Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's presidential commissioner for sanctions policy, said in a Facebook post on 28 October 2026. A magnetometer acts as an electronic compass: it lets the drone determine its heading without satellite signals, which makes Ukrainian GPS jamming less effective. Vlasiuk did not say which Geran models carry the sensor. He posted photos from a site where Ukraine examines components recovered from Russian weapons, and said that "Western electronics are at the core everywhere". He also said Ukraine is studying a new Russian jamming device, without saying where it was found or what it targets. Vlasiuk's statement matches an earlier finding by Ukrainian Defence Intelligence (HUR). On 5 October HUR reported that the upgraded Geran-4 carries a new navigation unit called "MOKh". It is built around a PNI5GA magnetometer from the US firm PNI Sensor and works with the SADRA inertial navigation module to reduce gyroscope drift and make autonomous navigation more accurate. According to HUR, the upgraded Geran-4 also carries the Susanin optical-correlation system, which compares camera images with a stored reference image to keep the drone on course. HUR says the new K5 version of the Geran-5 uses a similar system. The magnetometer adds a further layer to Russia's efforts to make the Geran family resistant to jamming. Earlier measures included adaptive multi-element (CRPA) antennas and camera-based terrain navigation. Presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov warned on 28 October that if Susanin is used widely at night, Ukraine may have to resume switching off lights, especially on roads. Apart from the PNI5GA, the article does not say where the other components come from. These are claims by Ukrainian officials and HUR that have not been independently verified.

On 4 October 2026 Ukrainian forces evacuated a wounded infantryman from the grey zone on the Kupiansk front by heavy drone. The soldier is Kostiantyn "Magura" Vlasenko of the 2nd Mechanised Battalion, 30th Separate Mechanised Brigade. He had held a position on the left bank of the Oskil River since 2 June 2025. On 14 February 2026 a Russian drone-dropped munition wounded him in both legs. He lost one foot and amputated the other himself. For months he could not be reached: the area was mined, Russian infantry had infiltrated around his position, and Russian FPV and reconnaissance drones attacked anything that moved. A ground robot sent to fetch him was destroyed on the way (Reuters says two), and infantry groups also failed. Drones resupplied him every four to five days. The aircraft was the Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 from Gurzuf Defence, a modular heavy-lift logistics drone with a cargo winch. Ukrainska Pravda gives its payload as up to 130 kg. Reuters reports roughly 90 kg over up to 25 km, so the sources disagree. The drone was adapted over about six months with help from the 92nd Separate Assault Brigade's unmanned ground systems company. The day before the flight it delivered a mountaineering harness (a Petzl Pitagor evacuation triangle) and a phone with instruction videos, so the soldier could clip himself to the winch cable. Reuters puts the cable height at about 70 m. The drone flew at first light without fog, at a time when Russian drone activity had been assessed as lowest. It used Starlink for control, and Ukrainska Pravda says no jamming was detected. Several measures protected it: Vampire heavy bomber drones flew decoy sorties in neighbouring sectors to draw Russian anti-aircraft FPV operators (one Vampire was shot down), an Autel quadcopter watched overhead, a Lucky Strike interceptor drone stood ready to ram Russian UAVs, and 10th Army Corps artillery fired on identified Russian drone-launch points. The soldier was then handed to a Peacemaker unmanned ground vehicle, which drove him another 8 km to a vehicle and a stabilisation point. Reuters gives different figures: a 15 km distance and a 15-minute flight. Ukrainska Pravda says the drone was airborne for under 1.5 hours in total. Vlasenko is now in rehabilitation in Kharkiv. The operation matters for tactics because ground casualty evacuation in the drone-saturated 'kill zone' has become nearly impossible, and the 'golden hour' is now rarely achievable. The developer itself warns against treating this as a new routine. Gurzuf co-founder Pavlo Feldblium calls aerial evacuation a last resort that needs unique, multi-unit planning (about 15 personnel, two months of planning, seven days of pre-flight preparation). Without decoys, escorts and counter-battery fire, he says, the slow drone with its underslung load would have been shot down by the first Russian interceptor. Gurzuf will nevertheless fit its mass-produced Ayudag-6 drones with evacuation mounting kits and is developing variants with higher payloads. The details come from Ukrainian military participants and the developer, and from Vlasenko's own account. They have not been independently verified.

War on the Rocks: deception against automated sensing and decision chains, drawing on Russo-Ukrainian combat lessons

2026-10-23

In an analysis for War on the Rocks, Édouard R. Maurin (call sign FIDES) argues that concealment alone no longer protects manoeuvre on a battlefield made transparent by sensors and drones. Maurin is a French veteran who served in 2024–2025 with an international volunteer section attached to a Ukrainian special-purpose battalion near Pokrovsk. He also runs Fog Defence, a company that develops decoys, which is a commercial interest in the subject. His argument is that deception should target the whole automated chain of sensing, sensor fusion, classification and decision, not only the human commander. He wants it judged on profitability: does the adversary spend more engaging a decoy than the decoy costs? He also wants it nested from section to corps level under one signature-management system. He adds that US doctrine (JP 3-13.4, FM 3-13.4) still assumes a human decision-maker. Most of the evidence comes from Russia and Ukraine. Maurin describes a fake radio call at Kotlyne on 5 January 2025, transmitted while Russian forces were listening, after which two soldiers left a bunker about two minutes later; he watched this himself through a drone's thermal camera. He also mentions feigned holding of terrain at Pokrovsk while survivors were evacuated. On the Russian side he cites Gerbera foam and wooden Parodiya decoy drones fitted with Luneburg lenses to mimic the radar signature of a Shahed/Geran. According to media reports, these decoys make up about half of the swarms, and Alabuga reportedly keeps their cost below $10,000. He contrasts Western interceptors costing hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars with Ukraine's Sting interceptor (about $2,500). He also quotes Unmanned Systems Forces commander Robert 'Magyar' Brovdi, who puts a mass-produced kamikaze drone at about $400. The article also describes Operation Vivaldi (September 2026). Ukraine's CODE380 develops portable D·Vision SDR analyzer that detects drone signals and displays analog drone video

2026-10-19

Ukrainian company CODE380 has developed the D·Vision SDR, a portable radio-frequency analyzer that scans the airwaves for drone signals and lets the operator view video transmitted over analog channels, Militarnyi reports, citing the Ukrainian tech outlet DOU and the manufacturer. Unlike conventional analog video receivers, the device uses software-defined radio to search for and identify characteristic drone signals across a wide frequency range. It detects PAL and NTSC analog video as well as compatible digital signals from DJI Mavic drones, but it can only play back analog video: DJI digital video is detected, not decoded. According to the manufacturer, analog video can be received and viewed at up to 10 km at 540–6,000 MHz and at 3.5–5 km above 6,000 MHz; DJI Mavic digital signals are detected at up to 2.5 km. The unit has two SMA antenna inputs, a built-in screen, microSD recording, a waterfall display of spectrum activity, an EW-system testing function and USB remote control. Battery life is up to three or six hours depending on the version; it weighs about 290 g without antennas and measures 130 × 80 × 22 mm. Development took about a year and a half in total, and the developers say it was field-tested with Ukrainian service members, including on combat missions. These performance figures are manufacturer claims and have not been independently verified. Militarnyi places the device in a wider Ukrainian trend of portable electronic-reconnaissance detectors for early warning of enemy drones, such as BABAI's Vydun 7.5 (analog video up to 7.5 GHz), the SENSE-4 (up to 8.2 GHz) and the Chuyka 3.0 extended to the 6–8.8 GHz band.
