Analyst Clément Molin counts 770 confirmed Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian supply trucks in September and warns that winter will make supplying Crimea even harder

2026-10-09

French open-source mapper Clément Molin, who follows Ukraine's counter-logistics campaign closely, counted 770 confirmed Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian trucks in September in the logistics zone behind the front line. His count includes only hits confirmed by video or photographs, so the real number is likely higher. His monthly figures are 214 in May, 586 in June, 656 in July, 630 in August and 770 in September, compared with a few dozen a month before that. In an X thread on 7 October he wrote that more than 3,000 trucks and vehicles have been hit on key roads since May 2026. The figures come from Molin's own open-source tally as reported by Euromaidan Press and have not been independently verified. The strikes target the main supply routes through occupied southern Ukraine to Crimea, especially the R-280 and the Mariupol-Melitopol road, the main artery of Russia's land corridor. Strikes on Black Sea cargo ships make it harder for Russia to move this traffic to sea. The drones named are the Hornet, B-2, RAM 2X, Chaklun and various long-endurance FPV quadcopters, many with onboard AI that detects a truck and homes in on it in the final seconds. Control runs through overlapping links, including Starlink terminals (some mounted on balloons) and mesh radio networks. In spring Russia began moving trucks in convoys with mobile fire-support groups. Molin says a typical convoy has three to five supply trucks and two to three air-defence vehicles, increasingly with jamming equipment. Ukrainian drones reportedly hit the gun trucks first, leaving the supply trucks exposed to follow-on strikes. Molin expects winter to make things worse for Russia, because bare trees give less cover from surveillance and poor off-road conditions push traffic onto the paved roads where most strikes happen. The article also cites a May assessment by Tochnyi that this pressure could weaken front-line positions enough to allow breakthrough operations in the south. It notes, however, that it is unclear whether Ukraine has enough reserves to exploit that.

Sources: Euromaidan Press

Ukrainian firm Phantom Defense says its counter-drone EW systems were tested at Fort Carson in the Pentagon's Drone Dominance programme

2026-10-08

Ukrainian defence tech company Phantom Defense says its electronic warfare (EW) systems were tested in the United States during the second stage ("Gauntlet II") of the Pentagon's Drone Dominance programme, at Fort Carson in Colorado. According to the company's press service, speaking to Ukrainska Pravda, Phantom Defense was one of three manufacturers engaged by the Pentagon to build a counter-UAV environment against which the competing drones were tested. The company deployed its Specter PRO dome and directional EW system, intended to protect strongpoints, brigade headquarters and critical infrastructure, plus its compact Specter L, X and XL systems. It also demonstrated Streamhunter, a tool that intercepts drone video feeds. All elements were tied into a C2 system for detection, classification, engagement and confirmation of suppression. Phantom Defense says its systems suppressed the tested drones, worked in various weather conditions and recreated the EW environment drone operators face on the front line; it says it has been invited to further Drone Dominance events. A follow-up piece by Defender Media, carried by Euromaidan Press as labelled advertorial content, names the C2 layer as Kupol, which shows separate tools on one shared map, allows remote EW operation, links sensors, interceptor drones and turrets, and suggests defeat options from terrain and a drone database; the company also claims its system neutralised more than 7,000 drones over more than 100 km² in one Ukrainian city within a year. These claims come from the company itself. According to the report, the Drone Dominance programme has a budget of US$1.1 billion and tested solutions from 25 manufacturers in its initial phase; after the first phase around 30,000 drones are to be bought from 11 companies for US$150 million. The US military earlier placed an additional order for 1,320 F10 FPV drones from Ukrainian firm F-Drones under the same programme. The item is relevant mainly as an indicator that Ukrainian counter-drone EW, developed against Russian drones, is being used to reproduce front-line conditions in US testing.

«Ястреб-С» против спутниковой связи (Topwar: "Yastreb-S" against satellite communications)

2026-10-08SINGLE SOURCE

Topwar (author Kirill Ryabov) describes the Yastreb ("Hawk") family of electronic warfare systems, developed by JSC NPP with backing from Russia's Foundation for Advanced Studies (FPI). The base Yastreb 1.0 was announced in late January 2026; according to TASS (26 September) it is in its final test phase and is to be handed to the army in November for troop trials, possibly in the war zone against real drone signals. Yastreb-S, reported by Izvestia on 7 October citing the Center for Unmanned Systems and Technologies, is a more advanced variant: the 1.0 complex plus a frequency-shifting module. Only 1.0 is currently being tested; no test data on Yastreb-S has been published. According to the developer, Yastreb 1.0 automatically scans 100 MHz to 6 GHz, while Yastreb-S reaches up to 15 GHz. That covers drone control and video bands (433 MHz to 5.8 GHz) and GPS/GLONASS. The system is said to detect links and their parameters, counter frequency hopping and jam drone links, and in reconnaissance mode locate emitters and sometimes intercept drone video. Instead of a battery of band-specific jammers and antennas, it reportedly uses a single compact emitter module that can be mounted on fixed sites or vehicles. The headline claim is the ability to disrupt satellite links such as Starlink, which Ukrainian units use for drone control. The editorial staff is notably sceptical. A 15 GHz ceiling covers Starlink's Ku-band user links but not the Ka-band. Fully blocking Starlink from the ground is called unlikely; temporarily disrupting a single link during a drone's approach is more realistic. The November date means troop trials, not combat readiness, and the vehicle is vulnerable to counter-EW and small drones. The author also notes that Russian industry has announced several anti-satellite-link systems whose serial production remains unclear. Single source: this is a report on developer claims by Topwar, a Russian military news site (not a state outlet), that has not been independently verified.

Ukraine lists the SHOT-L1, a roughly $9,300 fixed-wing drone that homes on Russian jammers such as the Pole-21 and Zhitel, on Brave1 Market

2026-10-07

A Ukrainian fixed-wing strike drone built to find Russian electronic warfare systems by their own radio emissions and dive onto them has been listed on Brave1 Market, the government-run online store where Ukrainian units order weapons, according to Ukrainian outlet Defender Media as relayed by Defence Blog. The drone, called SHOT-L1, can strike targets up to 80 km away and is priced at UAH 416,000 (about $9,300) per unit on the platform. The manufacturer has not been named, and there is no information on whether the drone has been codified, the formal approval that admits a product into Ukrainian military service. According to the manufacturer, an operator sends the drone toward the area where a target is believed to be. Once it reaches the assigned zone, an onboard system scans for radio emissions, determines the direction of a detected source and steers the drone onto it automatically. If several emitters are active at once, the drone goes for the strongest one. If no active signal is found within a set time, it switches to a backup mission and attacks a fixed ground target chosen before launch. The SHOT-L1 carries a 4 kg payload, cruises at 100 km/h with up to 60 minutes of flight time, and has a thermal camera, an analog video link and a terminal guidance system for the final approach. The manufacturer lists three Russian jamming systems as typical targets: the R-340RP Pole-21, the R-330Zh Zhitel and the Mgla. Pole-21 is designed to jam satellite navigation signals such as GPS, while the truck-mounted Zhitel is built to jam satellite phones, cellular networks and navigation signals. Ukrainian estimates put the cost of a single Zhitel at around $10 million, which gives the headline cost contrast with the roughly $9,300 drone. Both systems are widely used by Russian forces, and jamming is a constant problem for Ukrainian drone units that rely on radio control links and satellite navigation. Brave1 Market launched in 2025; Ukraine's Defense Ministry said troops ordered more than 590,000 drones through it in the first nine months of 2026, with interceptor drones the most popular category. The performance claims come from the manufacturer's listing; no combat results are reported.
