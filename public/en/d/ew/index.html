Ukraine carries out first known aerial casualty evacuation by heavy-lift drone: Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 extracts wounded soldier after 491 days in the grey zone near Kupiansk

2026-10-23

On 4 October 2026 Ukrainian forces evacuated a wounded infantryman from the grey zone on the Kupiansk front by heavy drone. Ukrainska Pravda and Reuters (republished by Meduza) both call it the first known operation of its kind. The soldier is Kostiantyn "Magura" Vlasenko of the 2nd Mechanised Battalion, 30th Separate Mechanised Brigade. He had held a position on the left bank of the Oskil River since 2 June 2025. On 14 February 2026 a Russian drone-dropped munition wounded him in both legs. He lost one foot and amputated the other himself. For months he could not be reached: the area was mined, Russian infantry had infiltrated around his position, and Russian FPV and reconnaissance drones attacked anything that moved. A ground robot sent to fetch him was destroyed on the way (Reuters says two), and infantry groups also failed. Drones resupplied him every four to five days. The aircraft was the Heavy Shot Ayudag-6 from Gurzuf Defence, a modular heavy-lift logistics drone with a cargo winch. Ukrainska Pravda gives its payload as up to 130 kg. Reuters reports roughly 90 kg over up to 25 km, so the sources disagree. The drone was adapted over about six months with help from the 92nd Separate Assault Brigade's unmanned ground systems company. The day before the flight it delivered a mountaineering harness (a Petzl Pitagor evacuation triangle) and a phone with instruction videos, so the soldier could clip himself to the winch cable. Reuters puts the cable height at about 70 m. The drone flew at first light without fog, at a time when Russian drone activity had been assessed as lowest. It used Starlink for control, and Ukrainska Pravda says no jamming was detected. Several measures protected it: Vampire heavy bomber drones flew decoy sorties in neighbouring sectors to draw Russian anti-aircraft FPV operators (one Vampire was shot down), an Autel quadcopter watched overhead, a Lucky Strike interceptor drone stood ready to ram Russian UAVs, and 10th Army Corps artillery fired on identified Russian drone-launch points. Units from the 14th and 43rd Mechanised Brigades also took part, with EW support from the 3rd Army Corps. The 16 km route needed a battery swap at a pre-positioned ground team. The soldier was then handed to a Peacemaker unmanned ground vehicle, which drove him another 8 km to a vehicle and a stabilisation point. Reuters gives different figures: a 15 km distance and a 15-minute flight. Ukrainska Pravda says the drone was airborne for under 1.5 hours in total. Vlasenko is now in rehabilitation in Kharkiv. The operation matters for tactics because ground casualty evacuation in the drone-saturated 'kill zone' has become nearly impossible, and the 'golden hour' is now rarely achievable. The developer itself warns against treating this as a new routine. Gurzuf co-founder Pavlo Feldblium calls aerial evacuation a last resort that needs unique, multi-unit planning (about 15 personnel, two months of planning, seven days of pre-flight preparation). Without decoys, escorts and counter-battery fire, he says, the slow drone with its underslung load would have been shot down by the first Russian interceptor. Gurzuf will nevertheless fit its mass-produced Ayudag-6 drones with evacuation mounting kits and is developing variants with higher payloads. The details come from Ukrainian military participants and the developer, and from Vlasenko's own account. They have not been independently verified.

War on the Rocks: deception against automated sensing and decision chains, drawing on Russo-Ukrainian combat lessons

2026-10-23

In an analysis for War on the Rocks, Édouard R. Maurin (call sign FIDES) argues that concealment alone no longer protects manoeuvre on a battlefield made transparent by sensors and drones. Maurin is a French veteran who served in 2024–2025 with an international volunteer section attached to a Ukrainian special-purpose battalion near Pokrovsk. He also runs Fog Defence, a company that develops decoys, which is a commercial interest in the subject. His argument is that deception should target the whole automated chain of sensing, sensor fusion, classification and decision, not only the human commander. He wants it judged on profitability: does the adversary spend more engaging a decoy than the decoy costs? He also wants it nested from section to corps level under one signature-management system. He adds that US doctrine (JP 3-13.4, FM 3-13.4) still assumes a human decision-maker. Most of the evidence comes from Russia and Ukraine. Maurin describes a fake radio call at Kotlyne on 5 January 2025, transmitted while Russian forces were listening, after which two soldiers left a bunker about two minutes later; he watched this himself through a drone's thermal camera. He also mentions feigned holding of terrain at Pokrovsk while survivors were evacuated. On the Russian side he cites Gerbera foam and wooden Parodiya decoy drones fitted with Luneburg lenses to mimic the radar signature of a Shahed/Geran. According to media reports, these decoys make up about half of the swarms, and Alabuga reportedly keeps their cost below $10,000. He contrasts Western interceptors costing hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars with Ukraine's Sting interceptor (about $2,500). He also quotes Unmanned Systems Forces commander Robert 'Magyar' Brovdi, who puts a mass-produced kamikaze drone at about $400. The article also describes Operation Vivaldi (September 2026). There, Ukraine's Third Army Corps first degraded Russian EW and drone-pilot teams and then used feints and fake signatures to retake the Nove settlement and its heights. According to deputy commander Maksym Zhorin, an EW 'protective dome' disrupted or suppressed about 20,000 Russian drones. Corps commander Andriy Biletsky called deception a key factor in the success. Maurin recommends company-level signature fleets, battalion-level signature management cells, and procurement of cheap active decoys. He states explicitly that the effect on automated classifiers is his hypothesis and has not been observed. Much of the Russo-Ukrainian evidence rests on media and unit reports and has not been independently verified.

Europe weighs letting utilities and infrastructure operators detect, jam and shoot down drones; France has already legislated, Austria and Poland follow

European governments are considering giving operators of critical infrastructure, including energy companies, powers to counter drones themselves, Reuters reports via Defense News. There is no single EU proposal: countries are moving separately, and the article says the technology is maturing faster than the rules for using it. France has gone furthest legally. In August its parliament passed a law allowing approved critical-infrastructure operators and their contractors to detect, jam and shoot down drones near designated sites, after drone flights over the Île Longue nuclear submarine base near Brest in December 2025. The defence ministry says the law covers only infrastructure under its authority. In Germany, where a drone carrying explosives was found in August near a Ukrainian cargo aircraft at Leipzig/Halle airport (Berlin blamed Russia, which denied it), counter-drone action remains a police task; the interior ministry is examining how operators could legally take part. Austria announced in September plans to give critical-infrastructure operators, including large energy companies, similar powers; Defence Minister Klaudia Tanner said the state cannot protect every site alone. Poland is furthest along in practice: operators have deployed counter-drone systems and trained with military and police units, and drone-restricted zones around critical energy sites let authorised security units destroy, disable or take control of drones. Finland's Fingrid and Fortum, by contrast, want responsibility to remain with the authorities and the armed forces; Fingrid's CEO called expanded powers "a very, very big change". Open questions are who may order an engagement and who is liable if a downed drone causes damage. Rheinmetall says the main obstacle is the delay between detection and the decision to act, and a French official expects operators eventually to respond "at the press of a button".

Ukrainian 'Nemesis' drone detachment caused about 70% of a US armored brigade's early casualties at Hohenfels, brigade commander writes

2026-10-20

Ukrainian drone operators playing the enemy in a US Army exercise caused about 70% of an American armored brigade's casualties in the first three days, according to an article in Military Review, the US Army's professional journal, reported by Defence Blog. The authors are Col. Michael Ziegelhofer, commander of the 3rd Armored Brigade Combat Team, 1st Cavalry Division ("Greywolf"), and six of his officers. They describe Combined Resolve 26-07, held in spring 2026 at the Hohenfels Training Area in Bavaria, where the opposing force was reinforced by a detachment of the 412th Regiment "Nemesis" of Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces. Over the roughly 10-day rotation the opposing force initiated contact with drones more than 750 times, either striking troops directly or spotting for artillery. By the end of day three the share of drone-caused casualties had fallen below 30%, without the enemy easing off: "Greywolf changed how it fought". According to the authors, roughly 60% of the brigade-level counter-drone fight is fieldcraft and drills. The brigade's counter-drone/EW systems jammed control links in protective bubbles up to 2 km wide, but had to be set up at fixed sites and moved forward in bounds, leaving units exposed on the move, and were less effective against the smaller number of unjammable fiber-optic drones. More than half the formation relied on cover, concealment, dispersion, smaller routes and fast battle drills. Survival had a price: crews switched off Blue Force Tracker, cut radio use and even dropped Starshield satellite links to reduce their electronic signature, and resupply took twice as long as planned because the enemy hunted supply lines. On the offensive side, the brigade brought in the 3rd Battalion, 504th Parachute Infantry Regiment (then an experimental drone battalion) and used more than 700 drones from platoon to brigade level; with relay drones, nearly all could fly 25-40 km, covering the whole ~14 x 27 km training area. That abundance initially overwhelmed the headquarters: collection-planning tools, the operations floor and per-drone airspace management could not keep up, and the staff never had a full picture of how many drones were airborne. The brigade adapted by giving drone units priorities and letting them hunt within the commander's intent, moving the coordinated fire line forward, making enemy drone ground control stations a top target and holding drone munitions in reserve; it ended the rotation with two simultaneous breaches about 6 km apart that the opposing force failed to stop. The officers recommend multifunctional drone strike troops in armored brigades (long-range surveillance to at least 40 km, medium/heavy bomber drones, strike drones with relays), one common operational picture for all drones modelled on Ukraine's Delta system, and counter-drone defences that work on the move, from hard-kill protection on every vehicle to brigade-level radar and defeat systems. This is a different, earlier exercise than the Stork and MV7 trials covered previously; it is one of the clearest first-hand accounts of how a NATO heavy brigade fares against Ukrainian-style drone TTPs, which mirror what Russian forces face and increasingly employ themselves.
