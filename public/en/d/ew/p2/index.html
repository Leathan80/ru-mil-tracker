Ukrainian EW monitor 'Flash' warns Russia is moving Molniya strike drones to digital video, blinding analog detectors

2026-09-09

According to Ukrainian EW monitor Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov (President Zelenskyy's defense-technology adviser), Russia is increasingly fitting its cheap Molniya strike drones with digital video transmission in place of analog. Once that transition is complete, the portable detectors Ukraine currently relies on for early warning will stop working, because those devices key on analog video signals. Beskrestnov urges Ukraine to field countermeasures before the switch completes rather than after. His proposed fix is to build digital-video detection into interception platforms now. Such a system would work differently from current gear: it would not see the camera image, but would detect the presence of the transmission and determine its bearing (direction). The Molniya costs roughly $300, is built from plywood, foam and off-the-shelf Chinese electronics, and is launched up to ten times a day in some frontline sectors. Earlier upgrades already moved its video to the 4.1-4.5 GHz band, and an autonomous, AI-guided variant with no radio link exists. Ukraine only fielded its first mass-produced portable drone detector in July 2026. These claims originate with Beskrestnov and are not independently confirmed.

Finland closes investigation into four armed Ukrainian strike drones that strayed into its territory in spring 2026

2026-09-09

Finland's National Bureau of Investigation and the Finnish Border Guard have completed their inquiry into four incidents in spring 2026 in which Ukrainian military drones crossed into Finnish territory. According to the Finnish authorities, all four drones originated in Ukraine, were similar models manufactured for military use, and each carried an explosive warhead. They came down in March at Kouvola (28 March), Luumäki and near Parikkala, and in April near Iitti. One drone detonated in mid-air; Finnish authorities destroyed the other three in controlled explosions. There were no injuries and no property damage. Finnish investigators concluded that Finland was not the target in any of the incidents. The drones had been flying toward Russian territory — described in Finnish and Ukrainian accounts as strikes aimed at Russian Baltic Sea ports — but, per the Finnish authorities, were knocked off course by GPS jamming/spoofing emanating from Russia's Kaliningrad exclave and diverted westward toward Finland. The wind at the time blew almost directly from the south, which may have further affected the drones' tracks. Officials characterised the drones as having been effectively "hijacked" in flight. The criminal investigation has been closed: "the preliminary investigations have not revealed any evidence that would have justified continuing the pursuit of criminal liability," the institutions stated. On the Ukrainian side, President Zelensky had earlier apologised, stressing that "Ukraine was not targeting Finland under any circumstances," and in May 2026 proposed a bilateral drone agreement to Finnish Prime Minister Orpo. The core findings — Ukrainian origin, explosive payloads, and Kaliningrad-based GNSS spoofing as the cause — are confirmed by the Finnish authorities; the relative weight of wind versus jamming, and the precise intended targets inside Russia, remain partly reconstruction. This is separate from e-20260905-10 (a Russian Geoscan 701 survey drone near Porvoo).

Ukraine unveils indigenous Shershen SAM with R-120K missile and Ai-Petri anti-glide-bomb EW system at MSPO 2026

2026-09-09

At the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Kielce, Poland, Ukrainian company Radionix presented its modular "Shershen" (Hornet) air-defence system, built around the Perun command-and-control vehicle, the Athena surveillance/tracking radar (detection range roughly 85 km) and four Borei launchers per battery. A more detailed report from The War Zone (TWZ, 10 September 2026) confirms and sharpens the picture: the unit on display was the second operational prototype and is described as "close to its production configuration." The design's core feature is a deliberately open architecture — the manufacturer states the system is "deliberately open... to use different missiles from different manufacturers" — letting the launcher fire both Soviet air-to-air missiles (R-27/AA-10, R-73/AA-11, R-60) and Western types (AIM-9 Sidewinder, AIM-132 ASRAAM), alongside the new, Ukrainian-developed R-120K. Manufacturer-claimed performance: engagement range up to 25 km, altitude up to 10,000 m, and up to six radar-guided missiles engaged simultaneously. TWZ situates Shershen within Ukraine's broader pattern of "FrankenSAM" improvisation — earlier examples include Buk launchers paired with Sea Sparrow missiles, Osa systems adapted to fire R-73s, and the UK-Danish containerized Gravehawk system — but argues Shershen is the first to systematize this approach, building calibre-agnostic launching in as a core design principle rather than an emergency workaround. TWZ is explicit about the caveats, however: advertised performance figures "have not been independently verified," the R-120K's motor is still under development so its "eventual performance and production status remain uncertain," and actual performance "will vary widely depending on the type of interceptor" fired. Separately, at the same exhibition, company Storkway showed the "Ai-Petri" electronic-warfare system (named after Crimea's highest peak), which jams both drone control links and the guidance of Russian glide bombs (KAB/UMPK). It operates across 300 MHz-6 GHz, scans a full 360 degrees, has a terrain-dependent range of 20-30 km, and draws 250 W in detection mode rising to about 2,000 W while jamming; the mobile pickup-mounted variant can be set up by a two-person crew in roughly 10 minutes. Unlike Shershen, Ai-Petri is not an exhibition concept: development began in 2014, tactical versions have been fielded since 2024, and the manufacturer claims around 400 systems delivered. As with Shershen, its claimed effectiveness against glide bombs remains independently unverified.

Ukraine's National Guard says fiber-optic drones rose from 20% to 70% of its drone war

2026-09-09

Ukraine's National Guard has rapidly rebuilt its FPV drone force around fiber-optic control. According to National Guard commander Oleksandr Pivnenko, speaking in a YouTube interview on the LB Live channel on 9 September 2026, the share of fiber-optic ("wired") drones in the Guard's drone employment has risen from roughly 20% to roughly 70%. These figures are National Guard statements and have not been independently verified. Pivnenko also said the Guard doubled its number of interceptor-drone crews over the past six months, and that National Guard drone crews have struck more than 243,000 enemy targets since February 2022. The Guard attributes the shift primarily to Russian electronic warfare. On a front where both sides jam radio signals around the clock, a fiber cable removes the radio link entirely, making the drone immune to jamming while preserving an interference-free, sharp video feed. Broader open-source reporting provides context: hit rates for radio-controlled FPV drones, estimated at 40-60% in 2023, reportedly fell to 20-30% or lower in heavily jammed sectors by mid-2024, pushing both sides toward tethered systems. Ukraine had approved more than 80 domestically designed fiber-optic systems by February 2026, and Ukraine's Defense Procurement Agency has opened competitive tenders for fiber-optic and deep-strike drones built to Defense Ministry specifications. Pivnenko acknowledged that adoption was initially difficult — problems with cable quality and how the aircraft handled — before crews mastered both reconnaissance and strike missions with the type. The technology's widely reported downsides, not itemized in this particular source but relevant to assessment: the weight and drag of the fiber spool cap effective range (roughly 10-20 km depending on how much fiber is carried), the cable can snag on vegetation, trees, structures and power lines, spent cables litter the terrain, and the drone loses the freedom of maneuver a radio link allows. For the close-range FPV attacks that dominate the front, Ukrainian operators judge these limitations acceptable against the gain in jamming immunity.

Ukraine's Defence Intelligence (HUR) has published data on 155 enterprises that are part of, or managed by, the Ruselectronics (Roselektronika) holding, a subsidiary of Russia's state corporation Rostec. The information was added to the "aggressor's military-industrial complex" section of HUR's War&Sanctions portal. The disclosure continues HUR's running effort, via the War&Sanctions portal, to map Russia's defence-industrial base and the foreign and domestic supply chains feeding it — earlier tranches covered foreign components found in Russian systems, the Pantsir-S1 supplier network, Su-35/Su-30 production, and named Russian scientists in military research. HUR calls for tighter supply-chain control to prevent sanctions circumvention, positioning the database as a targeting aid for Western sanctions authorities and OSINT researchers.

Почему танки перестали доминировать на поле боя? (Rybar video essay)

2026-09-05SINGLE SOURCEMILBLOGGER

Rybar published a short video essay (2:21) from its #ИсторияПодвига series, forwarded from the affiliated channel Тактикарь, asking why the tank no longer dominates the battlefield. Rybar first sketches the classic role: since the early twentieth century the tank was the "steel fist" of armies — it broke through defensive lines, supported infantry and served as an assault gun. Tanks entered the "SVO" in that role, with examples such as a T-80BV crew destroying Ukrainian armour and the "carousel" tactic used against Ukrainian infantry. Then, Rybar argues, everything changed because of "small, cheap products made from commercial components" — FPV drones — which he calls the most serious challenge to the tank since it first appeared on the battlefield. The factors cited are: mass-producible, very cheap FPV drones and anti-tank guided missiles that make any visible vehicle vulnerable; the loss of combined-arms cover (infantry, air defence, electronic warfare) so that tanks can no longer advance under protection; and a cost asymmetry in which a drone worth a few hundred dollars disables a vehicle worth millions. The consequence: both armies no longer use the tank for the breakthrough but as mobile artillery and standoff fire support — firing from cover, then withdrawing quickly. As countermeasures Rybar points to physical protection (cope cages, rubber and chain skirts, and more recently the heavier "hedgehog" cage armour), vehicle-mounted jammers against FPV control links, active protection systems, and above all adapted tactics that minimise exposure; he implicitly concedes that no single measure removes the drone threat. Caveat: this is a single Russian milblogger source (Rybar), a personal/editorial voice and not an MoD position; milbloggers contradict each other and the MoD. The item connects thematically to entry e-20260905-02 (hedgehog cage armour and Ukraine's decision to park its Abrams tanks).

Russian artillery brigades stand up organic counter-battery UAV subunits, Izvestia reports

2026-09-04SINGLE SOURCE

The pro-military Russian outlet Topwar (author Kirill Ryabov), on 7 September 2026, relays a 4 September 2026 Izvestia report citing unnamed sources that artillery brigades of the Russian Ground Forces have stood up their own organic UAV subunits dedicated to counter-battery warfare (RU: “подразделения БПЛА контрбатарейной борьбы”). The subunits are said to be already operational at the front. Each fields two drone types — a long-endurance reconnaissance/loitering type for surveillance and target detection, and a strike type (loitering munition) able to engage located targets itself — supplemented by ground-based radio-technical reconnaissance (RTR) and electronic-warfare systems to locate and jam enemy command and communication links. Primary targets are enemy tube and rocket artillery, counter-battery radars and EW complexes; a secondary task is detecting and suppressing enemy UAVs, especially FPV and artillery-spotting drones. The subunit passes target designation directly to the brigade's own artillery. The stated purpose is to compress the counter-battery cycle by removing the delays that arise when drone support has to be requested from outside units — decisive against “roving gun” tactics where a piece fires for seconds to a few minutes and then displaces. The article frames this as the latest step in a long arc: artillery–UAV cooperation became widespread after February 2022, with the Unmanned Systems Troops initiated by Defence Minister Belousov in December 2024 and declared structurally complete on 12 November 2025. Izvestia names no specific drone models, no number of subunits formed, and does not say which brigades hold them, and there is no open information on any force-wide rollout. Single-source caveat: this rests on a single Russian source chain — Izvestia's anonymous sources, relayed by the pro-military outlet Topwar — and has not been independently confirmed by Western or Ukrainian reporting.
