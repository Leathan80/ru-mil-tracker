Russia adapts to Ukraine's middle-strike drone campaign with mobile fire groups, Rubicon interceptors and Starlink jammers

2026-09-23

According to a Kyiv Independent field report from the southern front, Russian forces have adapted to Ukraine's expanded "middle strike" campaign. That campaign uses drones against targets from roughly 35 km (the end of FPV range) out to about 300 km behind the line. Within weeks of the campaign being scaled up in the south, Russia changed logistics routes and fielded new countermeasures. Columns carrying high-value cargo are now often escorted by mobile fire groups, some riding on the vehicles and some posted at intervals along key routes such as the E58 highway to Crimea. Russia's elite Rubicon drone unit has also published dozens of videos of its interceptor teams downing Starlink-equipped Hornet drones and other middle-strike drones. The commander of Ukraine's 422nd Unmanned Systems Regiment, Mykola "Tyson" Kolesnyk, said these measures now bring down "a certain percentage" of Ukrainian drones but have not critically changed his unit's work. He also said that moving Rubicon's interceptor teams to the rear to guard the highways has let more Ukrainian fixed-wing drones cross the front uncontested. The report also describes Russian efforts to take away Ukraine's Starlink advantage. Starlink was cut off for Russian forces in early February 2026 after mandatory terminal registration through Ukraine's Diia app was introduced. Since then, Russian fixed-wing drones, from the tactical Molniya to the long-range Geran class, have increasingly been fitted with decentralized mesh radio modems to keep their links and video feeds under Ukrainian EW. Russia has announced its own satellite internet system, Rassvet, but its rollout has suffered setbacks, including a satellite that reportedly fell from the sky and others that did not reach their intended orbits. Russia is also testing several Starlink jammers. The most prominent is the Peresvet-M, which according to Ukrainian presidential adviser Serhii "Flash" Beskretnov floods the terminal's uplink channel. Beskretnov said it can deny Starlink over about 20 km² and has been deployed to protect key targets in Crimea. Kolesnyk said his regiment, working with the SBU's Alpha centre, destroyed a first Russian Starlink jammer on 14 June 2026 and has since helped destroy at least five such systems. He described them as bulky and vulnerable because of their large power needs. About two weeks before the interview, according to Kolesnyk, Russia began running the jammers from the electricity grid instead of generators. He said the unit felt their effect only once and that it is still unclear whether Russia will scale the jammers up or whether they are mainly an information operation. The regiment says it struck a substation in occupied Zaporizhzhia Oblast that powered radars, EW equipment and Starlink jammers, and that Russian air defence downed two of the four Zozulia drones sent. All these claims come from a Ukrainian unit that is a party to the conflict and have not been independently confirmed.

Ukrainian radio relay survives 10 Russian strikes, keeps transmitting after building collapse

2026-09-16

HIMERA B1 is a Ukrainian-made autonomous radio repeater that extends the range of battlefield mesh radio networks. It uses AES-256 encryption and pseudo-random frequency hopping to resist interception, direction-finding and jamming. The manufacturer, Himera, was founded by a volunteer from Kyiv Oblast's territorial defense who identified critical battlefield communication gaps early in the full-scale invasion. A unit of this type was operated by Ukraine's 1st Border Guard Detachment from a telecom tower in Kharkiv Oblast, working alongside a sister relay 10.5 kilometers away, each covering roughly 5 kilometers of communications range. Russian forces struck this specific position ten times using FPV drones and guided (glide) bombs — a pattern suggesting deliberate targeting of Ukrainian communication nodes rather than incidental damage. In the final strike, the building partially collapsed and the tower came down, burying the repeater under rubble and destroying its power system. Despite the damage, the device reportedly kept transmitting and stayed connected even while buried, which is why border guards were able to recover it afterward. The episode highlights two things at once: Russia's sustained effort to disrupt Ukrainian battlefield communications through repeated precision strikes on relay infrastructure, and the emphasis Ukrainian manufacturers place on ruggedness and redundancy in this class of equipment as a countermeasure to that campaign.

Ukraine combat experience shapes Redwire's new Penguin Mk3 drone

2026-09-12

US defense company Redwire has delivered more than 250 of its Penguin reconnaissance drones — a Group 3 fixed-wing UAS with roughly 14 hours of endurance and EO/IR sensors for target recognition — to Ukraine since 2022, largely through US aid packages. That deployment generated a large body of operational data, now feeding into a next-generation variant: the Penguin Mk3. The central lesson is that raw specifications — range, endurance, payload — do not determine whether a tactical drone stays useful. Having the right aircraft, in the right configuration, available at the right moment, along with maintenance speed, training quality and operator workload, proved just as decisive. Russia's heavily contested electromagnetic environment also forced Redwire to rethink navigation and communications assumptions: rather than building in one permanent EW-hardening fix, the company adopted a modular architecture that allows rapid adaptation as adversaries roll out new jamming or spoofing techniques. As a result, the Mk3 adds fully automated vertical takeoff and landing, multiple larger payload bays, winter-operating provisions, and deployability by a two-person crew. An undisclosed NATO country has since signed a multi-year contract for the Mk3, valued in the high eight figures and supported through Redwire's European maintenance and engineering operation. The development reflects a broader shift among Western manufacturers away from treating drones as finished products at delivery and toward continuous iteration — a direct response to the Ukrainian reality that drone tactics and countermeasures can become obsolete within weeks or even days.

At the MSPO 2026 defence exhibition in Kielce, Poland, Ukrainian company Radionix presented its modular "Shershen" (Hornet) air-defence system, built around the Perun command-and-control vehicle, the Athena surveillance/tracking radar (detection range roughly 85 km) and four Borei launchers per battery. A more detailed report from The War Zone (TWZ, 10 September 2026) confirms and sharpens the picture: the unit on display was the second operational prototype and is described as "close to its production configuration." The design's core feature is a deliberately open architecture — the manufacturer states the system is "deliberately open... to use different missiles from different manufacturers" — letting the launcher fire both Soviet air-to-air missiles (R-27/AA-10, R-73/AA-11, R-60) and Western types (AIM-9 Sidewinder, AIM-132 ASRAAM), alongside the new, Ukrainian-developed R-120K.
